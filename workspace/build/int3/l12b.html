Lesson 12B

Inverse trigonometric functions: integrating

This lesson deals with the integrals associated with the inverse trigonometric functions, and with the standard applications of those integrals. Reversing the derivatives of Lesson 12A gives standard forms for \(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{a^2-x^2}}\) and \(\dfrac{1}{a^2+x^2}\). When \(x^2\) has a coefficient, take it out first. The reverse chain rule extends the forms, and a product-rule derivative gives the primitive of \(\sin^{-1}x\) itself.
{{sec:teal:key:Concept 1:the basic standard forms}}
Reversing the derivatives

Differentiation of the inverse trigonometric functions yields purely algebraic functions,

\[\frac{d}{dx}\sin^{-1}x=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\frac{d}{dx}\tan^{-1}x=\frac{1}{1+x^2}.\]

These are remarkable results, and indicate once again that trigonometric functions are very closely related to algebraic functions associated with squares and square roots. The relationship was already clear when the trigonometric functions were defined using the circle, whose equation \(x^2+y^2=r^2\) is Pythagoras’ theorem, which is purely algebraic.

This lesson concerns integration, and we begin by reversing the three standard forms for differentiation.

The third standard form

Lesson 12A also gave \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\cos^{-1}x=\dfrac{-1}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}\), so \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\big(-\cos^{-1}x\big)=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}\) as well.

4 Standard forms for integration
\[\int\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}\,dx=\sin^{-1}x+C\qquad\text{OR}\qquad\int\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}\,dx=-\cos^{-1}x+C\] \[\int\frac{1}{1+x^2}\,dx=\tan^{-1}x+C\]
Two answers that agree

The two primitives of \(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}\) differ only by a constant. From the identity \(\sin^{-1}x+\cos^{-1}x=\tfrac{\pi}{2}\) (revision page), \(-\cos^{-1}x=\sin^{-1}x-\tfrac{\pi}{2}\). So the constant \(-\tfrac{\pi}{2}\) is absorbed into \(C\), and a definite integral gives the same value with either form (see the example below).

Algebraic integrands

Thus some purely algebraic functions require the inverse trigonometric functions for their integration. We have seen this sort of phenomenon before with the standard form \(\displaystyle\int\frac1x\,dx=\log_e|x|\), where the logarithmic function was required for the integration of another purely algebraic function.

The functions \(y=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}\) and \(y=\dfrac{1}{1+x^2}\)

The primitives of both these functions have now been obtained, and they should therefore be regarded as reasonably standard functions whose graphs should be known. The sketch of each function, and some important definite integrals associated with them, are developed in Questions 15 and 16 of the exercise.

Exact values and calculator values

Enclose the primitive \(\tan^{-1}x\) in square brackets, substitute the upper and lower limits, and subtract. When the limits give special angles, such as \(\tan^{-1}1=\tfrac{\pi}{4}\) and \(\tan^{-1}0=0\), the answer is exact. Otherwise use the calculator, in radians, and round only at the end.

Because \(1+x^2\) is never zero, the function \(\dfrac{1}{1+x^2}\) is continuous for all real \(x\), so any limits may be used.

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 3:standard forms with one constant}}
More general standard forms

When constants are involved, the calculation of the primitive becomes fiddly. These standard integrals are commonly used.

5 Standard forms with one constant
\[\int\frac{1}{\sqrt{a^2-x^2}}\,dx=\sin^{-1}\frac xa+C\qquad\text{OR}\qquad\int\frac{1}{\sqrt{a^2-x^2}}\,dx=-\cos^{-1}\frac xa+C\] \[\int\frac{1}{a^2+x^2}\,dx=\frac1a\tan^{-1}\frac xa+C\]
Proof

Let \(u=\dfrac xa\). Then \(\dfrac{du}{dx}=\dfrac1a\), and

\[\int\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-u^2}}\,\frac{du}{dx}\,dx=\sin^{-1}u\qquad\text{and}\qquad\int\frac{1}{1+u^2}\,\frac{du}{dx}\,dx=\tan^{-1}u.\]

A

\[\begin{aligned}\int\frac{1}{\sqrt{a^2-x^2}}\,dx&=\int\frac{1}{a\sqrt{1-\big(\frac xa\big)^2}}\,dx\\&=\int\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\big(\frac xa\big)^2}}\times\frac1a\,dx\\&=\sin^{-1}\frac xa+C.\end{aligned}\]

B

\[\begin{aligned}\int\frac{1}{a^2+x^2}\,dx&=\int\frac{1}{a^2\big(1+\big(\frac xa\big)^2\big)}\,dx\\&=\frac1a\int\frac{1}{1+\big(\frac xa\big)^2}\times\frac1a\,dx\\&=\frac1a\tan^{-1}\frac xa+C.\end{aligned}\]
How the proof works

Here \(a\) is a positive constant. Both proofs take out the factor \(a^2\), then use the reverse chain rule with \(u=\dfrac xa\). By the chain rule, \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\sin^{-1}u=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1-u^2}}\times\dfrac{du}{dx}\) and \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\tan^{-1}u=\dfrac{1}{1+u^2}\times\dfrac{du}{dx}\). Reversing these gives the two integrals in the first line of the proof.

In A, \(a^2-x^2=a^2\big(1-\big(\tfrac xa\big)^2\big)\), and \(\sqrt{a^2}=a\) because \(a>0\). In B, \(a^2+x^2=a^2\big(1+\big(\tfrac xa\big)^2\big)\).

In B, \(\tfrac{1}{a^2}\) was written as \(\tfrac1a\times\tfrac1a\): one \(\tfrac1a\) is taken outside the integral, and the other is the \(\tfrac{du}{dx}\) that the reverse chain rule needs. The \(\cos^{-1}\) form follows in the same way from

\[\frac{d}{dx}\big(-\cos^{-1}u\big)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-u^2}}\times\frac{du}{dx}.\]
Reading off \(a\)

Match the integrand with the box: the number in front of \(x^2\) must be 1. Then \(a^2\) is the other number, and \(a\) is its positive square root. For example, \(\sqrt{9-x^2}\) has \(a=3\), and \(8+x^2\) has \(a=\sqrt8=2\sqrt2\). A constant factor in the numerator is taken outside the integral first.

Take the coefficient of \(x^2\) out first

In integrals such as \(\displaystyle\int\frac{6}{49+25x^2}\,dx\) and \(\displaystyle\int\frac{1}{\sqrt{5-3x^2}}\,dx\), the coefficients of \(x^2\) need to be taken out first, so that Box 5 can be used.

  • For \(a^2+b^2x^2\), take out \(b^2\): \(\ a^2+b^2x^2=b^2\Big(\dfrac{a^2}{b^2}+x^2\Big)\).
  • For \(\sqrt{a^2-b^2x^2}\), take out \(\sqrt{b^2}=b\): \(\ \sqrt{a^2-b^2x^2}=\sqrt{b^2\Big(\dfrac{a^2}{b^2}-x^2\Big)}=b\sqrt{\dfrac{a^2}{b^2}-x^2}\).
Fuller versions of the standard forms

Because manipulating the constants in parts c and d (Worked example 8c, d below) is still difficult, some prefer to remember these fuller versions of the standard forms. These forms can be proven in the same manner as the forms with a single constant, or they can be developed from those forms in the same way as was done in parts c and d.

Developing Box 6 from Box 5

Here \(a\) and \(b\) are positive constants. Take out the coefficient of \(x^2\):

\[\begin{aligned}\int\frac{1}{\sqrt{a^2-b^2x^2}}\,dx&=\frac1b\int\frac{1}{\sqrt{\big(\frac ab\big)^2-x^2}}\,dx\\&=\frac1b\sin^{-1}\Big(x\div\frac ab\Big)+C\\&=\frac1b\sin^{-1}\frac{bx}{a}+C,\end{aligned}\] \[\begin{aligned}\int\frac{1}{a^2+b^2x^2}\,dx&=\frac{1}{b^2}\int\frac{1}{\big(\frac ab\big)^2+x^2}\,dx\\&=\frac{1}{b^2}\times\frac ba\tan^{-1}\Big(x\div\frac ab\Big)+C\\&=\frac{1}{ab}\tan^{-1}\frac{bx}{a}+C.\end{aligned}\]

In the same way, the \(\cos^{-1}\) form of Box 5 gives \(-\dfrac1b\cos^{-1}\dfrac{bx}{a}+C\).

6 Standard forms with two constants
\[\int\frac{1}{\sqrt{a^2-b^2x^2}}\,dx=\frac1b\sin^{-1}\frac{bx}{a}+C\qquad\text{OR}\qquad-\frac1b\cos^{-1}\frac{bx}{a}+C\] \[\int\frac{1}{a^2+b^2x^2}\,dx=\frac{1}{ab}\tan^{-1}\frac{bx}{a}+C\]

With these more general forms, parts c and d can be written down without any intermediate working.

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 5:the reverse chain rule}}
Extending the standard forms

In the usual way, the standard forms can be extended to give forms appropriate for the reverse chain rule. These forms are the reversals of the chain-rule standard forms at the end of Lesson 12A.

Box 3 from Lesson 12A
\[\frac{d}{dx}\sin^{-1}u=\frac{u'}{\sqrt{1-u^2}},\qquad\frac{d}{dx}\cos^{-1}u=\frac{-u'}{\sqrt{1-u^2}},\qquad\frac{d}{dx}\tan^{-1}u=\frac{u'}{1+u^2}.\]
7 The reverse chain rule
\[\begin{aligned}\int\frac{u'}{\sqrt{1-u^2}}\,dx&=\sin^{-1}u+C\\\int\frac{u'}{\sqrt{1-u^2}}\,dx&=-\cos^{-1}u+C\\\int\frac{u'}{1+u^2}\,dx&=\tan^{-1}u+C\end{aligned}\]

OR

\[\begin{aligned}\int\frac{f'(x)}{\sqrt{1-\big(f(x)\big)^2}}\,dx&=\sin^{-1}f(x)+C\\\int\frac{f'(x)}{\sqrt{1-\big(f(x)\big)^2}}\,dx&=-\cos^{-1}f(x)+C\\\int\frac{f'(x)}{1+\big(f(x)\big)^2}\,dx&=\tan^{-1}f(x)+C\end{aligned}\]
Finding \(u\)

Write the squared term as \(u^2\), so that \(u\) is the quantity being squared, and find \(u'\). The numerator must be \(u'\). If it is only a constant multiple of \(u'\), adjust the constant outside the integral, as in Worked example 9b.

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 6:given a derivative, find an integral}}
The primitives of the inverse trigonometric functions

As always, the result of a product-rule differentiation can be used to obtain an integral. In particular, this allows the primitives of the inverse trigonometric functions to be obtained.

The method
  • Differentiate \(x\times(\text{the inverse function})\) by the product rule. One term of the derivative is the inverse function itself.
  • Integrate both sides of the derivative. The integral of a derivative is the function itself.
  • Rearrange to make the integral of the inverse function the subject. The other integral left over is found by the reverse chain rule.

Solution

a Let \(y=x\sin^{-1}x\).
Using the product rule with \(u=x\) and \(v=\sin^{-1}x\),

\[\begin{aligned}\frac{dy}{dx}&=\sin^{-1}x+\frac{x}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}.\end{aligned}\]
{{svg:c12b_ex10}}
\[\begin{aligned}\text{Hence}\quad\int\sin^{-1}x\,dx+\int\frac{x}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}\,dx&=x\sin^{-1}x\\\int\sin^{-1}x\,dx&=x\sin^{-1}x-\int\frac{x}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}\,dx.\end{aligned}\]

Using the reverse chain rule,

\[\begin{aligned}-\int\frac{x}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}\,dx&=\tfrac12\int\big(1-x^2\big)^{-\frac12}\,(-2x)\,dx\\&=\tfrac12\times\big(1-x^2\big)^{\frac12}\times\frac21\\&=\sqrt{1-x^2},\end{aligned}\]

Let \(u=1-x^2\).
Then \(\dfrac{du}{dx}=-2x\).

\[\int u^{-\frac12}\frac{du}{dx}\,dx=u^{\frac12}\times2.\]
\[\text{so}\quad\int\sin^{-1}x\,dx=x\sin^{-1}x+\sqrt{1-x^2}+C.\]

b

\[\begin{aligned}\int_0^1\sin^{-1}x\,dx&=\Big[x\sin^{-1}x+\sqrt{1-x^2}\,\Big]_0^1\\&=\big(1\times\tfrac{\pi}{2}+0\big)-(0+1)\\&=\tfrac{\pi}{2}-1\text{ square units.}\end{aligned}\]

This means that the area between \(y=\sin^{-1}x\) and the \(y\)-axis is 1, and subtracting this area from the rectangle of area \(\tfrac{\pi}{2}\) in the diagram above gives the same value \(\tfrac{\pi}{2}-1\) for the shaded area.
