\[\begin{aligned}\int x\sqrt{x-2}\,dx&=\int(u^2+2)\,u\times2u\,du\\&=\int(2u^4+4u^2)\,du\\&=\tfrac25u^5+\tfrac43u^3+C\\&=\tfrac25(x-2)^{\frac52}+\tfrac43(x-2)^{\frac32}+C\end{aligned}\]

Let \(x=u^2+2\).
Then \(dx=2u\,du\),
and \(\sqrt{x-2}=u\).

Here \(x-2=u^2\) and \(u\ge0\), so \(u=\sqrt{x-2}\). Then \(u^5=(x-2)^{\frac52}\) and \(u^3=(x-2)^{\frac32}\).

b

\[\begin{aligned}\int_{-3}^{1}x\sqrt{1-x}\,dx&=\int_{2}^{0}(1-u^2)\,u\,(-2u)\,du\\&=-2\int_{2}^{0}(u^2-u^4)\,du\\&=-2\Big[\tfrac13u^3-\tfrac15u^5\Big]_{2}^{0}\\&=-2\Big(0-\big(\tfrac83-\tfrac{32}{5}\big)\Big)\\&=2\big(\tfrac83-\tfrac{32}{5}\big)\\&=2\times\big(-\tfrac{56}{15}\big)\\&=-\tfrac{112}{15}\end{aligned}\]

Let \(x=1-u^2\).
Then \(dx=-2u\,du\),
and \(\sqrt{1-x}=u\).
When \(x=-3\), \(u=2\),
when \(x=1\), \(u=0\).

When \(x=-3\), \(1-u^2=-3\), so \(u^2=4\) and \(u=2\) (taking \(u\ge0\)). The value is negative because \(x\sqrt{1-x}\lt0\) for \(-3\lt x\lt0\), and that part outweighs the part from \(0\) to \(1\).

Let \(x=4\sin u\). Then \(dx=4\cos u\,du\), and \(\sqrt{16-x^2}=4\cos u\). When \(x=2\sqrt2\), \(u=\tfrac{\pi}{4}\), when \(x=4\), \(u=\tfrac{\pi}{2}\).

\[\begin{aligned}\int_{2\sqrt2}^{4}\sqrt{16-x^2}\,dx&=\int_{\frac{\pi}{4}}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}4\cos u\times4\cos u\,du\\&=\int_{\frac{\pi}{4}}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}16\cos^2u\,du\\&=\int_{\frac{\pi}{4}}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}16\big(\tfrac12+\tfrac12\cos2u\big)\,du\\&=\int_{\frac{\pi}{4}}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}(8+8\cos2u)\,du\\&=\Big[8u+4\sin2u\Big]_{\frac{\pi}{4}}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}\\&=\big(4\pi+4\sin\pi\big)-\big(2\pi+4\sin\tfrac{\pi}{2}\big)\\&=(4\pi+0)-(2\pi+4)\\&=2\pi-4\end{aligned}\]

Here \(16-x^2=16-16\sin^2u=16\cos^2u\), and \(\cos u\ge0\) for \(\tfrac{\pi}{4}\le u\le\tfrac{\pi}{2}\). When \(x=2\sqrt2\), \(\sin u=\tfrac{2\sqrt2}{4}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\).

b The chord \(x=2\sqrt2\) meets the circle \(x^2+y^2=16\) at the angles \(45^\circ\) and \(-45^\circ\), so it subtends \(90^\circ=\tfrac{\pi}{2}\) at the centre. The region is half of that segment:

\[\begin{aligned}\int_{2\sqrt2}^{4}\sqrt{16-x^2}\,dx&=\tfrac12\times\tfrac12\times4^2\big(\tfrac{\pi}{2}-\sin\tfrac{\pi}{2}\big)\\&=4\big(\tfrac{\pi}{2}-1\big)\\&=2\pi-4\end{aligned}\]

Tutor demo.

a Take \(-\tfrac{\pi}{2}\lt\theta\lt\tfrac{\pi}{2}\), so \(\sec\theta\gt0\).

\[\begin{aligned}\int\frac{1}{(9+x^2)^{\frac32}}\,dx&=\int\frac{3\sec^2\theta}{27\sec^3\theta}\,d\theta\\&=\tfrac19\int\cos\theta\,d\theta\\&=\tfrac19\sin\theta+C\\&=\frac{x}{9\sqrt{9+x^2}}+C\end{aligned}\]

Let \(x=3\tan\theta\).
Then \(dx=3\sec^2\theta\,d\theta\),
and \((9+x^2)^{\frac32}=27\sec^3\theta\).

Here \(9+x^2=9+9\tan^2\theta=9\sec^2\theta\), so \((9+x^2)^{\frac32}=(3\sec\theta)^3=27\sec^3\theta\). To return to \(x\): \(\tan\theta=\tfrac{x}{3}\), so \(\sec\theta=\sqrt{1+\tan^2\theta}=\tfrac{\sqrt{9+x^2}}{3}\) and \(\sin\theta=\tfrac{\tan\theta}{\sec\theta}=\tfrac{x}{\sqrt{9+x^2}}\).

b Take \(0\le\theta\le\pi\), so \(\sin\theta\ge0\).

\[\begin{aligned}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{4-x^2}}\,dx&=\int_{\frac{\pi}{2}}^{\frac{\pi}{3}}\frac{-2\sin\theta}{2\sin\theta}\,d\theta\\&=\int_{\frac{\pi}{2}}^{\frac{\pi}{3}}(-1)\,d\theta\\&=\Big[-\theta\Big]_{\frac{\pi}{2}}^{\frac{\pi}{3}}\\&=-\tfrac{\pi}{3}+\tfrac{\pi}{2}\\&=\tfrac{\pi}{6}\end{aligned}\]

Let \(x=2\cos\theta\).
Then \(dx=-2\sin\theta\,d\theta\),
and \(\sqrt{4-x^2}=2\sin\theta\).
When \(x=0\), \(\theta=\tfrac{\pi}{2}\),
when \(x=1\), \(\theta=\tfrac{\pi}{3}\).

Here \(4-x^2=4-4\cos^2\theta=4\sin^2\theta\). When \(x=1\), \(\cos\theta=\tfrac12\). The answer agrees with \(\sin^{-1}\tfrac12-\sin^{-1}0=\tfrac{\pi}{6}\) from Lesson 12B.

PP 1Sydney Girls High School, 2023, Trial Higher School Certificate Examination, Mathematics Extension 1, Q5
\[\begin{aligned}\int_0^{\sqrt3}\frac{1}{\big(x^2+1\big)^2}\,dx&=\int_0^{\frac{\pi}{3}}\frac{\sec^2\theta}{\big(\sec^2\theta\big)^2}\,d\theta\\&=\int_0^{\frac{\pi}{3}}\frac{1}{\sec^2\theta}\,d\theta\\&=\int_0^{\frac{\pi}{3}}\cos^2\theta\,d\theta\end{aligned}\]

Let \(x=\tan\theta\).
Then \(dx=\sec^2\theta\,d\theta\),
and \(x^2+1=\sec^2\theta\).
When \(x=0\), \(\theta=0\),
when \(x=\sqrt3\), \(\theta=\tfrac{\pi}{3}\).

Answer (A). Here \(x^2+1=\tan^2\theta+1=\sec^2\theta\), and \(\tan\theta=\sqrt3\) gives \(\theta=\tfrac{\pi}{3}\). Option (B) forgets to square \(x^2+1\). Options (C) and (D) use the upper limit \(\tfrac{\pi}{6}\), which belongs to \(x=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt3}\), not to \(x=\sqrt3\).

PP 2North Sydney Boys High School, 2022, HSC Course Assessment Task 3-Trial Examination, Mathematics Extension 1, Q4

Let \(y=\sin\theta\), with \(0\le\theta\le\tfrac{\pi}{2}\). Then \(dy=\cos\theta\,d\theta\) and \(\sqrt{1-y^2}=\sqrt{1-\sin^2\theta}=\cos\theta\). When \(y=0\), \(\theta=0\), and when \(y=\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\), \(\theta=\tfrac{\pi}{3}\). So

\[\begin{aligned}\int_0^{\frac{\sqrt3}{2}}\sqrt{1-y^2}\,dy&=\int_0^{\frac{\pi}{3}}\cos\theta\times\cos\theta\,d\theta\\&=\int_0^{\frac{\pi}{3}}\big(\tfrac12+\tfrac12\cos2\theta\big)\,d\theta\\&=\Big[\tfrac12\theta+\tfrac14\sin2\theta\Big]_0^{\frac{\pi}{3}}\\&=\big(\tfrac{\pi}{6}+\tfrac14\sin\tfrac{2\pi}{3}\big)-\big(0+\tfrac14\sin0\big)\\&=\tfrac{\pi}{6}+\tfrac14\times\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\\&=\tfrac{\pi}{6}+\tfrac{\sqrt3}{8}\end{aligned}\]

Answer (C). As a check, the integral is the area between the \(y\)-axis and the quarter circle \(x=\sqrt{1-y^2}\), from \(y=0\) to \(y=\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\). It is a sector of angle \(\tfrac{\pi}{3}\) between the \(x\)-axis and the radius to \(\big(\tfrac12,\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\big)\) (area \(\tfrac12\times1^2\times\tfrac{\pi}{3}=\tfrac{\pi}{6}\)) plus the right-angled triangle with vertices \(O\), \(\big(0,\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\big)\) and \(\big(\tfrac12,\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\big)\) (area \(\tfrac12\times\tfrac12\times\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}=\tfrac{\sqrt3}{8}\)). Option (A) leaves out the triangle. Options (B) and (D) use \(\tfrac{\pi}{3}\), the angle itself, where the primitive \(\tfrac12\theta\) gives \(\tfrac{\pi}{6}\).

Take \(0\le\theta\le\pi\), so that \(\sin\theta\ge0\) and \(\sqrt{1-\cos^2\theta}=\sqrt{\sin^2\theta}=\sin\theta\). For the limits, \(\theta=\cos^{-1}\big(-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\big)=\tfrac{3\pi}{4}\) and \(\theta=\cos^{-1}\tfrac12=\tfrac{\pi}{3}\).

Let \(x=\cos\theta\). Then \(dx=-\sin\theta\,d\theta\), and \(\sqrt{1-x^2}=\sin\theta\). When \(x=-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\), \(\theta=\tfrac{3\pi}{4}\), when \(x=\tfrac12\), \(\theta=\tfrac{\pi}{3}\).

\[\begin{aligned}\int_{-\frac{1}{\sqrt2}}^{\frac12}\frac{x}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}\,dx&=\int_{\frac{3\pi}{4}}^{\frac{\pi}{3}}\frac{\cos\theta}{\sin\theta}\times(-\sin\theta)\,d\theta\\&=-\int_{\frac{3\pi}{4}}^{\frac{\pi}{3}}\cos\theta\,d\theta\\&=\int_{\frac{\pi}{3}}^{\frac{3\pi}{4}}\cos\theta\,d\theta\\&=\Big[\sin\theta\Big]_{\frac{\pi}{3}}^{\frac{3\pi}{4}}\\&=\sin\tfrac{3\pi}{4}-\sin\tfrac{\pi}{3}\\&=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}-\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\\&=\tfrac{\sqrt2-\sqrt3}{2}\end{aligned}\]

Reversing the limits changes the sign of the integral. The answer is negative because the integrand is negative for \(x\lt0\), and that part outweighs the positive part.

Take \(u\ge4\). Then \(x-1=(u-4)^2\), so \(\sqrt{x-1}=u-4\) (the positive square root) and \(4+\sqrt{x-1}=u\). For the limits: when \(x=2\), \((u-4)^2=1\), so \(u-4=1\) and \(u=5\); when \(x=5\), \((u-4)^2=4\), so \(u-4=2\) and \(u=6\).
