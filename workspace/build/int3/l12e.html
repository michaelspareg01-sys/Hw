Lesson 12E

Further integration by substitution

The second stage of integration by substitution reverses the procedure of Lesson 12D and replaces \(x\) by a function of \(u\). The substitutions are therefore of the form ‘Let \(x=\) some function of \(u\).’ A substitution such as \(x=1-u^2\) removes a square root, and a trigonometric substitution such as \(x=6\sin u\) turns \(\sqrt{36-x^2}\) into \(6\cos u\). In a definite integral the limits are changed as well, so there is no need to return to \(x\).

Substituting \(x\) by a function of \(u\)

The second stage of integration by substitution reverses the procedure of Lesson 12D and replaces \(x\) by a function of \(u\). The substitutions are therefore of the form ‘Let \(x=\) some function of \(u\).’

As a first example, here is a quite different substitution which solves the integral given in a worked example of the last section.

The method, step by step

The earlier example is Lesson 12D Example 18, \(\displaystyle\int_0^1x\sqrt{1-x}\,dx\), where the substitution was \(u=1-x\).

Write the substitution as \(x=g(u)\). Then:

  • Differentiate: \(\dfrac{dx}{du}=g'(u)\), so \(dx=g'(u)\,du\). The \(dx\) in the integral must be replaced by \(g'(u)\,du\).
  • Replace every \(x\) in the integrand by \(g(u)\). The function \(g\) is chosen so that the awkward part of the integrand, usually a square root, becomes a simple expression in \(u\).
  • In a definite integral, change the limits from values of \(x\) to values of \(u\). Then there is no need to return to \(x\).
  • In an indefinite integral, the answer must be written in terms of \(x\) at the end. Use the substitution backwards, for example \(u=\sqrt{1-x}\) when \(x=1-u^2\).
The substitution \(x=1-u^2\) in detail

Let \(x=1-u^2\). Then

\[\begin{aligned}\frac{dx}{du}&=-2u\\dx&=-2u\,du\end{aligned}\]
\[\begin{aligned}1-x&=1-(1-u^2)\\&=u^2\\\sqrt{1-x}&=u\end{aligned}\]

The positive square root \(u\ge0\) is taken, so \(u=\sqrt{1-x}\). For the limits: when \(x=0\), \(1-u^2=0\), so \(u^2=1\) and \(u=1\); when \(x=1\), \(1-u^2=1\), so \(u^2=0\) and \(u=0\).

Change everything
  • Never just write \(du\) in place of \(dx\). Here \(dx=-2u\,du\), and the factor \(-2u\) stays in the integral.
  • The new limits may come out in the reverse order (here from \(u=1\) to \(u=0\)). Keep them in that order, and take the minus sign outside if it helps.

A trigonometric substitution

The next integral uses a trigonometric substitution, but can also be done using areas of segments.

Why \(x=a\sin u\) removes \(\sqrt{a^2-x^2}\)

When the integrand contains \(\sqrt{a^2-x^2}\), where \(a>0\), let \(x=a\sin u\). Then \(dx=a\cos u\,du\), and

\[\begin{aligned}a^2-x^2&=a^2-a^2\sin^2u\\&=a^2(1-\sin^2u)\\&=a^2\cos^2u,\end{aligned}\]

so \(\sqrt{a^2-x^2}=a\cos u\), because \(\cos u\ge0\) when \(-\tfrac{\pi}{2}\le u\le\tfrac{\pi}{2}\). The integral usually becomes a multiple of \(\cos^2u\), which is integrated with \(\cos^2u=\tfrac12+\tfrac12\cos2u\) (Lesson 12C).

The area of a segment (Year 11)

A chord of a circle of radius \(r\) that subtends an angle \(\theta\) (in radians) at the centre cuts off a segment. The segment is the sector minus the isosceles triangle formed by the two radii and the chord:

\[\text{segment}=\tfrac12r^2\theta-\tfrac12r^2\sin\theta=\tfrac12r^2(\theta-\sin\theta).\]

The sector has area \(\tfrac12r^2\theta\). The triangle has two sides \(r\) with the angle \(\theta\) between them, so its area is \(\tfrac12r\times r\times\sin\theta\).

A note on the values of \(u\)

Careful readers may notice a problem here, in that given the value \(x=3\sqrt2\), \(u\) is determined by \(\sin u=\tfrac12\sqrt2\), so there are infinitely many possible values of \(u\). A similar problem occurred in the previous worked example, where \(0=1-u^2\) had two solutions. These problems arise because the functions involved in the substitutions were \(x=1-u^2\) and \(x=6\sin u\), whose inverses were not functions. A full account of all this would require substitutions by restrictions of the functions given above so that they had inverse functions. In practice, however, this is rarely necessary, and it is certainly not a concern of this course.

As a rule of thumb, work with positive square roots, and with trigonometric functions, work in the same quadrants as were involved in the definitions of the inverse trigonometric functions in Chapter 17 of the Year 11 book.

10 Integration using the reverse chain rule and substitution

Here is a summary of Lessons 12D and 12E. The chain rule can be used in reverse in various ways to find integrals.

  • The reverse chain rule straightforwardly reverses the steps of differentiation by the chain rule.
  • A reverse-chain-rule formula can be developed for each standard form.
  • Substitution can be done by, ‘Let \(u\) be a function of \(x\).’
  • Substitution can be done by, ‘Let \(x\) be a function of \(u\).’
  • When substituting into a definite integral, the limits of integration can be substituted as well, so that there is no need ever to return from \(u\) to \(x\).
The rule of thumb in practice (\(a>0\))
  • \(x=1-u^2\) and similar: take \(u\ge0\), so that \(u=\sqrt{1-x}\).
  • \(x=a\sin\theta\): take \(-\tfrac{\pi}{2}\le\theta\le\tfrac{\pi}{2}\), so \(\theta=\sin^{-1}\dfrac{x}{a}\) and \(\cos\theta\ge0\).
  • \(x=a\cos\theta\): take \(0\le\theta\le\pi\), so \(\theta=\cos^{-1}\dfrac{x}{a}\) and \(\sin\theta\ge0\).
  • \(x=a\tan\theta\): take \(-\tfrac{\pi}{2}\lt\theta\lt\tfrac{\pi}{2}\), so \(\theta=\tan^{-1}\dfrac{x}{a}\) and \(\cos\theta\gt0\), \(\sec\theta\gt0\).
Which trigonometric substitution? (Exercise 12E Questions 8 to 12)

Each substitution turns a sum or difference of squares into a single square, using \(\sin^2\theta+\cos^2\theta=1\) and \(\tan^2\theta+1=\sec^2\theta\) (Lesson 12C):

\[\begin{aligned}x=a\sin\theta:&\quad a^2-x^2=a^2(1-\sin^2\theta)=a^2\cos^2\theta\\x=a\cos\theta:&\quad a^2-x^2=a^2(1-\cos^2\theta)=a^2\sin^2\theta\\x=a\tan\theta:&\quad a^2+x^2=a^2(1+\tan^2\theta)=a^2\sec^2\theta\\x=a\sec\theta:&\quad x^2-a^2=a^2(\sec^2\theta-1)=a^2\tan^2\theta\end{aligned}\]
Returning to \(x\) in an indefinite integral

The answer comes out as a function of \(\theta\), such as \(\tan\theta\) or \(\sin\theta\), and must be written in terms of \(x\). With \(x=a\sin\theta\), \(\sin\theta=\dfrac{x}{a}\) and \(\cos\theta=\sqrt{1-\sin^2\theta}=\dfrac{\sqrt{a^2-x^2}}{a}\), so

\[\tan\theta=\frac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta}=\frac{x}{\sqrt{a^2-x^2}}.\]

With \(x=a\tan\theta\), \(\sec\theta=\sqrt{1+\tan^2\theta}=\dfrac{\sqrt{a^2+x^2}}{a}\), so \(\sin\theta=\dfrac{\tan\theta}{\sec\theta}=\dfrac{x}{\sqrt{a^2+x^2}}\). The right-angled triangles below give the same results when \(\theta\) is acute.
