Lesson 12D

Integration by substitution

The reverse chain rule as we have been using it so far does not cover all the situations where the chain rule can be used in integration. This section and the next develop a more general method called integration by substitution. The first stage, covered in this lesson, begins by translating the reverse chain rule into a slightly more flexible notation. It involves substitutions of the form ‘Let \(u=\) some function of \(x\).’
{{sec:teal:key:Concept 1:the reverse chain rule, an example}}
The reverse chain rule: an example

Here is an example of the reverse chain rule as we have been using it. The working is set out in full on the right.

Tip: the reverse chain rule (Part 1, Lesson 5I)

Let \(u\) be a function of \(x\), and let \(F(u)\) be a primitive of \(f(u)\), so that \(F'(u)=f(u)\). The chain rule gives

\[\frac{d}{dx}F(u)=F'(u)\,\frac{du}{dx}=f(u)\,\frac{du}{dx}.\]

Reading this backwards gives the reverse chain rule. With \(f(u)=u^n\), where \(n\ne-1\), the primitive is \(F(u)=\dfrac{u^{n+1}}{n+1}\):

\[\int f(u)\,\frac{du}{dx}\,dx=F(u)+C\qquad\text{and}\qquad\int u^n\,\frac{du}{dx}\,dx=\frac{u^{n+1}}{n+1}+C.\]

Look for a function \(u\) whose derivative \(\frac{du}{dx}\) is also a factor of the integrand, apart from a constant.

Find \(\displaystyle\int x(1-x^2)^4\,dx\).
Solution
\[\begin{aligned}\int x(1-x^2)^4\,dx&=-\tfrac12\int(-2x)(1-x^2)^4\,dx\\&=-\tfrac12\times\tfrac15(1-x^2)^5+C\\&=-\tfrac{1}{10}(1-x^2)^5+C\end{aligned}\]

Let \(u=1-x^2\).
Then \(\dfrac{du}{dx}=-2x\),
and \(\displaystyle\int u^4\,\frac{du}{dx}\,dx=\tfrac15u^5\).

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 2:rewriting the example as integration by substitution}}
Rewriting this example as integration by substitution

We shall now rewrite this using a new notation. The key to this new notation is that the derivative \(\dfrac{du}{dx}\) is treated as a fraction. The \(du\) and the \(dx\) are split apart, so that the statement

\[\frac{du}{dx}=-2x\qquad\text{is written instead as}\qquad du=-2x\,dx.\]

The new variable \(u\) no longer remains in the working column on the right, but is brought over into the main sequence of the solution on the left.

Tip: why the new notation is valid

Let \(F(u)\) be a primitive of \(f(u)\). In the variable \(u\), \(\displaystyle\int f(u)\,du=F(u)+C\). In the variable \(x\), the reverse chain rule of Concept 1 gives \(\displaystyle\int f(u)\,\frac{du}{dx}\,dx=F(u)+C\). Both integrals equal \(F(u)+C\), so

\[\int f(u)\,\frac{du}{dx}\,dx=\int f(u)\,du.\]

So \(\dfrac{du}{dx}\,dx\) may always be replaced by \(du\), exactly as if \(\dfrac{du}{dx}\) were a fraction and the two \(dx\) cancelled.

Tip: the steps of integration by substitution
  1. Let \(u=\) some function of \(x\). Often it is the inside function, whose derivative is also a factor of the integrand.
  2. Differentiate, and write the result with \(du\) and \(dx\) split apart, for example \(du=-2x\,dx\).
  3. Rearrange this so that it matches the rest of the integrand, for example \(x\,dx=-\tfrac12\,du\).
  4. Rewrite the whole integral in terms of \(u\) and \(du\). No \(x\) and no \(dx\) may remain.
  5. Integrate with respect to \(u\).
  6. Replace \(u\) by its expression in \(x\), and add the constant of integration.
Tip: common errors
  • Keep the constant factor. From \(du=-2x\,dx\), divide both sides by \(-2\): \(x\,dx=-\tfrac12\,du\), not \(-2\,du\).
  • An integral such as \(\displaystyle\int x\,u^4\,du\) is meaningless: the \(x\) and the \(dx\) must both be replaced before integrating.
  • The answer to an indefinite integral in \(x\) must be given in terms of \(x\) again.

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 3:an advance on the reverse chain rule}}
An advance on the reverse chain rule

Some integrals that can be done in this way could only be done by the reverse chain rule in a rather clumsy manner.

Tip: writing the leftover \(x\) in terms of \(u\)

In \(\displaystyle\int x\sqrt{1-x}\,dx\), the derivative of the inside function \(1-x\) is \(-1\), so the factor \(x\) is not its derivative.

The substitution \(u=1-x\) deals with this. It gives \(du=-dx\), and the leftover \(x\) is written in terms of \(u\) by rearranging \(u=1-x\):

\[x=1-u.\]

The integrand then becomes \((1-u)\sqrt u\), which expands into a sum of powers of \(u\).

Tip: expanding with the index laws

Write \(\sqrt u=u^{\frac12}\). Then \(u\sqrt u=u^1\times u^{\frac12}=u^{\frac32}\), and

\[(1-u)\sqrt u=u^{\frac12}-u^{\frac32}\qquad\text{and}\qquad(u-1)\sqrt u=u^{\frac32}-u^{\frac12}.\]

Each power is then integrated with \(\displaystyle\int u^n\,du=\frac{u^{n+1}}{n+1}+C\), for example \(\displaystyle\int u^{\frac32}\,du=\frac{u^{\frac52}}{\frac52}+C=\tfrac25u^{\frac52}+C\).

Worked example 17From the text: keep for homework
Find \(\displaystyle\int x\sqrt{1-x}\,dx\), using the substitution \(u=1-x\).
Solution
\[\begin{aligned}\int x\sqrt{1-x}\,dx&=\int(1-u)\sqrt u\,(-du)\\&=\int\big(u^{\frac32}-u^{\frac12}\big)\,du\\&=\tfrac25u^{\frac52}-\tfrac23u^{\frac32}+C\\&=\tfrac25(1-x)^{\frac52}-\tfrac23(1-x)^{\frac32}+C\end{aligned}\]

Let \(u=1-x\).
Then \(du=-dx\),
and \(x=1-u\).

Tip: the second line of Example 17

The second line takes the minus sign inside: \(-(1-u)\sqrt u=(u-1)\sqrt u=u^{\frac32}-u^{\frac12}\).

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 4:substituting the limits of integration in a definite integral}}
Substituting the limits of integration in a definite integral

A great advantage of this new method is that the limits of integration can be changed from values of \(x\) to values of \(u\). There is then no need ever to go back to \(x\). The first worked example below repeats the previous integrand, but this time within a definite integral.

Tip: finding the new limits
  • Put each \(x\)-limit into the substitution to find the matching \(u\)-limit, and record this in the working column: ‘When \(x=a\), \(u=\dots\)’.
  • The \(u\)-value that comes from the lower \(x\)-limit is the lower \(u\)-limit, even when it is the larger number. Example 18 below gives \(\displaystyle\int_{1}^{0}\).
  • Evaluate the \(u\)-integral between the \(u\)-limits.
Tip: reversing the limits (Part 1, Lesson 5C)

Reversing the limits of an integral changes its sign:

\[\int_{b}^{a}f(u)\,du=-\int_{a}^{b}f(u)\,du.\]

So a minus sign in front of an integral can be removed by reversing its limits, for example \(\displaystyle-\int_{1}^{0}u^5\,du=\int_{0}^{1}u^5\,du\). Either form may be used, and both give the same value.

Tip: never mix the variables
  • The limits of an integral with \(du\) must be values of \(u\). Keeping the \(x\)-limits on a \(u\)-integral is wrong.
  • Do not substitute back to \(x\) and then use the \(u\)-limits.

Worked example 18From the text: keep for homework
Find \(\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}x\sqrt{1-x}\,dx\), using the substitution \(u=1-x\).
Solution
\[\begin{aligned}\int_{0}^{1}x\sqrt{1-x}\,dx&=-\int_{1}^{0}(1-u)\sqrt u\,du\\&=-\int_{1}^{0}\big(u^{\frac12}-u^{\frac32}\big)\,du\\&=-\Big[\tfrac23u^{\frac32}-\tfrac25u^{\frac52}\Big]_{1}^{0}\\&=-0+\big(\tfrac23-\tfrac25\big)\\&=\tfrac{4}{15}\end{aligned}\]

Let \(u=1-x\).
Then \(du=-dx\),
and \(x=1-u\).
When \(x=0\), \(u=1\),
when \(x=1\), \(u=0\).

Tip: the arithmetic in Example 18

The bracket is the value at the upper limit minus the value at the lower limit:

\[-\Big[\tfrac23u^{\frac32}-\tfrac25u^{\frac52}\Big]_{1}^{0}=-\Big(\big(0-0\big)-\big(\tfrac23-\tfrac25\big)\Big)=-0+\big(\tfrac23-\tfrac25\big)\]

At \(u=1\), the primitive is \(\tfrac23\times1^{\frac32}-\tfrac25\times1^{\frac52}=\tfrac23-\tfrac25\), and \(\tfrac23-\tfrac25=\tfrac{10}{15}-\tfrac{6}{15}=\tfrac{4}{15}\).

Worked example 19From the text: keep for homework
Find \(\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\pi}\sin x\cos^6x\,dx\), using the substitution \(u=\cos x\).
Solution
\[\begin{aligned}\int_{0}^{\pi}\sin x\cos^6x\,dx&=-\int_{1}^{-1}u^6\,du\\&=-\tfrac17\Big[u^7\Big]_{1}^{-1}\\&=-\tfrac17\times(-1)+\tfrac17\times1\\&=\tfrac27\end{aligned}\]

Let \(u=\cos x\).
Then \(du=-\sin x\,dx\).
When \(x=0\), \(u=1\),
when \(x=\pi\), \(u=-1\).

Tip: the bracket in Example 19

The bracket is the value at the upper limit minus the value at the lower limit:

\[-\tfrac17\Big[u^7\Big]_{1}^{-1}=-\tfrac17\big((-1)^7-1^7\big)=-\tfrac17\times(-1)+\tfrac17\times1\]
