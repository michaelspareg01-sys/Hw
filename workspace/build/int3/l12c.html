Lesson 12C

Further trigonometric integrals

The principal purpose of this section is the integration of \(\sin^2x\) and \(\cos^2x\). The keys are two forms of the \(\cos2\theta\) formulae: \(\sin^2\theta=\tfrac12-\tfrac12\cos2\theta\) and \(\cos^2\theta=\tfrac12+\tfrac12\cos2\theta\). Trigonometric integrals in general are quickly reviewed, the squares of the other trigonometric functions are integrated using the Pythagorean identities, and the products-to-sums formulae turn products such as \(\sin3x\cos2x\) into sums. Last, reverse-chain-rule integrations prepare for the next two sections on substitution.
{{sec:teal:key:Concept 1:six standard forms for integration}}
8 Six standard integrals

Reversing the derivatives of the six standard forms gives six standard integrals:

\[\begin{aligned}\int\cos x\,dx&=\sin x+C\\\int\sec^2x\,dx&=\tan x+C\\{}^*\!\!\int\sec x\tan x\,dx&=\sec x+C\end{aligned}\]
\[\begin{aligned}\int\sin x\,dx&=-\cos x+C\\{}^*\!\!\int\operatorname{cosec}^2x\,dx&=-\cot x+C\\{}^*\!\!\int\operatorname{cosec}x\cot x\,dx&=-\operatorname{cosec}x+C\end{aligned}\]

The full list is there only for completeness, and so that the patterns can be seen. The three integrals marked * need not be memorised. They are the reversals of derivatives in Question 19 of Exercise 7B.

Where the three starred integrals come from

Write each reciprocal function in terms of \(\sin x\) and \(\cos x\), and differentiate with the chain rule or the quotient rule.

\[\begin{aligned}\frac{d}{dx}\sec x&=\frac{d}{dx}(\cos x)^{-1}\\&=-(\cos x)^{-2}\times(-\sin x)\\&=\frac{1}{\cos x}\times\frac{\sin x}{\cos x}\\&=\sec x\tan x\end{aligned}\]
\[\begin{aligned}\frac{d}{dx}(-\operatorname{cosec}x)&=\frac{d}{dx}\big(-(\sin x)^{-1}\big)\\&=(\sin x)^{-2}\times\cos x\\&=\frac{1}{\sin x}\times\frac{\cos x}{\sin x}\\&=\operatorname{cosec}x\cot x\end{aligned}\]
\[\begin{aligned}\frac{d}{dx}(-\cot x)=\frac{d}{dx}\Big(-\frac{\cos x}{\sin x}\Big)&=-\frac{(-\sin x)\sin x-\cos x\cos x}{\sin^2x}\\&=\frac{\sin^2x+\cos^2x}{\sin^2x}\\&=\frac{1}{\sin^2x}=\operatorname{cosec}^2x\end{aligned}\]

Reading each line backwards gives the three starred integrals.

A linear function of \(x\) inside (Part 2, Lesson 7D)

When the angle is \(ax+b\), divide the standard primitive by \(a\), the derivative of \(ax+b\):

\[\begin{aligned}\int\cos(ax+b)\,dx&=\tfrac1a\sin(ax+b)+C\\\int\sin(ax+b)\,dx&=-\tfrac1a\cos(ax+b)+C\\\int\sec^2(ax+b)\,dx&=\tfrac1a\tan(ax+b)+C\end{aligned}\]

Check by differentiating: for example \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\big(\tfrac1a\sin(ax+b)\big)=\tfrac1a\times a\cos(ax+b)=\cos(ax+b)\). Many integrals in this lesson use this rule.

Expressing \(\sin^2\theta\) and \(\cos^2\theta\) in terms of \(\cos2\theta\) (Section 11A)

These two integrals are very important. The keys to finding them are the two further forms of the \(\cos2\theta\) formulae that we established in Section 11A,

\[\sin^2\theta=\tfrac12-\tfrac12\cos2\theta\qquad\text{and}\qquad\cos^2\theta=\tfrac12+\tfrac12\cos2\theta,\]

and it is better to remember these identities rather than the actual integrals.

Where the two forms come from

Start from \(\cos2\theta=\cos^2\theta-\sin^2\theta\). Using \(\sin^2\theta=1-\cos^2\theta\), then \(\cos^2\theta=1-\sin^2\theta\),

\[\cos2\theta=2\cos^2\theta-1\qquad\text{and}\qquad\cos2\theta=1-2\sin^2\theta.\]

From \(\cos2\theta=2\cos^2\theta-1\),

\[\begin{aligned}2\cos^2\theta&=1+\cos2\theta\\\cos^2\theta&=\tfrac12+\tfrac12\cos2\theta.\end{aligned}\]

From \(\cos2\theta=1-2\sin^2\theta\),

\[\begin{aligned}2\sin^2\theta&=1-\cos2\theta\\\sin^2\theta&=\tfrac12-\tfrac12\cos2\theta.\end{aligned}\]
The angle doubles

The square on the left becomes a cosine of the double angle on the right, and that can be integrated with the standard forms.

  • Replacing \(\theta\) by \(3x\) gives \(\sin^23x=\tfrac12-\tfrac12\cos6x\), with \(6x\), not \(3x\). Replacing \(\theta\) by \(\tfrac12x\) gives \(\cos^2\tfrac12x=\tfrac12+\tfrac12\cos x\).
  • Then divide by the coefficient of \(x\) in the new angle: \(\displaystyle\int\cos6x\,dx=\tfrac16\sin6x+C\).
  • \(\displaystyle\int\sin^2x\,dx\) is not \(\tfrac13\sin^3x+C\). Differentiating \(\tfrac13\sin^3x\) gives \(\sin^2x\cos x\), not \(\sin^2x\).
Higher even powers

For \(\sin^4x\) or \(\cos^4x\), write the power as a square of a square, for example \(\cos^4x=(\cos^2x)^2\). Replace \(\cos^2x\) using the identity, expand, and use the identity again on the \(\cos^22x\) term that appears (see Exercise 12C Question 10).

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 3:the squares of all six trigonometric functions}}
The squares of all six functions

Here are the primitives of the squares of all six trigonometric functions. Two are given as standard forms. The other four are given only in terms of the identities that are needed to obtain them.

The identities for \(\tan^2x\) and \(\cot^2x\)

For \(\tan^2x\) and \(\cot^2x\), divide \(\sin^2x+\cos^2x=1\) first by \(\cos^2x\) and then by \(\sin^2x\), as shown below.

\[\begin{aligned}\frac{\sin^2x}{\cos^2x}+\frac{\cos^2x}{\cos^2x}&=\frac{1}{\cos^2x}\\\tan^2x+1&=\sec^2x\\\tan^2x&=\sec^2x-1\end{aligned}\]
\[\begin{aligned}\frac{\sin^2x}{\sin^2x}+\frac{\cos^2x}{\sin^2x}&=\frac{1}{\sin^2x}\\1+\cot^2x&=\operatorname{cosec}^2x\\\cot^2x&=\operatorname{cosec}^2x-1\end{aligned}\]
9 Integrating the squares of the trigonometric functions
\[\begin{aligned}\int\sin^2x\,dx&=\int\big(\tfrac12-\tfrac12\cos2x\big)\,dx\\\int\sec^2x\,dx&=\tan x+C\\\int\tan^2x\,dx&=\int\big(\sec^2x-1\big)\,dx\end{aligned}\]
\[\begin{aligned}\int\cos^2x\,dx&=\int\big(\tfrac12+\tfrac12\cos2x\big)\,dx\\{}^*\!\!\int\operatorname{cosec}^2x\,dx&=-\cot x+C\\{}^*\!\!\int\cot^2x\,dx&=\int\big(\operatorname{cosec}^2x-1\big)\,dx\end{aligned}\]

The forms marked * are there for completeness.

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 4:products to sums (reviewed from Section 11A)}}
The products-to-sums formulae

Exercise 12C Question 6 uses these formulae from Section 11A. They come from the compound-angle formulae

\[\begin{aligned}\sin(A+B)&=\sin A\cos B+\cos A\sin B\\\sin(A-B)&=\sin A\cos B-\cos A\sin B\end{aligned}\]
\[\begin{aligned}\cos(A+B)&=\cos A\cos B-\sin A\sin B\\\cos(A-B)&=\cos A\cos B+\sin A\sin B\end{aligned}\]

Adding and subtracting each pair, the other terms cancel:

\[\begin{aligned}\sin(A+B)+\sin(A-B)&=2\sin A\cos B\\\sin(A+B)-\sin(A-B)&=2\cos A\sin B\\\cos(A+B)+\cos(A-B)&=2\cos A\cos B\\\cos(A+B)-\cos(A-B)&=-2\sin A\sin B\end{aligned}\]
Products to sums
\[\begin{aligned}2\sin A\cos B&=\sin(A+B)+\sin(A-B)\\2\cos A\sin B&=\sin(A+B)-\sin(A-B)\\2\cos A\cos B&=\cos(A+B)+\cos(A-B)\\-2\sin A\sin B&=\cos(A+B)-\cos(A-B)\end{aligned}\]

A product of a sine and a cosine, two cosines, or two sines cannot be integrated directly. Rewritten as a sum, each term is a standard form with a linear angle.

Trigonometric integrals using the reverse chain rule

It remains to demonstrate the use of the reverse chain rule in trigonometric integrals, in preparation for the next two sections on substitution.

The reverse chain rule (Parts 1 and 2)

Let \(u\) be a function of \(x\). The chain rule gives \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\dfrac{u^{n+1}}{n+1}=u^n\,\dfrac{du}{dx}\) and \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\log_e|u|=\dfrac{1}{u}\,\dfrac{du}{dx}\), so

\[\int u^n\,\frac{du}{dx}\,dx=\frac{u^{n+1}}{n+1}+C\quad(n\ne-1)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\int\frac{u'}{u}\,dx=\log_e|u|+C.\]

Look for a function \(u\) whose derivative \(u'\) is also a factor of the integrand, apart from a constant. With trigonometric functions, \(u=\sin x\) has \(u'=\cos x\), and \(u=\cos x\) has \(u'=-\sin x\). Set out the working for \(u\) in a column on the right.

Integrate \(\cot x\) using the reverse chain rule.
Solution
\[\begin{aligned}\int\cot x\,dx&=\int\frac{\cos x}{\sin x}\,dx\\&=\log_e|\sin x|+C\end{aligned}\]

Let \(u=\sin x\).
Then \(u'=\cos x\),
and \(\displaystyle\int\frac{u'}{u}\,dx=\log_e|u|\).

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 6:the reverse chain rule with powers of \(\sin x\) and \(\cos x\)}}
Powers of \(\sin x\) times \(\cos x\), and powers of \(\cos x\) times \(\sin x\)
  • In \(\cos x\sin^nx\), take \(u=\sin x\). Then \(\frac{du}{dx}=\cos x\) is already a factor, and the primitive is \(\dfrac{u^{n+1}}{n+1}\).
  • In \(\sin x\cos^nx\), take \(u=\cos x\). Then \(\frac{du}{dx}=-\sin x\). Write \(\sin x=-(-\sin x)\), so that the minus sign goes outside the integral.
