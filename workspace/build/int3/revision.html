Inverse trig functions, double angles and standard integrals

A short review of Year 11 Extension 1 Chapter 17 (17A, 17B, 17C and 17E) and of the standard integrals from Parts 1 and 2. Lessons 12A and 12B differentiate and integrate the inverse trigonometric functions, so they need their domains, ranges and graphs. Lesson 12C uses the \(\cos2\theta\) formulae to integrate \(\sin^2x\) and \(\cos^2x\). Lessons 12D to 12F use every standard integral below.

Inverse relations and functions (17A)

Suppose that the graph of a relation has been drawn.

  • The graph of the inverse relation is obtained by reflecting the original graph in the diagonal line \(y=x\).
  • The inverse relation of a given relation is a function if and only if no horizontal line crosses the original graph more than once.
  • In particular, suppose that the original relation is a function. If the function is one-to-one, then the inverse relation is also a function. If the function is many-to-one, then the inverse relation is not a function.
  • The domain and range of the inverse relation are the range and domain respectively of the original function.
  • To find the equation and conditions of the inverse relation, write \(x\) for \(y\) and \(y\) for \(x\) every time each variable occurs. If the resulting equation can be solved for \(y\), then the inverse is a function.

Each of the six trigonometric functions fails the horizontal line test, so its inverse relation is not a function. To create an inverse function, we restrict the domain to a piece of the curve between two stationary points (or between two asymptotes for \(\tan x\)). Because acute angles should be included, the choices below are the obvious ones.

The definition of \(y=\sin^{-1}x\) (17B)
  • \(y=\sin^{-1}x\) is not the inverse relation of \(y=\sin x\). It is the inverse function of the restriction of \(y=\sin x\) to \(-\tfrac{\pi}{2}\le x\le\tfrac{\pi}{2}\).
  • \(y=\sin^{-1}x\) has domain \(-1\le x\le1\) and range \(-\tfrac{\pi}{2}\le y\le\tfrac{\pi}{2}\).
  • \(y=\sin^{-1}x\) is an increasing function.
  • \(y=\sin^{-1}x\) has tangent \(y=x\) at the origin, and is vertical at its endpoints.

The line \(y=x\) is the tangent to \(y=\sin x\) at the origin. Reflection in \(y=x\) does not move this line, so it is also the tangent to \(y=\sin^{-1}x\). The curve \(y=\sin x\) is horizontal at its stationary points, so \(y=\sin^{-1}x\) is vertical at its endpoints. In the graphs, the dashed diagonal is \(y=x\).

The definition of \(y=\cos^{-1}x\) (17B)
  • \(y=\cos^{-1}x\) is not the inverse relation of \(y=\cos x\). It is the inverse function of the restriction of \(y=\cos x\) to \(0\le x\le\pi\).
  • \(y=\cos^{-1}x\) has domain \(-1\le x\le1\) and range \(0\le y\le\pi\).
  • \(y=\cos^{-1}x\) is a decreasing function.
  • \(y=\cos^{-1}x\) has gradient \(-1\) at its \(y\)-intercept, and is vertical at its endpoints.

The tangent to \(y=\cos x\) at its \(x\)-intercept \(\big(\tfrac{\pi}{2},0\big)\) is the line \(x+y=\tfrac{\pi}{2}\), with gradient \(-1\). Reflection in \(y=x\) maps this line onto itself, so it is also the tangent to \(y=\cos^{-1}x\) at its \(y\)-intercept \(\big(0,\tfrac{\pi}{2}\big)\). It is dashed in both graphs.

{{svg:rev_cos}}\(y=\cos x,\ 0\le x\le\pi\)
{{svg:rev_acos}}\(y=\cos^{-1}x\)
The definition of \(y=\tan^{-1}x\) (17B)
  • \(y=\tan^{-1}x\) is not the inverse relation of \(y=\tan x\). It is the inverse function of the restriction of \(y=\tan x\) to \(-\tfrac{\pi}{2}\lt x\lt\tfrac{\pi}{2}\) (an open interval).
  • \(y=\tan^{-1}x\) has domain the real line and range \(-\tfrac{\pi}{2}\lt y\lt\tfrac{\pi}{2}\).
  • \(y=\tan^{-1}x\) is an increasing function.
  • \(y=\tan^{-1}x\) has gradient 1 at its \(y\)-intercept.
  • The lines \(y=\tfrac{\pi}{2}\) and \(y=-\tfrac{\pi}{2}\) are horizontal asymptotes.

The vertical asymptotes \(x=\pm\tfrac{\pi}{2}\) of \(y=\tan x\) reflect onto the horizontal asymptotes \(y=\pm\tfrac{\pi}{2}\) of \(y=\tan^{-1}x\). The line \(y=x\) (dashed diagonal) is the tangent to both curves at the origin.

Radians, notation and interpreting the restrictions (17B)

In this course, radian measure is used exclusively when dealing with the inverse trigonometric functions (except on the calculator, if you leave it in degrees mode). Calculations using degrees should be avoided, or at least not included in the formal working of problems.

  • Use radians when dealing with inverse trigonometric functions.
  • The functions \(\text{cosec}^{-1}x\), \(\sec^{-1}x\) and \(\cot^{-1}x\) are not defined in this course.
  • Alternative notation: \(\arcsin x=\sin^{-1}x\), \(\ \arccos x=\cos^{-1}x\), \(\ \arctan x=\tan^{-1}x\). Note that \(\sin^{-1}x\) is not the reciprocal \((\sin x)^{-1}=\dfrac{1}{\sin x}\).

The key to calculations is to include the restriction every time an expression involving the inverse trigonometric functions is rewritten using trigonometric functions:

  • \(y=\sin^{-1}x\) means \(x=\sin y\) where \(-\tfrac{\pi}{2}\le y\le\tfrac{\pi}{2}\).
  • \(y=\cos^{-1}x\) means \(x=\cos y\) where \(0\le y\le\pi\).
  • \(y=\tan^{-1}x\) means \(x=\tan y\) where \(-\tfrac{\pi}{2}\lt y\lt\tfrac{\pi}{2}\).
Worked exampleTextbook Example 4 (17B)
Find:
a\(\cos^{-1}\big(-\tfrac12\big)\)b\(\tan^{-1}(-1)\)
Solution

a Let \(\alpha=\cos^{-1}\big(-\tfrac12\big)\).
Then \(\cos\alpha=-\tfrac12\), where \(0\le\alpha\le\pi\).
Hence \(\alpha\) is in the second quadrant,
and the related angle is \(\tfrac{\pi}{3}\),
so \(\alpha=\pi-\tfrac{\pi}{3}=\dfrac{2\pi}{3}\).

b Let \(\alpha=\tan^{-1}(-1)\).
Then \(\tan\alpha=-1\), where \(-\tfrac{\pi}{2}\lt\alpha\lt\tfrac{\pi}{2}\).
Hence \(\alpha\) is in the fourth quadrant,
and the related angle is \(\tfrac{\pi}{4}\),
so \(\alpha=-\dfrac{\pi}{4}\).

Worked exampleTextbook Example 5 (17B)
Evaluate these expressions exactly, rationalising denominators where necessary.
a\(\tan\Big(\sin^{-1}\big(-\tfrac15\big)\Big)\)b\(\sin\big(\cos^{-1}\tfrac45\big)\)c\(\sin\big(\sin^{-1}\tfrac45\big)\)
Solution

a Let \(\alpha=\sin^{-1}\big(-\tfrac15\big)\).
Then \(\sin\alpha=-\tfrac15\), where \(-\tfrac{\pi}{2}\le\alpha\le\tfrac{\pi}{2}\).
Hence \(\alpha\) is in the fourth quadrant. The third side of the triangle is \(\sqrt{5^2-1^2}=\sqrt{24}\), and \(\tan\alpha\) is negative in the fourth quadrant, so \[\tan\alpha=\dfrac{-1}{\sqrt{24}}\times\dfrac{\sqrt6}{\sqrt6}=\dfrac{-\sqrt6}{12}=-\tfrac{1}{12}\sqrt6.\] (Here \(\sqrt{24}\times\sqrt6=\sqrt{144}=12\).)

b Let \(\alpha=\cos^{-1}\tfrac45\).
Then \(\cos\alpha=\tfrac45\), where \(0\le\alpha\le\pi\).
Hence \(\alpha\) is in the first quadrant. The third side is \(\sqrt{5^2-4^2}=3\). So \(\sin\alpha=\tfrac35\).

c Let \(\alpha=\sin^{-1}\tfrac45\).
Then \(\sin\alpha=\tfrac45\) (where the condition \(-\tfrac{\pi}{2}\le\alpha\le\tfrac{\pi}{2}\) is now irrelevant), so \(\sin\big(\sin^{-1}\tfrac45\big)=\tfrac45\).

Graphs involving shifting and reflecting (17C)

Reflecting in either axis, and shifting, can both be applied to the graphs of the three inverse trigonometric functions. As always, substitute key values to confirm the graph. In the case of \(\tan^{-1}x\), it is wise to take limits to confirm the horizontal asymptotes.

Worked exampleTextbook Example 6a (17C)
Sketch this graph, stating its domain and range, and any asymptotes: \(\ y-\tfrac{\pi}{2}=\tan^{-1}(x+1)\).
Solution

\(y-\tfrac{\pi}{2}=\tan^{-1}(x+1)\) is \(y=\tan^{-1}x\) shifted left 1 unit and up \(\tfrac{\pi}{2}\) units.

Confirm this by a small table of values:

\(x\)\(-2\)\(-1\)\(0\)
\(y\)\(\tfrac{\pi}{4}\)\(\tfrac{\pi}{2}\)\(\tfrac{3\pi}{4}\)

For example, at \(x=-2\): \(y=\tfrac{\pi}{2}+\tan^{-1}(-1)=\tfrac{\pi}{2}-\tfrac{\pi}{4}=\tfrac{\pi}{4}\).

The domain is all real numbers, and the range is \(0\lt y\lt\pi\).
As \(x\to\infty\), \(y\to\pi\), and as \(x\to-\infty\), \(y\to0\),
so the horizontal asymptotes are \(y=0\) and \(y=\pi\).

Symmetries and the identity \(\sin^{-1}x+\cos^{-1}x=\tfrac{\pi}{2}\) (17C)
  • \(y=\sin^{-1}x\) is odd, that is, \(\sin^{-1}(-x)=-\sin^{-1}x\).
  • \(y=\tan^{-1}x\) is odd, that is, \(\tan^{-1}(-x)=-\tan^{-1}x\).
  • \(y=\cos^{-1}x\) has odd symmetry about its \(y\)-intercept \(\big(0,\tfrac{\pi}{2}\big)\), that is, \[\cos^{-1}(-x)=\pi-\cos^{-1}x.\]
  • Complementary angles: \(\sin^{-1}x+\cos^{-1}x=\tfrac{\pi}{2}\), for \(-1\le x\le1\). The graphs are reflections of each other in the horizontal line \(y=\tfrac{\pi}{4}\) (right).
{{svg:rev_comp}}

Proof of \(\cos^{-1}(-x)=\pi-\cos^{-1}x\).

\(\begin{aligned}[t]&\text{Let }\alpha=\cos^{-1}(-x).\\&\text{Then }-x=\cos\alpha,\\&\quad\text{where }0\le\alpha\le\pi,\\&\text{so }\cos(\pi-\alpha)=x,\\&\quad\text{because }\cos(\pi-\alpha)=-\cos\alpha,\\&\pi-\alpha=\cos^{-1}x,\\&\quad\text{because }0\le\pi-\alpha\le\pi,\\&\alpha=\pi-\cos^{-1}x.\end{aligned}\)

Proof of \(\sin^{-1}x+\cos^{-1}x=\tfrac{\pi}{2}\).

\(\begin{aligned}[t]&\text{Let }\alpha=\cos^{-1}x.\\&\text{Then }x=\cos\alpha,\\&\quad\text{where }0\le\alpha\le\pi,\\&\sin\big(\tfrac{\pi}{2}-\alpha\big)=x,\\&\quad\text{because }\sin\big(\tfrac{\pi}{2}-\alpha\big)=\cos\alpha,\\&\sin^{-1}x=\tfrac{\pi}{2}-\alpha,\\&\quad\text{because }-\tfrac{\pi}{2}\le\tfrac{\pi}{2}-\alpha\le\tfrac{\pi}{2},\\&\sin^{-1}x+\alpha=\tfrac{\pi}{2}\\&\sin^{-1}x+\cos^{-1}x=\tfrac{\pi}{2}.\end{aligned}\)

Compositions (17C)
  • \(\sin\big(\sin^{-1}x\big)=x\) and \(\cos\big(\cos^{-1}x\big)=x\) for \(-1\le x\le1\), and \(\tan\big(\tan^{-1}x\big)=x\) for all real \(x\).
  • The other way round needs the restriction: \(\sin^{-1}(\sin x)=x\) only for \(-\tfrac{\pi}{2}\le x\le\tfrac{\pi}{2}\), \(\ \cos^{-1}(\cos x)=x\) only for \(0\le x\le\pi\), and \(\tan^{-1}(\tan x)=x\) only for \(-\tfrac{\pi}{2}\lt x\lt\tfrac{\pi}{2}\).

The graph of \(y=\cos^{-1}(\cos x)\). The domain of this function is the whole real number line, and the graph is far more complicated. A table of values is probably the surest approach, but here is an argument based on symmetries.

A For \(0\le x\le\pi\), \(\cos^{-1}(\cos x)=x\), and the graph follows \(y=x\).
B Because \(\cos x\) is an even function, the graph in the interval \(-\pi\le x\le0\) is the reflection of the graph in the interval \(0\le x\le\pi\).
C We now have the shape of the graph in the interval \(-\pi\le x\le\pi\). Because the graph has period \(2\pi\), the rest of the graph is just a repetition of this section.

The double-angle formulae (17E)

Replacing both \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) by \(\theta\) in the compound-angle formula \(\sin(\alpha+\beta)=\sin\alpha\cos\beta+\cos\alpha\sin\beta\) gives

\[\sin2\theta=\sin(\theta+\theta)=\sin\theta\cos\theta+\cos\theta\sin\theta=2\sin\theta\cos\theta.\]

The same process gives expansions of \(\cos2\theta\) and \(\tan2\theta\):

\[\begin{aligned}\sin2\theta&=2\sin\theta\cos\theta\\\cos2\theta&=\cos^2\theta-\sin^2\theta\\\tan2\theta&=\dfrac{2\tan\theta}{1-\tan^2\theta}\end{aligned}\]

The \(\cos2\theta\) formulae. Using \(\sin^2\theta=1-\cos^2\theta\), then \(\cos^2\theta=1-\sin^2\theta\):

\[\begin{aligned}\cos2\theta&=\cos^2\theta-\sin^2\theta\\&=2\cos^2\theta-1\\&=1-2\sin^2\theta\end{aligned}\]

These rest on the Pythagorean identities \(\sin^2\theta+\cos^2\theta=1\) and \(1+\tan^2\theta=\sec^2\theta\), which Lesson 12A also uses. Lesson 12C rearranges the last two forms of \(\cos2\theta\) to integrate \(\cos^2x\) and \(\sin^2x\).

Worked exampleTextbook Example 10 (17E)
Prove that \((\sin x+\cos x)^2=1+\sin2x\).
Solution

\(\begin{aligned}[t]\text{LHS}&=(\sin x+\cos x)^2\\&=\sin^2x+\cos^2x+2\sin x\cos x\\&=1+\sin2x\\&=\text{RHS}.\end{aligned}\)

Worked exampleTextbook Example 13 (17E)
Find \(\sin\big(2\cos^{-1}\tfrac45\big)\).
Solution

Let \(\alpha=\cos^{-1}\tfrac45\).
Then \(\cos\alpha=\tfrac45\), where \(0\le\alpha\le\pi\).
Hence \(\alpha\) is in the first quadrant, and from the triangle (as in Example 5b), \(\sin\alpha=\tfrac35\).

\(\begin{aligned}[t]\text{So}\quad\sin2\alpha&=2\sin\alpha\cos\alpha\\&=2\times\tfrac35\times\tfrac45\\&=\tfrac{24}{25}.\end{aligned}\)

Standard integrals from Parts 1 and 2

Primitives and the fundamental theorem (4J, 5B, 5E). \(F(x)\) is a primitive of \(f(x)\) if \(F'(x)=f(x)\). If \(f(x)\) is continuous in \([a,b]\), then \[\int_a^bf(x)\,dx=\Big[F(x)\Big]_a^b=F(b)-F(a),\quad\text{where }F(x)\text{ is any primitive of }f(x).\] Enclose the primitive in square brackets, substitute the upper and lower limits, and subtract.

Powers (4J, 5E)
\(\displaystyle\int x^n\,dx=\dfrac{x^{n+1}}{n+1}+C,\ \ n\ne-1\)\(\displaystyle\int(ax+b)^n\,dx=\dfrac{(ax+b)^{n+1}}{a(n+1)}+C,\ \ n\ne-1\)
Reverse chain rule (5I)
\(\displaystyle\int u^n\,\dfrac{du}{dx}\,dx=\dfrac{u^{n+1}}{n+1}+C\)OR \(\ \displaystyle\int\big(f(x)\big)^nf'(x)\,dx=\dfrac{\big(f(x)\big)^{n+1}}{n+1}+C\)
Exponential (6D)
\(\displaystyle\int e^x\,dx=e^x+C\)\(\displaystyle\int e^{ax+b}\,dx=\dfrac1a\,e^{ax+b}+C\)
Reciprocal (6I)
\(\displaystyle\int\dfrac1x\,dx=\log_e|x|+C\)\(\displaystyle\int\dfrac{1}{ax+b}\,dx=\dfrac1a\log_e|ax+b|+C\)
\(\displaystyle\int\dfrac{u'}{u}\,dx=\log_e|u|+C\)OR \(\ \displaystyle\int\dfrac{f'(x)}{f(x)}\,dx=\log_e|f(x)|+C\)
Trigonometric (7D)
\(\displaystyle\int\cos x\,dx=\sin x+C\)\(\displaystyle\int\cos(ax+b)\,dx=\dfrac1a\sin(ax+b)+C\)
\(\displaystyle\int\sin x\,dx=-\cos x+C\)\(\displaystyle\int\sin(ax+b)\,dx=-\dfrac1a\cos(ax+b)+C\)
\(\displaystyle\int\sec^2x\,dx=\tan x+C\)\(\displaystyle\int\sec^2(ax+b)\,dx=\dfrac1a\tan(ax+b)+C\)
  • In every form, \(C\) is a constant. Pay attention to the signs in the \(\sin\) and \(\cos\) integrals.
  • For the reverse chain rule, the vital step is to identify \(u\) or \(f(x)\) and its derivative. For \(\dfrac{f'(x)}{f(x)}\), the top must be the derivative of the bottom.
  • Radians are the right units for calculus with trigonometric functions.
  • No calculation involving a primitive may cross an asymptote.
Worked exampleTextbook Example 34 (6I)
Evaluate these definite integrals using the first two standard forms for reciprocal functions.
a\(\displaystyle\int_e^{e^2}\dfrac5x\,dx\)b\(\displaystyle\int_1^4\dfrac{1}{1-2x}\,dx\)c\(\displaystyle\int_1^5\dfrac{1}{x-2}\,dx\)
Solution

a \(\begin{aligned}[t]\int_e^{e^2}\dfrac5x\,dx&=5\Big[\log_e|x|\Big]_e^{e^2}\\&=5\big(\log_ee^2-\log_ee\big)\\&=5(2-1)\\&=5\end{aligned}\)

b \(\begin{aligned}[t]\int_1^4\dfrac{1}{1-2x}\,dx&=-\tfrac12\Big[\log_e|1-2x|\Big]_1^4\\&=-\tfrac12\big(\log_e\lvert-7\rvert-\log_e\lvert-1\rvert\big)\\&=-\tfrac12\big(\log_e7-0\big)\\&=-\tfrac12\log_e7\end{aligned}\)

Here \(a=-2\) and \(b=1\) in the second standard form.

c This definite integral is meaningless, because it crosses the asymptote at \(x=2\).

Worked exampleTextbook Example 17 (7D)
Evaluate these definite integrals.
a\(\displaystyle\int_0^{\pi}\cos x\,dx\)b\(\displaystyle\int_0^{\frac{\pi}{3}}\sec^2x\,dx\)c\(\displaystyle\int_{\frac{\pi}{4}}^{\frac{3\pi}{4}}\sec^2x\,dx\)
Solution

a \(\begin{aligned}[t]\int_0^{\pi}\cos x\,dx&=\Big[\sin x\Big]_0^{\pi}\\&=\sin\pi-\sin0\\&=0\end{aligned}\)

(Use the graph of \(y=\sin x\) to see that \(\sin\pi=0\) and \(\sin0=0\).)

b \(\begin{aligned}[t]\int_0^{\frac{\pi}{3}}\sec^2x\,dx&=\Big[\tan x\Big]_0^{\frac{\pi}{3}}\\&=\tan\tfrac{\pi}{3}-\tan0\\&=\sqrt3\end{aligned}\)

(Here \(\tan\tfrac{\pi}{3}=\sqrt3\) and \(\tan0=0\).)

c This integral is meaningless, because it crosses the asymptote at \(x=\tfrac{\pi}{2}\).
