Lesson 12A

Inverse trigonometric functions: differentiating

The inverse trigonometric functions were defined in Chapter 17 of the Year 11 book. We can now apply the normal processes of calculus to them. This section develops their derivatives and applies them to curve-sketching and maximisation. The derivatives come from writing \(y=\sin^{-1}x\) as \(x=\sin y\), and so on, and the restriction on \(y\) decides which square root to take. The chain rule then extends each standard form.
{{sec:teal:key:Concept 1:the derivatives of sin−1x, cos−1x and tan−1x}}
Differentiating \(\sin^{-1}x\) and \(\cos^{-1}x\)

To differentiate \(y=\sin^{-1}x\) and \(y=\cos^{-1}x\), we change to the inverse functions and use the known derivatives of the sine and cosine functions. We need to keep track of the restrictions to the domain so that the choice can be made later between positive and negative square roots.

Two facts from Year 11

The derivations below use \(\cos^2y+\sin^2y=1\), and \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=1\Big/\dfrac{dx}{dy}\).

A Let \(y=\sin^{-1}x\).
Then \(x=\sin y\), where \(-\tfrac{\pi}{2}\le y\le\tfrac{\pi}{2}\),
so \(\dfrac{dx}{dy}=\cos y\).

Because \(y\) is in the first or fourth quadrant, \(\cos y\) is positive, so

\[\begin{aligned}\cos y&=+\sqrt{1-\sin^2y}\\&=\sqrt{1-x^2}\quad(\text{since }\sin y=x).\end{aligned}\]

Thus \(\dfrac{dx}{dy}=\sqrt{1-x^2}\), so \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}\).

Hence \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\sin^{-1}x=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}\).

B Let \(y=\cos^{-1}x\).
Then \(x=\cos y\), where \(0\le y\le\pi\),
so \(\dfrac{dx}{dy}=-\sin y\).

Because \(y\) is in the first or second quadrant, \(\sin y\) is positive, so

\[\begin{aligned}\sin y&=+\sqrt{1-\cos^2y}\\&=\sqrt{1-x^2}\quad(\text{since }\cos y=x).\end{aligned}\]

Thus \(\dfrac{dx}{dy}=-\sqrt{1-x^2}\), so \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=-\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}\).

Hence \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\cos^{-1}x=-\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}\).

Differentiating \(\tan^{-1}x\)

The problem of which square root to choose does not arise when differentiating \(y=\tan^{-1}x\).

Let \(y=\tan^{-1}x\).
Then \(x=\tan y\), where \(-\tfrac{\pi}{2}<y<\tfrac{\pi}{2}\),
so

\[\begin{aligned}\frac{dx}{dy}&=\sec^2y\\&=1+\tan^2y.\end{aligned}\]

Hence \(\dfrac{dx}{dy}=1+x^2\) (since \(\tan y=x\)),

and \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=\dfrac{1}{1+x^2}\),

giving the standard form \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\tan^{-1}x=\dfrac{1}{1+x^2}\).

1 Standard forms for differentiation
\(\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}\sin^{-1}x=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}\)\(\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}\cos^{-1}x=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}\)\(\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}\tan^{-1}x=\frac{1}{1+x^2}\)
The endpoints of \(\sin^{-1}x\) and \(\cos^{-1}x\)

The formulae for \(\sin^{-1}x\) and \(\cos^{-1}x\) hold only for \(-1<x<1\). At \(x=\pm1\) the denominator \(\sqrt{1-x^2}\) is zero, so the derivative is undefined. The graphs are vertical at their endpoints, as the Revision section noted. The formula for \(\tan^{-1}x\) holds for every real \(x\).

Differentiate these functions.
a \(y=x\tan^{-1}x\)
b \(y=\sin^{-1}(ax+b)\)
Solution

a \(y=x\tan^{-1}x\)

\[\begin{aligned}y'&=vu'+uv'\\&=\tan^{-1}x\times1+x\times\frac{1}{1+x^2}\\&=\tan^{-1}x+\frac{x}{1+x^2}\end{aligned}\]

Let \(u=x\) and \(v=\tan^{-1}x\).
Then \(u'=1\) and \(v'=\dfrac{1}{1+x^2}\).

b \(y=\sin^{-1}(ax+b)\)

\[\begin{aligned}\frac{dy}{dx}&=\frac{dy}{du}\times\frac{du}{dx}\\&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-(ax+b)^2}}\times a\\&=\frac{a}{\sqrt{1-(ax+b)^2}}\end{aligned}\]

Let \(u=ax+b\),
then \(y=\sin^{-1}u\).
Hence \(\dfrac{du}{dx}=a\)
and \(\dfrac{dy}{du}=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1-u^2}}\).
In the last line, \(u\) is replaced by \(ax+b\).

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 2:linear extensions, and tangents with a given gradient}}
Linear extensions

The method used in part b above can be applied to all three inverse trigonometric functions, giving a further set of standard forms. With \(u=ax+b\) and \(\dfrac{du}{dx}=a\), the chain rule gives

\[\begin{aligned}\frac{d}{dx}\cos^{-1}(ax+b)&=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-u^2}}\times a=-\frac{a}{\sqrt{1-(ax+b)^2}},\\\frac{d}{dx}\tan^{-1}(ax+b)&=\frac{1}{1+u^2}\times a=\frac{a}{1+(ax+b)^2}.\end{aligned}\]
2 Further standard forms for differentiation
\[\begin{aligned}\frac{d}{dx}\sin^{-1}(ax+b)&=\frac{a}{\sqrt{1-(ax+b)^2}}\\\frac{d}{dx}\cos^{-1}(ax+b)&=-\frac{a}{\sqrt{1-(ax+b)^2}}\\\frac{d}{dx}\tan^{-1}(ax+b)&=\frac{a}{1+(ax+b)^2}\end{aligned}\]
Finding where the tangent has a given gradient

Put \(y'\) equal to the given gradient and solve for \(x\). The square root is cleared by squaring, so check each solution in the domain. Then find each \(y\)-value from the equation of the curve.

Solution

a Differentiating, \(y'=-\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1-(x-1)^2}}\).

Put \(y'=-2\). Then

\[\begin{aligned}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-(x-1)^2}}&=-2\\\sqrt{1-(x-1)^2}&=\tfrac12\\1-(x-1)^2&=\tfrac14\\ (x-1)^2&=\tfrac34\\x-1&=\tfrac12\sqrt3\ \text{ or }\ {-\tfrac12\sqrt3}\\x&=1+\tfrac12\sqrt3\ \text{ or }\ 1-\tfrac12\sqrt3.\end{aligned}\]

b The domain is \(-1\le x-1\le1\), that is, \(0\le x\le2\), and the range is \(0\le y\le\pi\). The endpoints are \((0,\pi)\) and \((2,0)\). The short lines at \(A\) and \(B\) are the tangents of gradient \(-2\).

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 3:functions whose derivatives are zero are constants}}
Proving identities by differentiation

Several identities involving inverse trigonometric functions can be obtained by showing that some derivative is zero, and hence that the original function is a constant. The following identity is the clearest example. It has been proven already in Section 17C of the Year 11 book using symmetry arguments.

  • Show that the derivative of the function is zero for every \(x\) in its domain.
  • Then the function is a constant \(C\) on that domain.
  • Find \(C\) by substituting any convenient value of \(x\) in the domain.

Curve sketching using calculus

The usual methods of curve sketching can now be extended to curves whose equations involve the inverse trigonometric functions. The next worked example applies calculus to sketching the curve \(y=\cos^{-1}\cos x\), which was sketched without calculus in Section 17C of the Year 11 book.

  • A simple table of test values gives some key points.
  • The derivative gives the shape of the curve joining these points.
  • Remember that \(\sqrt{A^2}=|A|\). So \(\sqrt{\sin^2x}=\sin x\) when \(\sin x\) is positive, and \(\sqrt{\sin^2x}=-\sin x\) when \(\sin x\) is negative.

Worked example 4Keep for homework
Use calculus to sketch \(y=\cos^{-1}\cos x\).
Solution

The function is periodic with the same period \(2\pi\) as \(\cos x\). A simple table of test values gives some key points. The shape of the curve joining these points can be obtained by calculus.

\(x\)\(0\)\(\tfrac{\pi}{2}\)\(\pi\)\(\tfrac{3\pi}{2}\)\(2\pi\)\(\tfrac{5\pi}{2}\)\(3\pi\)\(\ldots\)
\(y\)\(0\)\(\tfrac{\pi}{2}\)\(\pi\)\(\tfrac{\pi}{2}\)\(0\)\(\tfrac{\pi}{2}\)\(\pi\)\(\ldots\)

Differentiating using the chain rule,

\[\begin{aligned}\frac{dy}{dx}&=\frac{dy}{du}\times\frac{du}{dx}\\&=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\cos^2x}}\times(-\sin x)\\&=\frac{\sin x}{\sqrt{1-\cos^2x}}\\&=\frac{\sin x}{\sqrt{\sin^2x}}.\end{aligned}\]

Let \(u=\cos x\),
then \(y=\cos^{-1}u\).
Hence \(\dfrac{du}{dx}=-\sin x\)
and \(\dfrac{dy}{du}=-\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1-u^2}}\).

When \(\sin x\) is positive, \(\sqrt{\sin^2x}=\sin x\), so \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=1\).
When \(\sin x\) is negative, \(\sqrt{\sin^2x}=-\sin x\), so \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=-1\).

\[\text{Hence}\quad\frac{dy}{dx}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{for }x\text{ in quadrants 1 and 2,}\\-1,&\text{for }x\text{ in quadrants 3 and 4.}\end{cases}\]

This means that the graph consists of a series of intervals, each with gradient 1 or \(-1\).

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 5:chain-rule extensions to the standard forms}}
Chain-rule extensions

The usual chain-rule extensions to the standard forms can be used with the inverse trigonometric forms. They provide an alternative to the chain-rule setting out. For example, if \(y=\sin^{-1}u\), where \(u\) is a function of \(x\), then

\[\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{dy}{du}\times\frac{du}{dx}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-u^2}}\times u'=\frac{u'}{\sqrt{1-u^2}}.\]
3 Chain-rule extensions to the standard forms
\[\begin{aligned}\frac{d}{dx}\sin^{-1}u&=\frac{u'}{\sqrt{1-u^2}}\\\frac{d}{dx}\cos^{-1}u&=-\frac{u'}{\sqrt{1-u^2}}\\\frac{d}{dx}\tan^{-1}u&=\frac{u'}{1+u^2}\end{aligned}\]

OR

\[\begin{aligned}\frac{d}{dx}\sin^{-1}f(x)&=\frac{f'(x)}{\sqrt{1-\big(f(x)\big)^2}}\\\frac{d}{dx}\cos^{-1}f(x)&=-\frac{f'(x)}{\sqrt{1-\big(f(x)\big)^2}}\\\frac{d}{dx}\tan^{-1}f(x)&=\frac{f'(x)}{1+\big(f(x)\big)^2}\end{aligned}\]

Use the chain-rule extension formulae to differentiate \(\tan^{-1}e^{-5x}\).
Solution

Here \(u=e^{-5x}\) and \(u'=-5e^{-5x}\),

\[\begin{aligned}\text{so}\quad\frac{d}{dx}\big(\tan^{-1}e^{-5x}\big)&=\frac{u'}{1+u^2}\\&=\frac{-5e^{-5x}}{1+e^{-10x}}.\end{aligned}\]

OR here \(f(x)=e^{-5x}\) and \(f'(x)=-5e^{-5x}\),

\[\begin{aligned}\text{so}\quad\frac{d}{dx}\big(\tan^{-1}e^{-5x}\big)&=\frac{f'(x)}{1+\big(f(x)\big)^2}\\&=\frac{-5e^{-5x}}{1+e^{-10x}}.\end{aligned}\]

In the last line, \(u^2=\big(e^{-5x}\big)^2=e^{-10x}\).
