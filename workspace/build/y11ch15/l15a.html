Lesson 15A

Binomial expansions and Pascal's triangle

A binomial expansion is the expansion of a power of a binomial, such as \((1-x)^3=1-3x+3x^2-x^3\). This lesson expands \((1+x)^n\) for small \(n\), builds Pascal's triangle from its three basic properties, and uses it to expand, to approximate, to find terms in products, to solve problems about coefficients, and to expand a trinomial.
{{sec:teal:key:Concept 1:expansions of (1 + x)ⁿ and Pascal's triangle}}
Binomials and binomial expansions
  • A binomial is a polynomial with two terms, such as \(1-x\) or \(3x^4-\tfrac12x^2\).
  • A binomial expansion is the expansion of a power of a binomial, for example \((1-x)^3=1-3x+3x^2-x^3\).
  • This lesson deals only with expansions of \((1+x)^n\), and the techniques that come from them. They are all based on Pascal's triangle and its three most basic properties.
Some expansions of \((1+x)^n\)

Each expansion is found from the one before, in two rows, with like terms in columns. To begin, \((1+x)^0=1\) and \((1+x)^1=1+x\). Then:

\[(1+x)^2=1(1+x)+x(1+x)\] \[\begin{alignedat}{3}&=1&&{}+x&&\\&&&{}+x&&{}+x^2\\&=1&&{}+2x&&{}+x^2\end{alignedat}\]
\[(1+x)^3=1(1+x)^2+x(1+x)^2\] \[\begin{alignedat}{4}&=1&&{}+2x&&{}+x^2&&\\&&&{}+x&&{}+2x^2&&{}+x^3\\&=1&&{}+3x&&{}+3x^2&&{}+x^3\end{alignedat}\]
\[(1+x)^4=1(1+x)^3+x(1+x)^3\] \[\begin{alignedat}{5}&=1&&{}+3x&&{}+3x^2&&{}+\boxed{1}\,x^3&&\\&&&{}+x&&{}+3x^2&&{}+\boxed{3}\,x^3&&{}+x^4\\&=1&&{}+4x&&{}+6x^2&&{}+\boxed{4}\,x^3&&{}+x^4\end{alignedat}\]

In the last expansion, the boxed 4 is the sum of the boxed 1 and 3. Also \((1+x)^2\) has 3 terms, \((1+x)^3\) has 4 terms, and in general \((1+x)^n\) has \(n+1\) terms, from \(x^0=1\) to \(x^n\). Be careful: this is inclusive counting.

{{svg:pascal_tri}}
1 Three basic properties of Pascal's triangle
  • 1. Each row starts and ends with 1.
  • 2. Each row is reversible. That is, the rows are symmetric.
  • 3. [The addition property] Every number in the triangle, apart from the 1s, is the sum of the number directly above, and the number above and to the left.

For example, \(3+1=4\). This happens because the coefficient of \(x^3\) in \((1+x)^4\) is the sum of the coefficients of \(x^3\) and \(x^2\) in \((1+x)^3\): multiplying \((1+x)^3\) by \(1\) and by \(x\) puts both of them in the \(x^3\) column. These properties are used in this lesson and proven formally later. The triangle can be built row by row with them.

Using the triangle to expand
  • The row for \(n\) gives the coefficients of \((1+x)^n\). To expand \((1+\text{term})^n\), replace \(x\) by the term, in brackets: \((1+2a)^6=1+6(2a)+15(2a)^2+\cdots\).
  • A power applies to the number as well as to the letter: \((2a)^3=8a^3\), not \(2a^3\).
  • When the term is negative, as in \((1-x)^n\) or \(\big(1-\tfrac23x\big)^n\), the signs alternate.

History. Pascal did not discover the triangle, though he wrote an important treatise on it in 1653. It was known to the ancient Indian mathematician Pingala in the 2nd century BC, and later to mediaeval Persian and Chinese mathematicians. The first known occurrence in Europe is in a 1527 book on business calculations.

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 2:approximations}}
Approximating a power of a number close to 1
  • Write the number as \(1+\text{(a small number)}\), for example \(1.02^8=(1+0.02)^8\), and expand with the row of Pascal's triangle.
  • The powers of a small number shrink quickly, so only the first few terms matter. Write out terms until the next ones are too small to affect the decimal place you need, then add.
  • For a number just below 1, such as \(0.98=1-0.02\), the signs alternate.

By expanding the first few terms of \((1+0.02)^8\), find an approximation of \(1.02^8\) correct to five decimal places.
Solution

Row 8 is 1, 8, 28, 56, 70, 56, 28, 8, 1, so

\[\begin{aligned}(1+0.02)^8&=1+8\times0.02+28\times(0.02)^2+56\times(0.02)^3+70\times(0.02)^4+\cdots\\&=1+0.16+0.0112+0.000448+0.0000112+\cdots\\&\doteq1.17166.\end{aligned}\]

The remaining four terms are too small to affect the fifth decimal place.

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 3:terms in a product of expansions}}
Finding one term of a product
  • Expand each factor, but only as far as you need. Write powers of \(x\) in a denominator as negative powers: \(\dfrac5x=5x^{-1}\).
  • To find the term in \(x^k\) in the product, multiply each term of the first factor by the term of the second factor that makes the power of \(x\) equal to \(k\). Then add these products.
  • The constant term, or term independent of \(x\), is the term in \(x^0\).

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 4:particular terms and the ratio of coefficients}}
Writing down only the terms you need
  • A single term can be written straight from the row. In \((1+2kx)^6\) the term in \(x^3\) is \(20(2kx)^3=160k^3x^3\). You need not write out the whole expansion.
  • If two coefficients are in a given ratio, write the ratio as a fraction equal to the given ratio. Cancel the common power of \(k\) and solve for \(k\).

\[\begin{aligned}(1+2kx)^6&=\cdots+15(2kx)^2+20(2kx)^3+15(2kx)^4+\cdots\\&=\cdots+60k^2x^2+160k^3x^3+240k^4x^4+\cdots\end{aligned}\]

b Put

\[\dfrac{240k^4}{160k^3}=\dfrac23.\]

Then \(\tfrac32k=\tfrac23\), so \(k=\tfrac49\).

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 5:a harder example, expanding a trinomial}}
Treating part of a trinomial as one term
  • A trinomial such as \(1+x+x^2\) can be written as \(1+X\), where \(X=x+x^2=x(1+x)\).
  • Expand \((1+X)^n\) with a row of Pascal's triangle. Each power \(X^r=x^r(1+x)^r\) is then expanded with Pascal's triangle again.
  • Finally multiply out and collect like terms. Writing like terms in columns helps.
