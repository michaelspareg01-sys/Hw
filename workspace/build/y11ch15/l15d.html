Lesson 15D

Identities in Pascal's triangle

Each pattern in Pascal's triangle is described by an identity on the binomial coefficients \({}^nC_r\). This lesson proves such identities in several ways: by counting subsets, by substituting into the binomial expansion, by the addition property, by differentiating and then substituting, by the formula for \({}^nC_r\), and by equating coefficients.
{{sec:teal:key:Concept 1:patterns and three methods of proof}}
Identities in Pascal's triangle

There are a great number of patterns in Pascal's triangle. Some are quite straightforward to recognise and to prove, others are more complicated. They are very important in all applications of the binomial theorem, and in particular they will be important in Year 12 binomial probability.

Each pattern in Pascal's triangle is described by an identity on the binomial coefficients \({}^nC_r\). These identities sometimes have a rather forbidding appearance, so take the time to interpret each identity as some sort of pattern in Pascal's triangle. Use small values of \(n\) such as \(n=3\), \(n=4\) and \(n=5\) to work out what the identity is saying. Verify each identity in the triangle, either before or after the proof is completed.

Methods of proof

Methods of proof, as well as the identities themselves, are the subject of this lesson. Each proof begins with some form of the binomial expansion. There are three basic approaches, and many identities can be proven using two or even all three of them.

  • Combinatorics. Use the definition of \({}^nC_r\) as the number of \(r\)-member subsets of an \(n\)-member set.
  • Algebraic substitution. Substitute values into the binomial expansion \[(x+y)^n={}^nC_0\,x^ny^0+{}^nC_1\,x^{n-1}y^1+{}^nC_2\,x^{n-2}y^2+\cdots+{}^nC_n\,x^0y^n,\] or into the simpler expansion of \((1+x)^n\).
  • The addition property. It can be proven by both methods above, and in turn it can be used to prove some important identities.
The sum of a row

The row indexed by \(n\) has sum \(2^n\), that is,

\[{}^nC_0+{}^nC_1+{}^nC_2+\cdots+{}^nC_n=2^n.\]

For example, the row indexed by \(n=5\) adds to \(1+5+10+10+5+1=32=2^5\). The worked example below proves this identity by all three methods.

Worked exampleKeep for homework (the textbook's example)
Prove, in three different ways, that the row indexed by \(n\) has sum \(2^n\), that is, \[{}^nC_0+{}^nC_1+{}^nC_2+\cdots+{}^nC_n=2^n.\]
Solution

Proving the result using combinatorics. Let \(S\) be an \(n\)-member set. The left-hand side \({}^nC_0+{}^nC_1+{}^nC_2+\cdots+{}^nC_n\) is the sum of the numbers of 0-member subsets, 1-member subsets, 2-member subsets, …, \(n\)-member subsets of \(S\). That takes account of all the subsets of \(S\), so the LHS is just the number of subsets of \(S\).

We can count the subsets of \(S\) a different way. To form a subset, take each element in turn and choose whether it is in or out of the subset. The multiplication principle allows us to calculate the total number of ways of doing this:

1st element2nd element3rd element…\(n\)th element
222…2
\[\begin{aligned}\text{Hence number of subsets}&=2\times2\times2\times\cdots\times2\\&=2^n,\ \text{which proves the result.}\end{aligned}\]

Proving the result using algebraic substitution. Start with the binomial expansion

\[(x+y)^n={}^nC_0\,x^ny^0+{}^nC_1\,x^{n-1}y^1+{}^nC_2\,x^{n-2}y^2+\cdots+{}^nC_n\,x^0y^n.\]

Now substitute \(x=y=1\). Because \(1+1=2\),

\[2^n={}^nC_0+{}^nC_1+{}^nC_2+\cdots+{}^nC_n.\]

Proving the result using the addition property. The entries in the row indexed by \(n\) are the sum of two entries in the row above, and every entry in the row above is used in exactly two sums to calculate entries in the row below. (We can regard the 1s on the ends as being \(0+1\) and \(1+0\).) Thus the sum of the row indexed by \(n\) is double the sum of the row above.

The first row is a single 1, which is \(2^0\), and the row is indexed by \(n=0\). Thus, continuing down the rows one by one, the result is clear.

A further approach: differentiate, then substitute
  • Differentiate both sides of the binomial expansion of \((1+x)^n\), and then substitute a value of \(x\), such as \(x=1\) or \(x=-1\). This is done in Questions 1, 2 and 3 of the homework, and in Question 13 of the Chapter review, which uses double differentiation. These results will be needed in Year 12 binomial probability.
  • On the left, \(\dfrac{d}{dx}(1+x)^n=n(1+x)^{n-1}\). On the right, the constant term \({}^nC_0\) has derivative 0, and each term \({}^nC_r\,x^r\) has derivative \(r\times{}^nC_r\,x^{r-1}\).
  • After substituting, each identity can be checked on a row of Pascal's triangle.

Worked exampleKeep for homework
Differentiate both sides of the binomial expansion \((1+x)^n={}^nC_0+{}^nC_1\,x+{}^nC_2\,x^2+\cdots+{}^nC_n\,x^n\), and then substitute \(x=2\), to prove that \[1\times{}^nC_1+2\times2\times{}^nC_2+3\times2^2\times{}^nC_3+\cdots+n\times2^{n-1}\times{}^nC_n=n\times3^{n-1}.\] Then check the result on the row indexed by \(n=3\).
Solution

Differentiating both sides of the expansion,

\[n(1+x)^{n-1}={}^nC_1+2\times{}^nC_2\,x+3\times{}^nC_3\,x^2+\cdots+n\times{}^nC_n\,x^{n-1}.\]

Now substitute \(x=2\). Because \(1+2=3\),

\[n\times3^{n-1}=1\times{}^nC_1+2\times2\times{}^nC_2+3\times2^2\times{}^nC_3+\cdots+n\times2^{n-1}\times{}^nC_n.\]

Check. The row indexed by \(n=3\) is \(1\ \,3\ \,3\ \,1\), so

\[\begin{aligned}\text{LHS}&=1\times3+2\times2\times3+3\times4\times1=27,\\\text{RHS}&=3\times3^2=27.\end{aligned}\]
{{sec:teal:key:Concept 3:the formula for ⁿCᵣ, and equating coefficients}}
Two more approaches
  • Use the formula. Use the formula \({}^nC_r=\dfrac{n!}{r!\,(n-r)!}\) to express the LHS and the RHS in terms of factorials, and then simplify. To find a common denominator, write a factorial in terms of a smaller one, for example \(r!=r\times(r-1)!\) and \((n-r+1)!=(n-r+1)\times(n-r)!\). This method is used in Questions 7, 10 and 14 of the homework.
  • Equate coefficients. Expand both sides of an algebraic identity, such as \((1+x)^n(1+x)^n=(1+x)^{2n}\), and equate the coefficients of one power of \(x\). This method is more elaborate. A couple of examples are included in the Enrichment questions, and trial papers use it too.

Worked exampleKeep for homework
a Use the formula for \({}^nC_r\) to prove that \(\dfrac{{}^nC_{r+1}}{{}^nC_r}=\dfrac{n-r}{r+1}\), for \(0\le r\le n-1\).
b By equating coefficients of \(x^2\) on both sides of \((1+x)^m(1+x)^n=(1+x)^{m+n}\), prove that\({}^mC_2+{}^mC_1\times{}^nC_1+{}^nC_2={}^{m+n}C_2\), for \(m\ge2\) and \(n\ge2\). Check the result in part b for \(m=3\) and \(n=4\).
Solution

a Using the formula, and then \((r+1)!=(r+1)\times r!\) and \((n-r)!=(n-r)\times(n-r-1)!\),

\[\begin{aligned}\dfrac{{}^nC_{r+1}}{{}^nC_r}&=\dfrac{n!}{(r+1)!\,(n-r-1)!}\times\dfrac{r!\,(n-r)!}{n!}\\&=\dfrac{r!}{(r+1)\times r!}\times\dfrac{(n-r)\times(n-r-1)!}{(n-r-1)!}\\&=\dfrac{n-r}{r+1}.\end{aligned}\]

b On the left, the terms in \(x^2\) come from (term in \(x^k\)) \(\times\) (term in \(x^{2-k}\)), for \(k=0,1,2\):

\[\begin{aligned}(1+x)^m(1+x)^n&=\big({}^mC_0+{}^mC_1\,x+{}^mC_2\,x^2+\cdots\big)\big({}^nC_0+{}^nC_1\,x+{}^nC_2\,x^2+\cdots\big)\\&=\cdots+\big({}^mC_0\times{}^nC_2+{}^mC_1\times{}^nC_1+{}^mC_2\times{}^nC_0\big)x^2+\cdots\end{aligned}\]

On the right, the coefficient of \(x^2\) in \((1+x)^{m+n}\) is \({}^{m+n}C_2\). Equating the coefficients of \(x^2\), and using \({}^mC_0={}^nC_0=1\),

\[{}^mC_2+{}^mC_1\times{}^nC_1+{}^nC_2={}^{m+n}C_2.\]

Check. When \(m=3\) and \(n=4\), the LHS is \(3+3\times4+6=21\), and the RHS is \({}^7C_2=21\).
