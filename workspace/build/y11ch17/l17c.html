Lesson 17C

Graphs involving inverse trigonometric functions

This section deals mostly with graphs that can be obtained using transformations of the graphs of the three inverse trigonometric functions. It also proves the symmetries of the three functions and the identity \(\sin^{-1}x+\cos^{-1}x=\dfrac\pi2\), and sketches compositions such as \(\sin\big(\sin^{-1}x\big)\) and \(\cos^{-1}(\cos x)\).
{{sec:teal:key:Concept 1:graphs involving shifting and reflecting}}
Graphs involving shifting and reflecting

Reflecting in either axes, and shifting, can both be applied, but as always substitution of key values should be used to confirm the graph. In the case of \(\tan^{-1}x\), it is wise to take limits so as to confirm the horizontal asymptotes.

Solution

a \(y-\dfrac\pi2=\tan^{-1}(x+1)\) is \(y=\tan^{-1}x\) shifted left 1 unit and up \(\dfrac\pi2\) units. Confirm this by a small table of values:

\(x\)\(-2\)\(-1\)\(0\)
\(y\)\(\dfrac\pi4\)\(\dfrac\pi2\)\(\dfrac{3\pi}{4}\)

The domain is all real numbers, and the range is \(0<y<\pi\).

As \(x\to\infty\), \(y\to\pi\), and as \(x\to-\infty\), \(y\to0\), so the horizontal asymptotes are \(y=0\) and \(y=\pi\).

\(x\)\(-1\)\(0\)\(1\)

The domain is \(-1\le x\le1\), and the range is \(-\pi\le y\le0\).

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 2:symmetries and complementary angles}}
Symmetries of the inverse trigonometric functions

The functions \(y=\sin^{-1}x\) and \(y=\tan^{-1}x\) are both odd. The function \(y=\cos^{-1}x\) also has odd symmetry, not about the origin, but about its \(y\)-intercept \(\Big(0,\dfrac\pi2\Big)\).

8 Symmetries of the inverse trigonometric functions
  • \(y=\sin^{-1}x\) is odd, that is, \(\sin^{-1}(-x)=-\sin^{-1}x\).
  • \(y=\tan^{-1}x\) is odd, that is, \(\tan^{-1}(-x)=-\tan^{-1}x\).
  • \(y=\cos^{-1}x\) has odd symmetry about its \(y\)-intercept \(\Big(0,\dfrac\pi2\Big)\), that is, \(\cos^{-1}(-x)=\pi-\cos^{-1}x\).
Proof of the third symmetry

These were proven in Questions 8, 9 and 10 of Exercise 17B. The last identity is a little more difficult, and its proof is repeated here.

\[\begin{aligned}\text{Let}\quad\alpha&=\cos^{-1}(-x).\\\text{Then}\quad-x&=\cos\alpha,\ \text{ where }0\le\alpha\le\pi,\\\text{so}\quad\cos(\pi-\alpha)&=x,\ \text{ because }\cos(\pi-\alpha)=-\cos\alpha,\\\pi-\alpha&=\cos^{-1}x,\ \text{ because }0\le\pi-\alpha\le\pi,\\\alpha&=\pi-\cos^{-1}x,\ \text{ as required.}\end{aligned}\]
The identity \(\sin^{-1}x+\cos^{-1}x=\dfrac\pi2\)

The graphs of \(y=\sin^{-1}x\) and \(y=\cos^{-1}x\) are reflections of each other in the horizontal line \(y=\dfrac\pi4\). Hence adding the graphs pointwise, it should be clear that \(\sin^{-1}x+\cos^{-1}x=\dfrac\pi2\) (Box 9 below).

This was proven algebraically in Question 11 of the previous Exercise 17B, where it was clear that the identity is really only another form of the complementary angle identity \(\cos\Big(\dfrac\pi2-\theta\Big)=\sin\theta\). The result is important, and the algebraic proof is repeated here.

{{svg:c_comp}}
\[\begin{aligned}\text{Let}\quad\alpha&=\cos^{-1}x.\\\text{Then}\quad x&=\cos\alpha,\ \text{ where }0\le\alpha\le\pi,\\\sin\Big(\frac\pi2-\alpha\Big)&=x,\ \text{ because }\sin\Big(\frac\pi2-\alpha\Big)=\cos\alpha,\\\sin^{-1}x&=\frac\pi2-\alpha,\ \text{ because }-\frac\pi2\le\frac\pi2-\alpha\le\frac\pi2,\\\sin^{-1}x+\alpha&=\frac\pi2\\\sin^{-1}x+\cos^{-1}x&=\frac\pi2.\end{aligned}\]

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 3:compositions of a function with its inverse}}
The graphs of \(\sin(\sin^{-1}x)\), \(\cos(\cos^{-1}x)\) and \(\tan(\tan^{-1}x)\)

The composite function defined by \(y=\sin\big(\sin^{-1}x\big)\) has the same domain as \(\sin^{-1}x\), that is, \(-1\le x\le1\). Because it is the function \(y=\sin^{-1}x\) followed by the function \(y=\sin x\), the composite is therefore the identity function \(y=x\) restricted to \(-1\le x\le1\).

\(y=\tan\big(\tan^{-1}x\big)\)

The same remarks apply to \(y=\cos\big(\cos^{-1}x\big)\) and \(y=\tan\big(\tan^{-1}x\big)\), except that the domain of \(y=\tan\big(\tan^{-1}x\big)\) is the whole real number line.

The graph of \(\cos^{-1}(\cos x)\)

The domain of this function is the whole real number line, and the graph is far more complicated. Constructing a simple table of values is probably the surest approach, but under the graph is an argument based on symmetries.

{{svg:c_cic}}

A For \(0\le x\le\pi\), \(\cos^{-1}(\cos x)=x\), and the graph follows \(y=x\).

B Because \(\cos x\) is an even function, the graph in the interval \(-\pi\le x\le0\) is the reflection of the graph in the interval \(0\le x\le\pi\).

C We now have the shape of the graph in the interval \(-\pi\le x\le\pi\). Because the graph has period \(2\pi\), the rest of the graph is just a repetition of this section.
