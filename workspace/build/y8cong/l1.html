Lesson 1

Congruent figures

Two figures are congruent when they have exactly the same size and shape. This lesson sets up the language of congruence: which figures are congruent, which vertices, sides and angles of two congruent figures match, and how to write a congruence statement with the symbol \(\equiv\). The teaching follows Year 8 section 8D, Congruent figures.
{{sec:teal:key:Concept 1:congruent figures}}
Congruent figures (Year 8, 8D)

If two or more objects are identical in size and shape, we say they are congruent.

Each brochure being printed is identical, so the images on them are congruent.

{{svg:l1_start}}
Key ideas (8D)
  • A figure is a shape, diagram or illustration.
  • Congruent figures have the same size and shape. They also have the same area. They are identical in every way.
{{svg:l1_four}}
  • The image of a figure that is reflected, translated or rotated is congruent to the original figure.
  • Two circles are congruent if they have equal radii.
Testing two figures

Two figures are congruent when one of them can be moved to sit exactly on top of the other. Reflections, translations and rotations move a figure without changing any length or any angle, which is why the image is always congruent. A figure that has been stretched, squashed or enlarged is not congruent to the original, even if it looks alike: some of its lengths have changed.

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 2:corresponding parts and congruence statements}}
Key ideas (8D), continued
  • Corresponding (matching) parts of a figure have the same geometric properties.
    • Vertex \(C\) corresponds to vertex \(E\).
    • Side \(AB\) corresponds to side \(FD\).
    • \(\angle B\) corresponds to \(\angle D\).
{{svg:l1_keytri}}
  • A congruence statement can be written using the symbol \(\equiv\). For example, \(\triangle ABC\equiv\triangle FDE\).
    • The symbol \(\cong\) can also be used for congruence.
    • In a congruence statement vertices are named in matching order. For example, \(\triangle ABC\equiv\triangle FDE\) not \(\triangle ABC\equiv\triangle DEF\) because \(B\) matches \(D\).
Reading the marks in the diagram

The marks show which angles match. The right angles are at \(A\) and \(F\), the dots are at \(B\) and \(D\), and the open circles are at \(C\) and \(E\). So \(A\) matches \(F\), \(B\) matches \(D\) and \(C\) matches \(E\). A side matches the side that joins the matching vertices: \(AB\) joins \(A\) and \(B\), so it matches \(FD\). Writing the vertices in the same order gives \(\triangle ABC\equiv\triangle FDE\). When a diagram has no marks, compare lengths and angles instead: the longest side matches the longest side, the largest angle matches the largest angle, and so on.

Solution
Explanation
a vertex \(G\)
\(C\) sits opposite \(A\) and \(\angle A\) is the smallest angle. \(G\) sits opposite \(E\) and \(\angle E\) is also the smallest angle.
b side \(EH\)
Sides \(AB\) and \(EH\) are both the longest sides of their respective shapes. \(A\) corresponds to \(E\) and \(B\) corresponds to \(H\).
c \(\angle G\)
\(\angle C\) and \(\angle G\) are both the largest angle in their corresponding quadrilateral.
Steps the book skips: Example 7

Turn \(EFGH\) through half a turn (\(180^\circ\)) and it sits exactly on \(ABCD\), with \(E\) on \(A\), \(H\) on \(B\), \(G\) on \(C\) and \(F\) on \(D\). Every answer can be read from this matching. The book’s explanation finds the same matching from the sizes of the angles and the lengths of the sides.
