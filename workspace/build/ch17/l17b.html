Lesson 17B

Binomial distributions

Now that we have dealt with individual binomial probabilities, we can look at the whole distribution. The binomial distribution is a discrete distribution, and Chapter 13 of the Year 11 book was devoted to discrete distributions. As we have done before, we will draw its graph and work with its mean and variance. For \(X\sim B(n,p)\), the mean is \(\mu=np\) and the variance is \(\sigma^2=npq\). The distribution is symmetric when \(p=\tfrac12\), and skewed otherwise.
{{sec:teal:key:Concept 1:notation, and the Bernoulli distribution}}
Notation for binomial distributions

Some notation is convenient. The binomial distribution consisting of \(n\) independent trials, each with probability \(p\) of success, is denoted by \(B(n,p)\) or \(\text{Bin}(n,p)\). In particular, a Bernoulli experiment with probability \(p\) is a special case of a binomial experiment, so is denoted by \(B(1,p)\).

To say that \(X\) is a binomial random variable for the distribution \(B(n,p)\), we can use the symbolic notation

\[X\sim B(n,p)\qquad\text{OR}\qquad X\sim\text{Bin}(n,p).\]

In particular, \(X\sim B(1,p)\) means that \(X\) is a Bernoulli random variable (there is only one stage) with probability \(p\) of success.

2 Binomial and Bernoulli distributions
  • The binomial distribution with \(n\) independent Bernoulli stages, each with probability \(p\) of success, is denoted by \(B(n,p)\) or \(\text{Bin}(n,p)\).
  • The Bernoulli distribution with probability \(p\) is therefore \(B(1,p)\).
  • \(X\sim B(n,p)\) means that \(X\) is a random variable for the distribution \(B(n,p)\).
The mean and variance of a Bernoulli distribution

A Bernoulli trial with probabilities \(p\) of ‘success’ and \(q=1-p\) of ‘failure’ has mean \(p\) and variance \(pq\),

\[\mu=p\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sigma^2=pq,\]

so that its standard deviation is \(\sigma=\sqrt{pq}\). These formulae are easy to prove because there are only two outcomes (see the worked example below).

Worked exampleFrom the text: keep for homework
Prove that a Bernoulli trial with probabilities \(p\) of ‘success’ and \(q=1-p\) of ‘failure’ has mean \(\mu=p\) and variance \(\sigma^2=pq\).
Solution

We use the formula \(\mu=\sum xP(X=x)\), where the sum is taken over the distribution, meaning for \(x=0\) and \(x=1\),

\[\begin{aligned}\text{so}\qquad\mu&=0\times P(X=0)+1\times P(X=1)\\&=0\times q+1\times p\\&=p.\end{aligned}\]

Similarly, summing over the distribution,

\[\begin{aligned}\sigma^2&=\sum x^2P(X=x)-\mu^2\\&=(0^2\times q+1^2\times p)-p^2\\&=p-p^2\\&=p(1-p)\\&=pq\quad(\text{either form is an appropriate answer}).\end{aligned}\]
{{sec:teal:key:Concept 2:the mean and variance of a binomial distribution}}
The mean and variance of a binomial distribution

The mean and variance of the binomial random variable \(X\sim B(n,p)\) with \(n\) trials and probabilities \(p\) and \(q\) of ‘success’ and ‘failure’ are

\[\mu=np\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sigma^2=npq,\]

so that the standard deviation is \(\sigma=\sqrt{npq}\).

These two results \(\mu=np\) and \(\sigma^2=npq\) seem to follow immediately from the results \(\mu=p\) and \(\sigma^2=pq\) for Bernoulli trials just by multiplying by \(n\). And they do: it is true in general that if we have a number of independent random variables, then the mean of the sum is the sum of the means, and the variance of the sum is the sum of the variances.

Unfortunately, however, those two very general theorems are too difficult to prove, and instead we must prove the result for binomial distributions directly. The proofs below are not conceptually difficult, but they do require a sequence of computations. We begin with a lemma about binomial coefficients.

A lemma about binomial coefficients

Lemma: Let \(n\) and \(x\) be whole numbers with \(x\le n\).

a If \(x\ge1\), then \(\quad x\times{}^nC_x=n\times{}^{n-1}C_{x-1}\).
b If \(x\ge2\), then \(\quad x(x-1)\times{}^nC_x=n(n-1)\times{}^{n-2}C_{x-2}\).

Proof:

a

\[\begin{aligned}x\times{}^nC_x&=x\times\frac{n!}{x!\times(n-x)!}\\&=\frac{x\times n\times(n-1)!}{x\times(x-1)!\times(n-x)!}\\&=n\times\frac{(n-1)!}{(x-1)!\times(n-x)!}\\&=\text{RHS}\end{aligned}\]

b

\[\begin{aligned}&x(x-1)\times{}^nC_x\\&\quad=x(x-1)\times\frac{n!}{x!\times(n-x)!}\\&\quad=\frac{x(x-1)\times n(n-1)\times(n-2)!}{x(x-1)\times(x-2)!\times(n-x)!}\\&\quad=n(n-1)\times\frac{(n-2)!}{(x-2)!\times(n-x)!}\\&\quad=\text{RHS}\end{aligned}\]
Proving that the mean is \(np\)

This proof requires binomial expansions, together with part a of the lemma above.

\[\begin{aligned}\text{E}(X)&=\sum xP(X=x),\ \text{summed over the distribution,}\\&=0\times{}^nC_0\times p^0q^n+1\times{}^nC_1\times p^1q^{n-1}+2\times{}^nC_2\times p^2q^{n-2}+\cdots\\&\qquad+n\times{}^nC_n\times p^nq^0.\end{aligned}\]

The first term is 0, then we apply part a of the lemma to the remaining terms,

\[\begin{aligned}\text{E}(X)&=n\times{}^{n-1}C_0\,p^1q^{n-1}+n\times{}^{n-1}C_1\,p^2q^{n-2}+\cdots+n\times{}^{n-1}C_{n-1}\,p^nq^0\\&=np\big({}^{n-1}C_0\,p^0q^{n-1}+{}^{n-1}C_1\,p^1q^{n-2}+\cdots+{}^{n-1}C_{n-1}\,p^{n-1}q^0\big).\end{aligned}\]

The bit in brackets is the binomial expansion of \((p+q)^{n-1}\), where \(p+q=1\), so

\[\begin{aligned}\text{E}(X)&=np(p+q)^{n-1}\\&=np\times1\\&=np.\end{aligned}\]
Proving that the variance is \(npq\)

This proof again requires binomial expansions. First, however, we need to develop yet another formula for the variance.

\[\begin{aligned}\text{Var}(X)&=\text{E}(X^2)-\big(\text{E}(X)\big)^2\\&=\text{E}\big(X(X-1)\big)+\text{E}(X)-\big(\text{E}(X)\big)^2\\&=\text{E}\big(X(X-1)\big)+np-n^2p^2,\quad\text{because }\text{E}(X)=np.\end{aligned}\]

We can find \(\text{E}\big(X(X-1)\big)\) using binomial expansions and part b of the lemma,

\[\begin{aligned}\text{E}\big(X(X-1)\big)&=\sum x(x-1)P(X=x),\ \text{summed over the distribution,}\\&=0\times(-1)\times{}^nC_0\times p^0q^n+1\times0\times{}^nC_1\times p^1q^{n-1}+2\times1\times{}^nC_2\times p^2q^{n-2}\\&\qquad+3\times2\times{}^nC_3\times p^3q^{n-3}+\cdots+n(n-1)\times{}^nC_n\times p^nq^0.\end{aligned}\]

The first two terms are 0, then applying part b of the lemma,

\[\begin{aligned}&=n(n-1)\times{}^{n-2}C_0\times p^2q^{n-2}+n(n-1)\times{}^{n-2}C_1\times p^3q^{n-3}\\&\qquad+n(n-1)\times{}^{n-2}C_2\times p^4q^{n-4}+\cdots+n(n-1)\times{}^{n-2}C_{n-2}\times p^nq^0\\&=n(n-1)p^2\big({}^{n-2}C_0\,p^0q^{n-2}+{}^{n-2}C_1\,p^1q^{n-3}+\cdots+{}^{n-2}C_{n-2}\,p^{n-2}q^0\big).\end{aligned}\]

The bit in brackets is the binomial expansion of \((p+q)^{n-2}\), where \(p+q=1\), so

\[\begin{aligned}&=n(n-1)p^2(p+q)^{n-2}\\&=n^2p^2-np^2.\end{aligned}\]
\[\begin{aligned}\text{Hence }\text{Var}(X)&=\text{E}\big(X(X-1)\big)+np-n^2p^2\\&=n^2p^2-np^2+np-n^2p^2\\&=np(1-p)\\&=npq.\end{aligned}\]
3 Mean and variance of a binomial distribution
  • For a binomial random variable \(X\sim B(n,p)\), where \(q=1-p\), \[\mu=np\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sigma^2=npq\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sigma=\sqrt{npq}.\]
  • In particular, for a Bernoulli random variable \(X\sim B(1,p)\), \[\mu=p\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sigma^2=pq\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sigma=\sqrt{pq}.\]
Notation

The symbols \(\text{E}(X)\) and \(\mu\) are interchangeable: \(\mu\) is more concise, but the notation \(\text{E}(X)\) indicates that when we run the experiment, there is a sense in which we are ‘expecting’ to get \(\text{E}(X)\). Similarly, \(\sigma^2\) and \(\text{Var}(X)\) are interchangeable.

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 3:conditions on the mean and standard deviation}}
Finding \(n\) or \(p\)

The formulae in Box 3 can also be used to find \(n\) or \(p\) when a condition is placed on the mean or the standard deviation. A condition on \(\sigma\) is usually easier to use after squaring, because \(\sigma^2=npq\) has no square root. Both sides are positive, so squaring keeps the inequality sign. When \(n\) is fixed and the condition is on \(p\), the result is a quadratic inequality in \(p\) (see Worked example 7).

Finding \(n\) and \(p\) from \(\mu\) and \(\sigma^2\)

If the mean \(\mu\) and the variance \(\sigma^2\) are both given, divide \(\sigma^2=npq\) by \(\mu=np\):

\[q=\frac{\sigma^2}{\mu},\qquad\text{then}\qquad p=1-q\qquad\text{and}\qquad n=\frac{\mu}{p}.\]

Check that \(n\) is a whole number.

Worked example 7Keep for homework
A binomial random variable has parameters \(n=100\) and \(p=\tfrac15\).
a Find the mean and standard deviation.
b Keeping \(p=\tfrac15\), what would \(n\) need to be increased to so that the standard deviation is less than 1% of the number \(n\) of trials?
c Keeping \(n=100\), what must \(p\) be decreased to so that the standard deviation is less than 1? Can \(p\) be increased to give a standard deviation less than 1?
Solution

a

\[\begin{aligned}\mu&=np\\&=100\times\tfrac15\\&=20,\end{aligned}\]
\[\begin{aligned}\sigma^2&=npq\quad(\text{where }q=1-p=\tfrac45)\\&=100\times\tfrac15\times\tfrac45\\&=16,\end{aligned}\]
\[\begin{aligned}\sigma&=\sqrt{16}\\&=4.\end{aligned}\]

b Put \(\sigma<\dfrac{n}{100}\).

\[\begin{aligned}\text{Squaring, }\quad npq&<\frac{n^2}{10\,000}\\n^2&>10\,000\times\tfrac{4}{25}\times n,\quad\text{because }pq=\tfrac15\times\tfrac45=\tfrac{4}{25}.\\\text{Hence}\quad n&>1600,\quad\text{because }n\text{ is positive.}\end{aligned}\]

c Put \(\sigma<1\).

\[\begin{aligned}\text{Squaring, }\quad npq&<1\\100p(1-p)&<1\\100p^2-100p+1&>0.\end{aligned}\]

The quadratic \(100p^2-100p+1=0\), with \(a=100\), \(b=-100\) and \(c=1\), has axis of symmetry \(p=\tfrac12\) and discriminant \(\Delta=9600=40^2\times6\),

\[\begin{aligned}\text{so its roots are}\quad p&=\frac{100-40\sqrt6}{200}&\text{and}\quad p&=\frac{100+40\sqrt6}{200}\\&=\tfrac12-\tfrac15\sqrt6&&=\tfrac12+\tfrac15\sqrt6\\&\doteq0.0101&&\doteq0.9899,\end{aligned}\]

so \(p\) should be decreased to less than \(\tfrac12-\tfrac15\sqrt6\) (roughly, less than 0.01), or increased to more than \(\tfrac12+\tfrac15\sqrt6\) (roughly, greater than 0.99).

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 4:skewed and non-skewed binomial distributions}}
Symmetric when \(p=q=\tfrac12\)

If \(p=q=\tfrac12\), such as when we toss a coin a number of times, the distribution is symmetric. For example, in Section 15E (Year 11) we graphed the distribution when a coin is tossed 20 times and the number of heads is recorded.

{{svg:c17b_coin20}}

The mean is \(\mu=np=10\), and the distribution is symmetric about \(x=10\), with standard deviation \(\sigma=\sqrt{npq}=\sqrt5\doteq2.236\). This symmetry is easily seen from the symmetry of the binomial coefficient. For example, \({}^{20}C_7={}^{20}C_{13}\), so

\[P(X=7)={}^{20}C_7\times\big(\tfrac12\big)^7\times\big(\tfrac12\big)^{13}={}^{20}C_{13}\times\big(\tfrac12\big)^{13}\times\big(\tfrac12\big)^7=P(X=13).\]
Skewed to the right, and skewed to the left

When, however, we throw a die 20 times, recording ‘success’ when the result is 6, the distribution is decidedly skewed to the right (positive skewness). Here \(p=\tfrac16\) and \(q=\tfrac56\), with mean \(\mu=3\tfrac13\doteq3.333\) and standard deviation \(\sigma=\tfrac53\doteq1.667\).

{{svg:c17b_die20}}

Remember that skewed to the right means that there is a longer tail on the right-hand side, and the peak is on the left-hand side.

On the other hand, when we throw the die 20 times but record ‘success’ as ‘not getting 6’, then the distribution is skewed to the left (negative skewness). Here \(p=\tfrac56\) and \(q=\tfrac16\), with mean \(\mu=16\tfrac23\doteq16.667\) and the same standard deviation \(\sigma=\tfrac53\doteq1.667\).

{{svg:c17b_die20r}}

Again, remember that skewed to the left means that there is a longer tail on the left-hand side, and the peak is on the right-hand side.

The graphs are reflections of each other in \(x=10\). For example,

\[P(7\text{ sixes})={}^{20}C_7\times\big(\tfrac16\big)^7\times\big(\tfrac56\big)^{13}={}^{20}C_{13}\times\big(\tfrac56\big)^{13}\times\big(\tfrac16\big)^7=P(13\text{ non-sixes}).\]

Question 11 in Exercise 17B shows how, for a fixed number of trials, the standard deviation decreases as the distribution becomes more skewed. Worked example 8 is a simple example.

4 Skewed binomial distributions

Suppose that a binomial distribution consists of \(n\) independent Bernoulli trials, each with probability \(p\) of success and probability \(q=1-p\) of failure.

  • Reversing ‘success’ and ‘failure’ reverses the probabilities \(p\) and \(q\), and reflects the graph in \(x=\tfrac12n\).
  • If \(p=q=\tfrac12\), then the distribution is symmetric about \(x=\tfrac12n\).
  • If \(p<\tfrac12\), the distribution is skewed to the right (positive skewness).
    If \(p>\tfrac12\), the distribution is skewed to the left (negative skewness).
  • For distributions with the same number of trials, the standard deviation gets smaller as \(p\) gets closer to 0 or 1, that is, as the skewness becomes more pronounced.

Worked example 8Keep for homework
Two binomial random variables \(X\) and \(Y\) both consist of 100 Bernoulli trials. For \(X\), \(p=q=\tfrac12\), and for \(Y\), \(p=\tfrac{1}{10}\). Find the ratio of their standard deviations.
Solution
\[\begin{aligned}\text{For }X,\quad\sigma_X^2&=npq\\&=100\times\tfrac12\times\tfrac12\\&=25,\\\text{so}\quad\sigma_X&=5.\end{aligned}\]
\[\begin{aligned}\text{For }Y,\quad\sigma_Y^2&=npq\\&=100\times\tfrac{1}{10}\times\tfrac{9}{10}\\&=9,\\\text{so}\quad\sigma_Y&=3.\end{aligned}\]
