PP 2James Ruse Agricultural High School, 2020, Year 12 Trial HSC Examination, Mathematics Extension 1, Q12(b)

Using the normal approximation without a continuity correction, as in the school solution.

Let \(X\) be the number of patients who cannot be cured. Then \(X\sim B(150,0.2)\), with \(np=30\) and \(nq=120\). Both are greater than 5 (indeed greater than 10), so the normal approximation is justified.

\[\mu=np=30,\qquad\sigma=\sqrt{npq}=\sqrt{150\times0.2\times0.8}=\sqrt{24}\doteq4.899\] \[P(X\le37)\doteq P\Big(Z\le\frac{37-30}{\sqrt{24}}\Big)\doteq P(Z\le1.43)\doteq0.9236.\]

(The school solution works with the number cured, \(P(X\ge113)\), and gets the same \(z\)-score, \(-1.43\).)

PP 3Ascham School, 2024, Year 12 Trial Examination, Mathematics Extension 1, Q11(e)

Using the normal approximation without a continuity correction, as in the school solution.

Let \(X\) be the number of left-handed people. Then \(X\sim B(100,0.1)\), with \(np=10\) and \(nq=90\), both greater than 5.

\[\mu=np=10,\qquad\sigma^2=npq=9,\qquad\sigma=3.\]

5% of one hundred is 5, so we need \(P(X<5)\):

\[\begin{aligned}P(X<5)&\doteq P\Big(Z<\frac{5-10}{3}\Big)\\&=P(Z<-1.67)\\&=1-\Phi(1.67)\\&\doteq1-0.9525\\&=0.0475.\end{aligned}\]

(The school solution works with the sample proportion, Lesson 17D, and gets the same \(z\)-score.)

PP 6Killara High School, 2024, Year 12 Examination, Mathematics Extension 1 HSC Trial Examination, Q13(d)

Using the normal approximation without a continuity correction, as in the school solution’s main method.

Let \(N\) be the number absent, so \(N\sim B(n,0.05)\), with \(\mu=0.05n\) and \(\sigma=\sqrt{0.05\times0.95\,n}=\sqrt{0.0475n}\).

From the table, \(\Phi(1.32)\doteq0.9066\), so \(P(N\le20)=0.90658\) gives \(z=1.32\):

\[\frac{20-0.05n}{\sqrt{0.0475n}}=1.32,\qquad\text{so}\qquad0.05n+1.32\sqrt{0.0475}\,\sqrt n-20=0.\]

This is a quadratic in \(u=\sqrt n\):   \(0.05u^2+0.28769u-20=0\). Since \(u>0\),

\[u=\frac{-0.28769+\sqrt{0.28769^2+4}}{0.1}\doteq17.329,\qquad n=u^2\doteq300.3,\qquad\text{so}\qquad n=300.\]

(Check: \(np=15\) and \(nq=285\), so the normal approximation is reasonable. The school solution also gives the continuity-correction version, with 20.5 in place of 20, which leads to \(n\doteq309\).)

PP 7James Ruse Agricultural High School, 2023, Year 12 Trial HSC Examination, Mathematics Extension 1, Q13(b)(ii)

Using the normal approximation without a continuity correction, as in the school solution.

Let \(X\) be the number who take less than 12 minutes, so \(X\sim B(n,0.1)\), with \(\mu=0.1n\) and \(\sigma=\sqrt{0.1\times0.9\,n}=0.3\sqrt n\).

From the table, \(\Phi(0.40)\doteq0.6554\), so \(\Phi(-0.40)\doteq0.3446\). Hence \(P(X<9)=0.3446\) gives \(z=-0.4\):

\[\frac{9-0.1n}{0.3\sqrt n}=-0.4,\qquad\text{so}\qquad0.1n-0.12\sqrt n-9=0.\]

This is a quadratic in \(u=\sqrt n\):   \(0.1u^2-0.12u-9=0\). Since \(u>0\),

\[u=\frac{0.12+\sqrt{0.0144+3.6}}{0.2}\doteq10.106,\qquad n=u^2\doteq102.13,\qquad\text{so}\qquad n=102.\]

(The school solution also squares the equation, giving \(0.01n^2-1.8144n+81=0\), with roots \(n\doteq102.13\) and \(n\doteq79.31\). It rejects \(n=79\), because then \(\mu=7.9\) is below 9, so the \(z\)-score of 9 would be positive, not \(-0.4\).)
