The normal distribution

A short review of Year 12 Advanced Chapter 16D–16F: the standard normal distribution and its table, general normal distributions and \(z\)-scores, and applications. Lessons 17C and 17D approximate binomial distributions and sample proportions by normal distributions, so they use all of this. The standard normal table is on the last page of this section.

The standard normal distribution (16D)

Let \(Z\) be the standard normal random variable. Its probability density function is

\[\phi(z)=\dfrac{e^{-\frac12z^2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\qquad\Big(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\doteq0.4\Big)\]
  • The graph of the PDF is a bell-shaped curve, with global maximum at \(z=0\) (the mode) and points of inflection at \(z=1\) and \(z=-1\).
  • The mean is \(\mu=0\) and the standard deviation is \(\sigma=1\). The points of inflection are each one standard deviation from the mean.
{{svg:phi_pdf}}{{svg:phi_area}}
  • The total area under the curve \(y=\phi(z)\) is 1, and the curve is even: it has line symmetry in the \(y\)-axis.
  • The cumulative distribution function is \(\Phi(z)=P(Z\le z)=\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{z}\phi(t)\,dt\), the area under the curve to the left of \(z\). The primitive of \(\phi(z)\) cannot be written with our usual functions, so we read \(\Phi(z)\) from the standard normal table (last page of this section), or use a calculator.
  • Reading the table: go to the row for the units and first decimal place of \(z\), then to the column for the second decimal place. For example, \(\Phi(1.63)\) is in row 1.6, column \(+.03\), so \(\Phi(1.63)\doteq0.9484\). The table gives only \(z\ge0\), because \(\phi(z)\) is even.

Rules for juggling areas (always sketch the curve and shade the area you want):

\[P(Z=a)=0\qquad\Phi(0)=0.5\qquad P(Z\ge z)=1-\Phi(z)\] \[\Phi(-z)=P(Z\ge z)=1-\Phi(z)\qquad P(a\le Z\le b)=\Phi(b)-\Phi(a)\]
  • Because \(P(Z=a)=0\), there is no need to distinguish between \(\le\) and \(\lt\), or between \(\ge\) and \(\gt\).

Worked exampleMore symmetry: intervals that are not centred on 0
Use symmetry and the standard normal table to find:
a\(P(-2.3\le Z\le-0.6)\)b\(P(-1.4\le Z\le0.9)\)
Solution

a Both ends are negative. Reflect in the \(y\)-axis, because \(\phi(z)\) is even:

\(\begin{aligned}[t]P(-2.3\le Z\le-0.6)&=P(0.6\le Z\le2.3)\\&=\Phi(2.3)-\Phi(0.6)\\&\doteq0.9893-0.7257\\&\doteq0.2636.\end{aligned}\)

The empirical rule, and reading the table in reverse (16D)

From the table, \(P(-1\le Z\le1)\doteq0.6827\doteq68\%\), \(P(-2\le Z\le2)\doteq0.9545\doteq95\%\) and \(P(-3\le Z\le3)\doteq0.9973\doteq99.7\%\). These are the empirical rule, or the 68–95–99.7 rule. In a normal distribution, the proportion of scores lying:

  • within 1 standard deviation of the mean is 68%,
  • within 2 standard deviations of the mean is 95%,
  • within 3 standard deviations of the mean is 99.7%.
{{svg:emp1}}{{svg:emp2}}{{svg:emp3}}

Use the symmetry of the curve with these: for example, \(P(Z\gt1)\doteq\tfrac12(100\%-68\%)=16\%\) and \(P(0\le Z\le2)\doteq\tfrac12\times95\%=47.5\%\).

Reading the table in reverse. To solve \(\Phi(z)=p\), find \(p\) in the body of the table and read off \(z\). Take the closest entry, or interpolate between two entries. The graph of \(y=\Phi(z)\) on the right shows this as a horizontal line at height \(p\). If \(p\lt0.5\), then \(z\) is negative: use \(\Phi(-z)=1-p\).

It is standard practice to round percentiles and quartiles of the normal to two decimal places. The quartiles are \(Q_1\doteq-0.67\) and \(Q_3\doteq0.67\), and \(\text{IQR}\doteq1.35\). By the IQR criterion, an outlier is a score more than \(1.5\times\text{IQR}\) beyond a quartile, that is, outside \(-2.70\le z\le2.70\).

Worked exampleTextbook Example 13 (16D)
a Find the 9th decile of the standard normal distribution.
b Find the third quartile \(Q_3\) and the first quartile \(Q_1\) of \(\Phi(z)\).
c Using the IQR criterion, what proportion of the scores of a standard normal random variable would be expected to be outliers?
Solution

a For the 9th decile, we need to solve \(\Phi(z)\doteq0.9\).
From the table, \(\Phi(1.2)\doteq0.8849\) and \(\Phi(1.3)\doteq0.9032\), with difference \(0.0183\) making the 9th decile about \(1.28\).
This agrees with the horizontal line with height 0.9 on the graph of \(\Phi(z)\).

b For the upper quartile, we need to solve \(\Phi(z)\doteq0.75\).
From the table, \(\Phi(0.6)\doteq0.7257\) and \(\Phi(0.7)\doteq0.7580\), with difference \(0.0323\).
Thus \(Q_3\doteq0.675\), and because \(\phi(z)\) is even, \(Q_1\doteq-0.675\).
This agrees with the horizontal line with height 0.75 on the graph of \(\Phi(z)\).

c From part b, the IQR is about \(1.35\), so \(Q_3+1.5\times\text{IQR}\doteq2.70\).
The IQR criterion is that an outlier lies outside \(-2.70\le z\le2.70\).

\(\begin{aligned}[t]\text{Hence}\quad P(Z\text{ is an outlier})&=P(Z\gt2.70)+P(Z\lt-2.70)\\&=2\times P(Z\gt2.70)\\&=2\big(1-\Phi(2.70)\big)\\&\doteq0.007,\end{aligned}\)

so roughly 7 in 1000 scores would be expected to be outliers.

Note: Correct to five significant figures, \(Q_3\doteq0.67449\). It is standard practice to round percentiles and quartiles of the normal to two decimal places, giving \(Q_1\doteq-0.67\), \(Q_3\doteq0.67\) and \(\text{IQR}\doteq1.35\). In a normal distribution, the IQR criterion makes just under 1% of scores outliers.

With the two-decimal table you can read these directly: \(\Phi(1.28)\doteq0.8997\) is the entry closest to \(0.9\), so the 9th decile is \(z\doteq1.28\), and \(\Phi(0.67)\doteq0.7486\) is the entry closest to \(0.75\), so \(Q_3\doteq0.67\).

General normal distributions and \(z\)-scores (16E)

Let \(f(x)\) be the PDF of a normal distribution with mean \(\mu\) and standard deviation \(\sigma\). We write \(X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)\).

  • \(f(x)\) is obtained from the standard normal PDF by stretching horizontally with factor \(\sigma\) and vertically with factor \(\dfrac1\sigma\), then shifting right by \(\mu\) units: \[f(x)=\dfrac1\sigma\,\phi\Big(\dfrac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\Big)=\dfrac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\,e^{-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}}\]
{{svg:gen_normal}}
  • The points of inflection are each one standard deviation from the mean, at \(x=\mu-\sigma\) and \(x=\mu+\sigma\). The mean, the median and the mode coincide.
  • The \(z\)-score of a score \(x\) is the number of standard deviations that \(x\) lies above the mean (negative if \(x\) is below the mean). The conversions are \[z=\dfrac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\qquad\text{and}\qquad x=\mu+\sigma z.\]
  • If \(X\) is normal, then \(Z=\dfrac{X-\mu}{\sigma}\) is standard normal, so \(P(X\le a)=P\Big(Z\le\dfrac{a-\mu}{\sigma}\Big)=\Phi\Big(\dfrac{a-\mu}{\sigma}\Big)\). This is called standardising.
  • The empirical rule becomes: 68% of scores lie in \(\mu-\sigma\le x\le\mu+\sigma\), 95% in \(\mu-2\sigma\le x\le\mu+2\sigma\), and 99.7% in \(\mu-3\sigma\le x\le\mu+3\sigma\).
Worked exampleTextbook Example 15 (16E)
A normally distributed random variable \(X\) has mean 100 and standard deviation 20.
a Write down the two conversion formulae between \(z\)-scores and values of \(x\).
b Find:   i \(P(X\le110)\)   ii \(P(X\ge90)\)
c Find (nearest whole number) the value of \(a\) such that \(P(X\le a)=0.98\).
Solution

a \(z=\dfrac{x-100}{20}\quad\text{and}\quad x=100+20z.\)

b i \(\begin{aligned}[t]&P(X\le110)\\&\quad=P(Z\le0.5)\\&\quad=0.69\end{aligned}\)

ii \(\begin{aligned}[t]P(X\ge90)&=P(Z\ge-0.5)\\&=P(Z\le0.5)\qquad(\phi(x)\text{ is even})\\&=0.69\end{aligned}\)

c From the table, \(\Phi(2.0)=0.9772\) and \(\Phi(2.1)=0.9821\),
so by interpolation, \(\Phi(2.06)=0.98\).
Converting back to \(x\)-values, \(a=100+20\times2.06=141\).

With the two-decimal table you can read \(z\) directly: \(\Phi(2.05)\doteq0.9798\) is the entry closest to \(0.98\), so \(z\doteq2.05\) and \(a\doteq100+20\times2.05=141\), the same answer.

Applications of the normal distribution (16F)
  • Identify \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\), and convert each score to a \(z\)-score. Sketch the curve and shade the region you want.
  • If the \(z\)-scores are whole numbers of standard deviations, the empirical rule is often enough. Otherwise use the table (or technology).
  • A percentage of a population is a probability times 100%. The expected number of scores in a region is (number of scores) \(\times\) (probability).
  • To find a score from a probability, read the table in reverse to find \(z\), then convert back with \(x=\mu+\sigma z\).
Worked exampleTextbook Example 17 (16F)
The Happytime Chocolate Company manufactures a 100 g chocolate–nougat bar. As with any manufacturing process, these chocolate bars do not all have precisely the same weight, and these bars are known to be normally distributed with standard deviation 2 g. To reduce the number of complaints, the company has adjusted its machinery so that the mean is 102 g (such an adjustment does not affect the standard deviation).
a Using the empirical rule where possible, and tables or technology otherwise, find the percentage of chocolate bars:
iof weight less than the stated weight of 100 g,iiof weight greater than 105 g.
b What would the mean weight need to be for there to be less than 1 chocolate bar in 1000 under 100 g?
Solution

a i A weight of 100 g is 1 standard deviation below the mean of 102 g.

By the empirical rule, \(P(-1\le Z\le1)=68\%\),
and using the complement, \(P(Z\lt-1\text{ or }Z\gt1)=32\%\),
so by the even symmetry, \(P(Z\lt-1)=32\%\div2=16\%\).

ii A weight of 105 g is 1.5 standard deviations above the mean of 102 g.

From the table, \(P(Z\le1.5)=93\%\),
so using complements, \(P(Z\gt1.5)=7\%\).

b Reading the table backwards, \(P(Z\le3.1)=0.999\),
so by symmetry and complements, \(P(Z\le-3.1)=0.001\).
Hence we need to make the mean \(3.1\sigma\) above 100 g,
meaning that we set the controls so that \(\mu=100+3.1\times2=106.2\) g.
