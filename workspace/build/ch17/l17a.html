Lesson 17A

Binomial probability

This lesson is about individual binomial probabilities, in preparation for the later sections on binomial distributions. The experiments have a special form: the stages are independent, each stage has just two outcomes, ‘success’ and ‘failure’, with the same probabilities at each stage, and we record only the number of successes, not their order. The probability of \(x\) successes in \(n\) stages is \({}^nC_x\,p^x\,q^{n-x}\), a term of the binomial expansion of \((p+q)^n\). Complementary events and cases handle ‘at least’ and ‘at most’.
{{sec:teal:key:Concept 1:Bernoulli trials and binomial experiments}}
Bernoulli trials

It is convenient to have a name for the independent stages and to describe them carefully. A Bernoulli trial or Bernoulli experiment is a single-stage random experiment with just two outcomes, conventionally called ‘success’ and ‘failure’, to which we usually assign the probabilities \(p\) and \(q=1-p\).

  • The classic example of a Bernoulli trial is tossing a coin, where ‘success’ is heads and ‘failure’ is tails. The probability of success is then \(p=\tfrac12\), and the probability of failure is \(q=1-p=\tfrac12\).
  • The other classic example is throwing a die, provided that we define ‘success’. If we define ‘success’ to be ‘throwing a six’, and record only that, then we have a Bernoulli trial with \(p=\tfrac16\).
  • A Bernoulli trial is completely determined by just one parameter, the probability \(p\) of ‘success’.
Binomial experiments

A binomial experiment is an \(n\)-stage experiment in which:

  • each stage is a Bernoulli trial with the same probability \(p\) of success,
  • the stages are independent: no stage affects any other stage,
  • the random variable \(X\) is the number of successes: order is irrelevant.

Think now about tossing a coin 12 times and counting the number of heads. Or think about throwing four dice and counting the number of sixes.

A binomial experiment is thus completely determined by just two parameters, the number \(n\) of trials, and the probability \(p\) of success at each stage.

Each stage of a binomial experiment is trivially a binomial experiment with just one stage. Thus a Bernoulli trial is a special case of a binomial experiment.

Individual probabilities in a binomial experiment

Here is a classic example. A die is thrown four times. Find the probabilities of getting 0, 1, 2, 3 or 4 sixes. Let S (success) be ‘throwing a six’ and F (failure) be ‘not throwing a six’, so that \(p=\tfrac16\) and \(q=1-p=\tfrac56\).

  • A probability tree diagram shows the sixteen possible outcomes taking account of order, and their probabilities (see the worked example below).
  • When we ignore order, these 16 outcomes collapse to five, and the resulting binomial random variable \(X\) has five possible values, 0, 1, 2, 3 and 4.
  • The outcome ‘two sixes’, for example, can be obtained in \({}^4C_2=6\) different ways, because there are \(\dfrac{4!}{2!\times2!}={}^4C_2\) ways of arranging two Ss and two Fs. Each of these six outcomes has the same probability, so the probabilities add.
  • The five probabilities are the successive terms in the binomial expansion of \(\big(\tfrac16+\tfrac56\big)^4=1^4=1\), so they add to 1.

Worked exampleFrom the text: keep for homework
A die is thrown four times. Find the probabilities of getting 0, 1, 2, 3 or 4 sixes.
Solution

Let S (success) be ‘throwing a six’ and F (failure) be ‘not throwing a six’, so that \(p=\tfrac16\) and \(q=1-p=\tfrac56\). Here is the probability tree diagram showing the sixteen possible outcomes taking account of order, and their respective probabilities. Each branch to S has probability \(\tfrac16\) and each branch to F has probability \(\tfrac56\), so for example \(P(\text{SSFF})=\tfrac16\times\tfrac16\times\tfrac56\times\tfrac56=\tfrac{25}{1296}\).

{{svg:c17a_tree}}

When we ignore order, these 16 outcomes collapse to five, and the resulting binomial random variable \(X\) has five possible values, 0, 1, 2, 3 and 4.

The outcome ‘two sixes’, for example, can be obtained in \({}^4C_2=6\) different ways: SSFF, SFSF, SFFS, FSSF, FSFS, FFSS, because there are \(\dfrac{4!}{2!\times2!}={}^4C_2\) ways of arranging two Ss and two Fs. Each of these six outcomes has the same probability \(\big(\tfrac16\big)^2\times\big(\tfrac56\big)^2\), so

\[P(X=2)={}^4C_2\times\big(\tfrac16\big)^2\times\big(\tfrac56\big)^2.\]

Similar arguments apply to the probabilities of getting 0, 1, 3 and 4 sixes:

ResultProbabilityApproximation
0 sixes\({}^4C_0\times\big(\tfrac16\big)^0\times\big(\tfrac56\big)^4\)0.482\(\,\)25
1 six\({}^4C_1\times\big(\tfrac16\big)^1\times\big(\tfrac56\big)^3\)0.385\(\,\)80
2 sixes\({}^4C_2\times\big(\tfrac16\big)^2\times\big(\tfrac56\big)^2\)0.115\(\,\)74
3 sixes\({}^4C_3\times\big(\tfrac16\big)^3\times\big(\tfrac56\big)^1\)0.015\(\,\)43
4 sixes\({}^4C_4\times\big(\tfrac16\big)^4\times\big(\tfrac56\big)^0\)0.000\(\,\)77
Sum \(=1\)

The five probabilities of course add up to 1, because no other outcomes are possible. This is also clear because the five probabilities are the successive terms in the binomial expansion of \(\big(\tfrac16+\tfrac56\big)^4=1^4=1\):

\[\begin{aligned}\big(\tfrac16+\tfrac56\big)^4&={}^4C_0\times\big(\tfrac16\big)^0\times\big(\tfrac56\big)^4+{}^4C_1\times\big(\tfrac16\big)^1\times\big(\tfrac56\big)^3+{}^4C_2\times\big(\tfrac16\big)^2\times\big(\tfrac56\big)^2\\&\qquad+{}^4C_3\times\big(\tfrac16\big)^3\times\big(\tfrac56\big)^1+{}^4C_4\times\big(\tfrac16\big)^4\times\big(\tfrac56\big)^0.\end{aligned}\]

Remember that

\[(x+y)^4={}^4C_0\,x^0y^4+{}^4C_1\,x^1y^3+{}^4C_2\,x^2y^2+{}^4C_3\,x^3y^1+{}^4C_4\,x^4y^0.\]
{{sec:teal:key:Concept 3:binomial probability, the general case}}
Binomial probability: the general case

Suppose that a multi-stage experiment consists of \(n\) identical stages, and at each stage the probability of ‘success’ is \(p\) and of ‘failure’ is \(q\), where \(p+q=1\). Then

\[P(x\text{ successes and }n-x\text{ failures in that order})=p^x\,q^{n-x}.\]

But there are \({}^nC_x\) ways of ordering \(x\) successes and \((n-x)\) failures, so

\[P(x\text{ successes and }n-x\text{ failures in any order})={}^nC_x\,p^x\,q^{n-x}.\]

This is the term in \(p^xq^{n-x}\) in the expansion of the binomial \((p+q)^n\).

1 Bernoulli trials and binomial probability
  • A Bernoulli trial or Bernoulli experiment is an experiment with two outcomes, ‘success’ and ‘failure’, usually assigned the probabilities \(p\) and \(q=1-p\).
  • A binomial random variable is the outcome of an \(n\)-stage experiment in which:
    each stage is a Bernoulli trial with the same probability \(p\) of success,
    the stages are independent,
    the random variable \(X\) is the number of successes, not their order.
  • Suppose that the probabilities of ‘success’ and ‘failure’ in any stage of an \(n\)-stage binomial experiment are \(p\) and \(q=1-p\) respectively. Then \[P(x\text{ successes})={}^nC_x\,p^x\,q^{n-x}.\]
  • This probability is the term in \(p^xq^{n-x}\) in the expansion of \((p+q)^n=1^n=1\), and the sum of the \(n+1\) binomial probabilities is 1.
  • In particular, if \(p=q=\tfrac12\), then the formula simplifies to \[P(x\text{ successes})={}^nC_x\,\big(\tfrac12\big)^n.\]
Notation, and ‘at least’ questions
  • The pronumeral \(x\) is usually used for the values of a discrete random variable. In this chapter we will therefore mostly use \(x\) in place of the \(r\) that was used in Chapters 14–15 of the Year 11 book.
  • Complementary events and cases answer ‘at least’ and ‘at most’ questions. For example, \(P(\text{at least one})=1-P(\text{none})\), and \(P(\text{at least four of six})=P(4)+P(5)+P(6)\), adding the cases because they are mutually exclusive (see Worked example 2).

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 4:an example where \(p=q=\tfrac12\)}}
When \(p=q=\tfrac12\)

A particular case of binomial probability is when the probabilities \(p\) and \(q\) of ‘success’ and ‘failure’ are both \(\tfrac12\). Then \(p^xq^{n-x}=\big(\tfrac12\big)^n\) for every \(x\), and as stated in Box 1,

\[P(x\text{ heads in }n\text{ tosses of a coin})={}^nC_x\times\big(\tfrac12\big)^n.\]

Worked example 3Keep for homework
If a coin is tossed 100 times, what is the probability that it comes up heads exactly 50 times (correct to four significant figures)?
Solution

Taking \(p=q=\tfrac12\),

\[\begin{aligned}P(50\text{ heads})&={}^{100}C_{50}\times\big(\tfrac12\big)^{50}\times\big(\tfrac12\big)^{50}\\&={}^{100}C_{50}\times\big(\tfrac12\big)^{100}\\&\doteq0.07959.\end{aligned}\]

Note: This is a fairly low probability. Should we have expected a higher probability than this? Hardly, because any result from about 45 to 55 heads would be unlikely to surprise us. In general, as already stated in Box 1 above,

\[P(x\text{ heads in }n\text{ tosses of a coin})={}^nC_x\times\big(\tfrac12\big)^n.\]
{{sec:teal:key:Concept 5:experimental probabilities and binomial probability}}
Experimental probabilities

Some of the most straightforward and important applications of binomial theory arise in situations where the probabilities of ‘success’ and ‘failure’ are determined experimentally. For example, a manufacturer may find by careful testing that 1% of its products are defective, and then use \(p=0.01\) for each item in a box (see Worked example 4).

How many trials are needed?

Some questions (for example Exercise 17A Q12) ask how many trials \(n\) are needed to make \(P(\text{at least one success})\) larger than some number \(c\). Use the complement: \(P(\text{at least one success})=1-q^n\). Then solve \(1-q^n>c\), that is, \(q^n<1-c\), by taking logarithms. Since \(0<q<1\), \(\ln q\) is negative, so the inequality sign reverses when we divide by \(\ln q\):

\[n\ln q<\ln(1-c),\qquad n>\frac{\ln(1-c)}{\ln q}.\]

The answer is the smallest whole number \(n\) that works. Check it, and the number before it.

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 6:each stage is a compound event}}
When each stage is a compound event

Sometimes, when each stage of the experiment is itself a compound event, it may take some work to find the probability of success at each stage. Find \(p\) first, using the product rule and the addition rule (a tree diagram may help). Then apply the binomial formula with that \(p\).

Worked example 5Keep for homework
Joe King and his sister Fay make shirts for a living. Joe works more slowly, but more accurately, making 20 shirts a day, of which 2% are defective. Fay works faster, making 30 shirts a day, of which 4% are defective. If they send out their shirts in randomly mixed parcels of 30 shirts, what is the probability (correct to three significant figures) that no more than two shirts in a box are defective?
Solution

If a shirt is chosen at random from one parcel, then using the product rule and the addition rule, the probability \(p\) that the shirt is defective is

\[\begin{aligned}p&=P(\text{Joe made it, and it is defective})+P(\text{Fay made it, and it is defective})\\&=\frac{20}{50}\times\frac{2}{100}+\frac{30}{50}\times\frac{4}{100}\\&=\frac{4}{125},\end{aligned}\]

so \(p=\tfrac{4}{125}\) and \(q=\tfrac{121}{125}\). Let \(X\) be the number of defective shirts. Then

\[\begin{aligned}P(X\le2)&=P(X=0)+P(X=1)+P(X=2)\\&=\big(\tfrac{121}{125}\big)^{30}+{}^{30}C_1\times\big(\tfrac{121}{125}\big)^{29}\times\tfrac{4}{125}+{}^{30}C_2\times\big(\tfrac{121}{125}\big)^{28}\times\big(\tfrac{4}{125}\big)^2\\&\doteq0.930.\end{aligned}\]
