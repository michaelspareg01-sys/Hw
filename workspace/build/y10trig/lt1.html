Lesson T1

Angles of any size

The definitions of sin, cos and tan are extended from acute angles to angles of any size, positive or negative, using a circle in the coordinate plane. The quadrant of the angle and the ASTC rule give the sign, and the related acute angle gives the size, so every value comes back to an acute angle. The lesson ends with the graphs of sin, cos and tan, and the angle of inclination of a line, where the gradient is \(\tan\alpha\). Sources: Year 11 Advanced book 5D, 5E and 6B; Maths Pathway sheets The Unit Circle, Using Symmetry on the Unit Circle and Graphs of Trigonometric Functions.
{{sec:teal:key:Concept 1:angles of any size and their rays}}
Trigonometric functions of a general angle (Year 11 Advanced, 5D)

The definitions of the trigonometric functions given in Section 5A only apply to acute angles, because in a right-angled triangle, both other angles are acute angles.

This section introduces more general definitions based on circles in the coordinate plane (whose equations are Pythagoras’ theorem, as we saw in Section 3G). The new definitions will apply to any angle, but will, of course, give the same values as the previous definitions for acute angles.

Putting a general angle on the coordinate plane. Let \(\theta\) be any angle — possibly negative, possibly obtuse or reflex, possibly greater than 360°. We shall associate with \(\theta\) a ray with vertex at the origin.

8 The ray corresponding to \(\theta\)
  • The positive direction of the \(x\)-axis is the ray representing the angle 0°.
  • For all other angles, rotate this ray anticlockwise through an angle \(\theta\).

If the angle is negative, the ray is rotated backwards, which means clockwise.

Here are some examples of angles and the corresponding rays. The angles have been written at the ends of the arrows representing the rays.

Notice that one ray can correspond to many angles. For example, all the following angles have the same ray as 40°:

…, −680°, −320°, 40°, 400°, 760°, …

A given ray thus corresponds to infinitely many angles, all differing by multiples of 360°. The relation from rays to angles is thus one-to-many.

{{svg:t1_rays}}
9 Corresponding angles and rays
  • To each angle, there corresponds exactly one ray.
  • To each ray, there correspond infinitely many angles, all differing from each other by multiples of 360°.
Finding angles with the same ray

Add or subtract 360° as many times as you need. One full turn brings the ray back to where it started. For example, \(400^\circ-360^\circ=40^\circ\) and \(40^\circ-360^\circ=-320^\circ\). To find the angle between 0° and 360° with the same ray as a negative angle, keep adding 360° until the answer is positive.

Defining the trigonometric functions for general angles (Year 11 Advanced, 5D)

Let \(\theta\) be any angle, positive or negative.

Construct a circle with centre the origin and any positive radius \(r\). Let the ray corresponding to \(\theta\) intersect the circle at the point \(P(x,y)\).

The six trigonometric functions are now defined in terms of \(x\), \(y\) and \(r\) as follows:

{{svg:t1_def}}
10 Definitions of the trigonometric functions
\[\sin\theta=\frac yr\qquad\qquad\cos\theta=\frac xr\qquad\qquad\tan\theta=\frac yx\]

Note: We chose \(r\) to be ‘any positive radius’. If a different radius were chosen, the two figures would be similar, so the lengths \(x\), \(y\) and \(r\) would stay in the same ratio. Because the definitions depend only on the ratios of the lengths, the values of the trigonometric functions would not change.

In particular, we may use a circle of radius 1 in the definitions. When this is done, however, we would then lose the intuition that a trigonometric function is not a length, but is the ratio of two lengths.

Agreement with the earlier definition. Let \(\theta\) be an acute angle. Construct the ray corresponding to \(\theta\). Let the perpendicular from \(P\) meet the \(x\)-axis at \(M\). Then \(\theta=\angle POM\), so relating the sides to the angle \(\theta\),

hypotenuse = \(OP=r\),   opposite = \(PM=y\),   adjacent = \(OM=x\).

Hence the old and the new definitions are in agreement.

{{svg:t1_agree}}

Note: Most people find that the diagram above is the easiest way to learn the new definitions of the trigonometric functions. Take the old definitions in terms of hypotenuse, opposite and adjacent sides, and make the replacements,

hypotenuse \(\longleftrightarrow r\),   opposite \(\longleftrightarrow y\),   adjacent \(\longleftrightarrow x\).

Left out of this lesson: cosec, sec and cot

The book defines six functions here. The other three, cosec, sec and cot, are the reciprocals of sin, cos and tan. They are not in the Year 10 course, so this pack leaves them out of Boxes 10, 11, 12 and 14 and of the graphs. Where a sentence of the book mentions them, read only the part about sin, cos and tan.

The unit circle (Maths Pathway, The Unit Circle and Using Symmetry on the Unit Circle)

Note: the values in this module have been rounded to two decimal places.

The unit circle has a radius of 1. Angles are measured starting from the positive side of the \(x\)-axis.

The unit circle has a tangent that touches at the point \((1,0)\). The tangent of any angle \((\tan\theta)\) is the \(y\)-value where the angle meets the tangent to the circle.

\(\tan40^\circ=0.84\)

{{svg:t1_unit}}{{svg:t1_tanline}}
Why the unit circle is useful

With \(r=1\), Box 10 becomes \(\sin\theta=\dfrac y1=y\) and \(\cos\theta=\dfrac x1=x\). So the point where the ray meets the unit circle is \((\cos\theta,\sin\theta)\): cos is the \(x\)-coordinate and sin is the \(y\)-coordinate. Dividing, \(\tan\theta=\dfrac yx=\dfrac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta}\). On the tangent line \(x=1\), the ray reaches the height \(\tan\theta\), because the triangles are similar: \(\dfrac{\text{height}}{1}=\dfrac yx\).

Integer multiples of 90°, that is

…, −90°, 0°, 90°, 180°, 270°, 360°, 450°, …

The domains of the trigonometric functions. The trigonometric functions are defined everywhere except where the denominator is zero.

12 Domains of the trigonometric functions
  • \(\sin\theta\) and \(\cos\theta\) are defined for all angles \(\theta\).
  • \(\tan\theta\) and \(\sec\theta\) are undefined when \(x=0\), that is, when \(\theta=\ldots,\,-90^\circ,\,90^\circ,\,270^\circ,\,450^\circ,\,\ldots\)
Reading the table

At 90° the point is \((0,r)\), so \(\tan90^\circ=\dfrac yx=\dfrac r0\), which is undefined: you cannot divide by zero. A boundary angle outside 0° to 270° has the same values as the angle with the same ray. For example, 450° has the same ray as 90°, and −90° has the same ray as 270°.

Quadrant, sign, and related acute angle (Year 11 Advanced, 5E)

Symmetry is an essential aspect of trigonometric functions. In this section we will use symmetry to express the values of the trigonometric functions of any angle in terms of trigonometric functions of acute angles.

The diagram shows the conventional anticlockwise numbering of the four quadrants of the coordinate plane. Acute angles are in the first quadrant, obtuse angles are in the second, and reflex angles are in the third and fourth.

{{svg:t1_quads}}

The signs of the trigonometric functions. The signs of the trigonometric functions depend only on the signs of \(x\) and \(y\). (The radius \(r\) is always positive.) The signs of \(x\) and \(y\) depend in turn only on the quadrant in which the ray lies. Thus we can easily compute the signs of the trigonometric functions from the accompanying diagram and the definitions.

In NSW, these results are usually remembered by the phrase:

14 Signs of the trigonometric functions

‘All Stations To Central’

indicating that the four letters A, S, T and C are placed successively in the four quadrants as shown. The significance of the letters is:

A means all six functions are positive.
S means only sine (and cosecant) are positive.
T means only tangent (and cotangent) are positive.
C means only cosine (and secant) are positive.

Using ASTC with any angle

First find the quadrant of the ray. For a negative angle or an angle greater than 360°, first add or subtract multiples of 360° to get an angle between 0° and 360° with the same ray. Then read the letter: in quadrant 1 all three are positive, in quadrant 2 only sin, in quadrant 3 only tan, in quadrant 4 only cos. The other two are negative.

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 5:the related acute angle}}
The quadrant and the related acute angle (Year 11 Advanced, 5E)

The diagram to the right shows the four rays corresponding to the four angles 30°, 150°, 210° and 330°.

These four rays lie in each of the four quadrants of the plane, and they all make the same acute angle of 30° with the \(x\)-axis. The four rays are thus the reflections of each other in the two axes.

{{svg:t1_30}}
13 Quadrant and related acute angle

Let \(\theta\) be any angle.

  • The quadrant of \(\theta\) is the quadrant (1, 2, 3 or 4) in which the ray lies.
  • The related acute angle of \(\theta\) is the acute angle between the ray and the \(x\)-axis.

Each of the four angles above has the same related acute angle 30°. Notice that \(\theta\) and its related angle are only the same when \(\theta\) is an acute angle.

The angle and the related acute angle. In the diagram to the right, a circle of radius \(r\) has been added to the earlier diagram showing the four angles 30°, 150°, 210° and 330°.

The four points \(P\), \(Q\), \(R\) and \(S\) where the four rays meet the circle are all reflections of each other in the \(x\)-axis and \(y\)-axis. Because of this symmetry, the coordinates of these four points are identical, apart from their signs.

Hence the trigonometric functions of these angles will all be the same too, except that the signs may be different.

{{svg:t1_pqrs}}
15 The angle and the related acute angle
  • The trigonometric functions of any angle \(\theta\) are the same as the trigonometric functions of its related acute angle, apart from a possible change of sign.
  • The sign is best found using the ASTC diagram.

Reference angles (Maths Pathway, Using Symmetry on the Unit Circle)

Every angle on the unit circle has a reference angle in the first quadrant based on the triangle it makes with the \(x\)-axis.

The values from the first quadrant will help us figure out the values of \(\sin\theta\), \(\cos\theta\), and \(\tan\theta\) for other angles.

For example, in question 3 you used the angle 40° as a reference for three other angles (140°, 220° and 320°).

Finding the related acute angle

"Reference angle" is the Maths Pathway name for the related acute angle. For an angle \(\theta\) between 0° and 360°:

  • quadrant 1: the related angle is \(\theta\);  quadrant 2: \(180^\circ-\theta\);
  • quadrant 3: \(\theta-180^\circ\);  quadrant 4: \(360^\circ-\theta\).

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 6:obtuse angles and formulae with general angles}}
General angles with pronumerals (Year 11 Advanced, 5E)

This quadrant-diagram method can be used to generate formulae for expressions such as \(\sin(180^\circ+A)\) or \(\cot(360^\circ-A)\). The trick is to place \(A\) on the quadrant diagram as if it were acute.

17 Some formulae with general angles
\(\begin{aligned}\sin(180^\circ-A)&=\sin A\\ \cos(180^\circ-A)&=-\cos A\\ \tan(180^\circ-A)&=-\tan A\end{aligned}\)\(\begin{aligned}\sin(180^\circ+A)&=-\sin A\\ \cos(180^\circ+A)&=-\cos A\\ \tan(180^\circ+A)&=\tan A\end{aligned}\)\(\begin{aligned}\sin(360^\circ-A)&=-\sin A\\ \cos(360^\circ-A)&=\cos A\\ \tan(360^\circ-A)&=-\tan A\end{aligned}\)

Some people prefer to learn this list of identities to evaluate trigonometric functions, but this seems unnecessary when the quadrant-diagram method is so clear.

Obtuse angles in the Year 10 course

If \(A\) is obtuse, then \(180^\circ-A\) is its related acute angle, and \(A\) is in quadrant 2, where only sin is positive. So, for any obtuse angle \(A\),

\[\begin{aligned}\sin A&=\sin(180^\circ-A)\\ \cos A&=-\cos(180^\circ-A)\\ \tan A&=-\tan(180^\circ-A)\end{aligned}\]

These are the first column of Box 17 read the other way. For example, \(\sin150^\circ=\sin30^\circ\) and \(\cos150^\circ=-\cos30^\circ\).

Negative angles. The ray for \(-A\) is the reflection of the ray for \(A\) in the \(x\)-axis: \(x\) stays the same and \(y\) changes sign. So \(\sin(-A)=-\sin A\), \(\cos(-A)=\cos A\) and \(\tan(-A)=-\tan A\).

The graphs of the trigonometric functions (Year 11 Advanced, 5E)

The sine and cosine graphs are waves, and they are the basis of all the mathematics that deals with waves. The later trigonometry in this course will mostly deal with these wave properties. These two graphs each repeat every 360°, and the graphs are therefore said to have a period of 360°.

The piece of the sine wave from \(\theta=0^\circ\) to \(\theta=90^\circ\) is enough to construct the whole sine wave and the whole cosine wave — use reflections, rotations and translations.

The graphs from −360° to 360° (Year 11 Advanced, 5E)
{{svg:t1_gsin}}{{svg:t1_gcos}}{{svg:t1_gtan}}
Graphs of trigonometric functions (Maths Pathway, Graphs of Trigonometric Functions)

Graphing \(\sin\theta\), \(\cos\theta\) and \(\tan\theta\) makes it easier to look at angles which are equivalent on the unit circle. The horizontal axis will show the value of the angle, \(\theta\). The vertical axis will show the function value: \(\sin\theta\), \(\cos\theta\) or \(\tan\theta\).

Reading the three graphs
  • Each graph of sin and cos stays between −1 and 1. Its value at an angle is the \(y\)-coordinate (sin) or \(x\)-coordinate (cos) of the point on the unit circle.
  • The graph of \(y=\tan x\) repeats every 180°, so its period is 180°. It has vertical asymptotes at \(x=\ldots,-270^\circ,-90^\circ,90^\circ,270^\circ,\ldots\), where \(\tan x\) is undefined (Box 12).
  • Each graph is above the \(x\)-axis exactly in the quadrants where ASTC says the function is positive. For example, \(\cos x\lt0\) for \(90^\circ\lt x\lt270^\circ\), quadrants 2 and 3.
  • A horizontal line \(y=k\) with \(0\lt k\lt1\) or \(-1\lt k\lt0\) meets the sine graph twice between 0° and 360°: two angles with the same sine. The line \(y=0\) meets it three times, at 0°, 180° and 360°.

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 8:gradient and the angle of inclination}}
Gradient (Year 11 Advanced, 6B Box 5, in brief)

The gradient of the interval from \(P(x_1,y_1)\) to \(Q(x_2,y_2)\) is \(\dfrac{\text{rise}}{\text{run}}=\dfrac{y_2-y_1}{x_2-x_1}\). Horizontal intervals have gradient zero, and vertical intervals have no gradient. A line that rises from left to right has positive gradient, and one that falls has negative gradient.

Gradient and the angle of inclination (Year 11 Advanced, 6B)

The angle of inclination of a line is the angle between the upward direction of the line and the positive direction of the \(x\)-axis.

{{svg:t1_incl}}

The two diagrams above show that lines with positive gradients have acute angles of inclination, and lines with negative gradients have obtuse angles of inclination. They also illustrate the trigonometric relationship between the gradient and the angle of inclination \(\alpha\).

8 Angle of inclination

Suppose that a line has angle of inclination \(\alpha\). Then:

  • gradient of line \(=\tan\alpha\)   OR   the line is vertical and \(\alpha=90^\circ\).
Proof

A When \(\alpha\) is acute, as in the first diagram above, then the rise \(MP\) and the run \(OM\) are the opposite and adjacent sides of the triangle \(POM\), so

\[\tan\alpha=\frac{MP}{OM}=\text{gradient of }OP.\]

B When \(\alpha\) is obtuse, as in the second diagram, then \(\angle POM=180^\circ-\alpha\), so

\[\tan\alpha=-\tan\angle POM=-\frac{MP}{OM}=\text{gradient of }OP.\]
Steps the book skips: the proof, part B

\(\tan\alpha=-\tan(180^\circ-\alpha)\) is the obtuse-angle rule from Concept 6. In the second diagram \(M\) is to the left of \(O\), so the run from \(O\) to \(P\) is \(-OM\), and the gradient of \(OP\) is \(\dfrac{MP}{-OM}=-\dfrac{MP}{OM}\).

Steps the book skips: Worked example 6

a \(\tan^{-1}\tfrac53\doteqdot59.04^\circ\). For \(\tan\alpha=-\tfrac53\) the calculator gives \(\tan^{-1}\big(-\tfrac53\big)\doteqdot-59.04^\circ\), which is not an angle of inclination.
