Lesson T3

The ambiguous case

When two sides and the included angle are known, the area formula gives the area; when the area is known, it gives a side or an angle, and an angle found this way has two possible values. When two sides and a non-included angle are known, the sine rule gives one value for the sine of the unknown angle, and there may be two triangles: this is the ambiguous case. The angle sum decides whether the obtuse answer is possible. Source: Year 11 Advanced book 5I (and two Challenge questions from 5J). The lesson ends with a chapter review of Lessons T1 to T3.
Revision from Topic 11 (see the revision pages at the front of this pack)

In any triangle \(ABC\), with side \(a\) opposite \(A\), side \(b\) opposite \(B\) and side \(c\) opposite \(C\):

  • Sine rule (Box 30): \(\dfrac{a}{\sin A}=\dfrac{b}{\sin B}=\dfrac{c}{\sin C}\). To find a side, place the unknown side at the top left (Box 31).
  • Cosine rule (Boxes 35 and 39): \(a^2=b^2+c^2-2bc\cos A\), or \(\cos A=\dfrac{b^2+c^2-a^2}{2bc}\).
  • Area formula (Box 32): \(\text{area}=\tfrac12bc\sin A\), half the product of two sides times the sine of the included angle.
  • From Lesson T1: \(\sin(180^\circ-\theta)=\sin\theta\). So an acute angle and its supplement have the same sine.

Using the area formula — the SAS congruence situation (Year 11 Advanced, 5I)

The area formula requires the SAS congruence situation in which two sides and the included angle are known.

33 Using the area formula

In the SAS congruence situation:
  area = (half the product of two sides) × (sine of the included angle).

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 2:using the area formula to find a side or an angle}}
Using the area formula to find a side or an angle (Year 11 Advanced, 5I)

Substituting into the area formula when the area is known may allow an unknown side or angle to be found.

When finding an angle, the formula will always give a single answer for \(\sin\theta\). There will be two solutions for \(\theta\), however, one acute and one obtuse.

Why both angles work here

With two sides and the included angle, any angle between 0° and 180° makes a triangle. The two answers \(\theta\) and \(180^\circ-\theta\) have the same sine (Lesson T1), so they give two different triangles with the same area. The only exception is \(\sin\theta=1\), which gives the single angle 90°.

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 3:the sine rule in the ambiguous ASS situation}}
Using the sine rule to find an angle — the ambiguous ASS situation (Year 11 Advanced, 5I)

The SAS congruence test requires that the angle be included between the two sides. When two sides and a non-included angle are known, the resulting triangle may not be determined up to congruence, and two triangles may be possible. This situation may be referred to as ‘the ambiguous ASS situation’.

When the sine rule is applied in the ambiguous ASS situation, there is only one answer for the sine of an angle. There may be two possible solutions for the angle itself, however, one acute and one obtuse.

34 Using the sine rule to find an angle

In the ambiguous ASS situation, in which two sides and a non-included angle of the triangle are known,

\[\frac{\text{sine of unknown angle}}{\text{its opposite side}}=\frac{\text{sine of known angle}}{\text{its opposite side}}.\]

Always check the angle sum to see whether both answers are possible.

Worked example 26From the text: keep for homework
Find the angle \(\theta\) in the triangle drawn to the right.
Solution

\(\dfrac{\sin\theta}{7\sqrt6}=\dfrac{\sin45^\circ}{14}\) (always place the unknown at the top left)

\(\sin\theta=7\times\sqrt6\times\dfrac{1}{14}\times\dfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\), since \(\sin45^\circ=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\),

\(\sin\theta=\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\)

\(\theta=60^\circ\) or \(120^\circ\).

{{svg:t3_ex26}}

Note: There are two angles whose sine is \(\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\), one of them acute and the other obtuse. Moreover,

\(120^\circ+45^\circ=165^\circ\)

leaving just 15° for the third angle in the obtuse case, so it all seems to work. Opposite is the ruler-and-compasses construction of the triangle, showing how two different triangles can be produced from the same given ASS measurements.

In many examples, however, the obtuse angle solution can be excluded using the fact that the angle sum of a triangle cannot exceed 180°.

{{svg:t3_ex26c}}
Steps the book skips: Worked example 26

Multiply both sides by \(7\sqrt6\): \(\sin\theta=\dfrac{7\sqrt6\sin45^\circ}{14}\). Then \(\dfrac{7}{14}=\dfrac12\) and \(\dfrac{\sqrt6}{\sqrt2}=\sqrt3\), so \(\sin\theta=\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\). The related angle is 60°, and sine is positive in quadrants 1 and 2, so \(\theta=60^\circ\) or \(180^\circ-60^\circ=120^\circ\). In the construction, the arc of radius 14 about the top vertex cuts the base line twice: once where the angle is 60° and once where it is 120°.

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 4:checking whether the obtuse answer is possible}}
The angle-sum test

Suppose the sine rule gives an acute answer \(\alpha\) for the unknown angle, and the known angle is \(K\). The obtuse answer \(180^\circ-\alpha\) is possible only if \((180^\circ-\alpha)+K\lt180^\circ\), that is, only if \(\alpha\gt K\). Put another way, a larger side is always opposite a larger angle: if the known angle is opposite the longer of the two given sides, the unknown angle must be smaller than it, so only the acute answer is possible.
