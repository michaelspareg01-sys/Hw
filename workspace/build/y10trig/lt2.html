Lesson T2

Exact values and trigonometric equations

The exact values of sin, cos and tan at 30°, 45° and 60° come from two special triangles. With the complementary-angle identity and the ratio identity, they give exact answers without a calculator. Given one trigonometric function, a circle diagram and Pythagoras’ theorem give the others. Then trigonometric equations are solved over a given domain by the quadrants-and-related-angle method, and the Hard band adds compound angles such as \(2x\). Sources: Year 11 Advanced book 5A, 5F, 5G and 5H (and Example 22 of 5I); Maths Pathway sheet Solving Trigonometric Equations. All angles are in degrees.
{{sec:teal:key:Concept 1:exact values for 30°, 45° and 60°}}
Special angles (Year 11 Advanced, 5A)

The values of the trigonometric functions for the three acute angles 30°, 45° and 60° can be calculated exactly using half a square and half an equilateral triangle, and applying Pythagoras’ theorem.

{{svg:t2_special}}

Take half a square with side length 1.
The resulting right-angled triangle \(ABC\) has two angles of 45°.
By Pythagoras’ theorem, the hypotenuse \(AC\) has length \(\sqrt2\)

Take half an equilateral triangle with side length 2 by dropping an altitude.
The resulting right-angled \(\Delta PQR\) has angles of 60° and 30°.
By Pythagoras’ theorem, \(PQ=\sqrt3\).

Applying the definitions in Box 1 gives the values in the table below.

2 A table of exact values
\(\theta\)\(\sin\theta\)\(\cos\theta\)\(\tan\theta\)
30°\(\dfrac12\)\(\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\)\(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt3}\)
45°\(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\)\(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\)1
60°\(\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\)\(\dfrac12\)\(\sqrt3\)
Trigonometric functions of other angles (Year 11 Advanced, 5A)

A calculator is usually used to approximate trigonometric functions of other angles. Always check first that the calculator is in degrees mode — there is usually a key labelled mode or DRG or something similar.

Reading the table from the triangles

Box 1 is \(\sin\theta=\dfrac{\text{opp}}{\text{hyp}}\), \(\cos\theta=\dfrac{\text{adj}}{\text{hyp}}\), \(\tan\theta=\dfrac{\text{opp}}{\text{adj}}\) (see the revision pages). In \(\Delta ABC\), from the 45° angle at \(C\): opp = 1, adj = 1, hyp = \(\sqrt2\). In \(\Delta PQR\), from the 60° angle at \(R\): opp = \(\sqrt3\), adj = 1, hyp = 2; from the 30° angle at \(P\): opp = 1, adj = \(\sqrt3\), hyp = 2. The book’s table also has cosec, sec and cot columns, which are not in the Year 10 course and are left out. The book’s next sentence, on radian mode, is also left out: every angle in this pack is in degrees.

Exact values are often written with a rational denominator: \(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt2}=\dfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\) and \(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt3}=\dfrac{\sqrt3}{3}\). Combined with Lesson T1, this gives exact values for every angle whose related angle is 30°, 45° or 60°.

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 2:tan = sin/cos and complementary angles}}
Trigonometric identities (Year 11 Advanced, 5G)

Working with the trigonometric functions requires knowledge of a number of formulae called trigonometric identities, which relate trigonometric functions to each other.

The two ratio identities. Again using the definitions of the trigonometric functions:

20 The ratio identities

For any angle \(\theta\): \(\tan\theta=\dfrac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta}\quad\)(provided that \(\cos\theta\ne0\))

The three identities for complementary angles. The angles \(\theta\) and \(90^\circ-\theta\) are called complementary angles because they add to a right angle. Three trigonometric identities relate the values of the trigonometric functions at an angle \(\theta\) and the complementary angle \(90^\circ-\theta\).

22 The complementary angle identities

For any angle \(\theta\): \(\cos(90^\circ-\theta)=\sin\theta\)

For example,  \(\cos20^\circ=\sin70^\circ\).

Proof

A [Acute angles]
The triangle to the right shows that when a right-angled triangle is viewed from \(90^\circ-\theta\) instead of from \(\theta\), then the opposite side and the adjacent side are exchanged. Hence

\[\cos(90^\circ-\theta)=\frac ac=\sin\theta.\]
{{svg:t2_comp}}

B [General angles]
For general angles, we take the full circle diagram, and reflect it in the diagonal line \(y=x\). Let \(P'\) be the image of \(P\) under this reflection.

  1. The image \(OP'\) of the ray \(OP\) corresponds to the angle \(90^\circ-\theta\).
  2. The image \(P'\) of \(P(x,y)\) has coordinates \(P'(y,x)\), because reflection in the line \(y=x\) reverses the coordinates of each point.

Applying the definitions of the trigonometric functions to the angle \(90^\circ-\theta\):

\[\cos(90^\circ-\theta)=\frac yr=\sin\theta.\]
{{svg:t2_reflect}}
The other complementary identities

Box 20 also has \(\cot\theta=\dfrac{\cos\theta}{\sin\theta}\), and Box 22 and its proof also have lines for cot and cosec. They are not in the Year 10 course and are left out.

In words, the sine of an angle is the cosine of its complement, and the cosine of an angle is the sine of its complement: \(\sin A=\cos(90^\circ-A)\) and \(\cos A=\sin(90^\circ-A)\). This is where the name cosine comes from (5G Box 23: cosine θ = sine (complement of θ)).

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 3:given one trigonometric function, find another}}
Given one trigonometric function, find another (Year 11 Advanced, 5F)

When the exact value of one trigonometric function is known for an angle, the exact values of the other trigonometric functions can easily be found using the circle diagram and Pythagoras’ theorem.

18 Given one trigonometric function, find another
  • Place a ray or rays on a circle diagram in the quadrants allowed in the question.
  • Complete the triangle and use Pythagoras’ theorem to find whichever of \(x\), \(y\) and \(r\) is missing.
How the definitions are used

From Lesson T1, \(\sin\theta=\dfrac yr\), \(\cos\theta=\dfrac xr\) and \(\tan\theta=\dfrac yx\), with \(r\gt0\) always. The given ratio tells you two of \(x\), \(y\) and \(r\) (any circle will do, because only the ratios matter), Pythagoras’ theorem \(x^2+y^2=r^2\) gives the third, and the quadrant gives its sign.

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 4:trigonometric equations: the domain and boundary angles}}
Trigonometric equations (Year 11 Advanced, 5H)

This piece of work is absolutely vital, because so many problems in later work end up with a trigonometric equation that has to be solved.

There are many small details and qualifications in the methods, and the subject needs a great deal of careful study.

Pay attention to the domain. To begin with a warning, before any other details:

25 The domain

Always pay careful attention to the domain in which the angle can lie.

Equations involving boundary angles. Boundary angles are a special case because they do not lie in any quadrant.

26 The boundary angles

If the solutions are boundary angles, read the solutions off a sketch of the graph.

Inverse trigonometric functions (Maths Pathway, Solving Trigonometric Equations)

The inverse of a trigonometric function swaps the input and the output. For example:

FunctionInverse function
\(\sin(90^\circ)=1\)\(\sin^{-1}(1)=90^\circ\)
\(\cos(0^\circ)=1\)\(\cos^{-1}(1)=0^\circ\)
\(\tan(45^\circ)=1\)\(\tan^{-1}(1)=45^\circ\)

If we know the value of \(\sin\theta\), \(\cos\theta\), or \(\tan\theta\), we can use the inverse function to find value of \(\theta\).

One answer from the calculator, many from the graph

The calculator gives just one angle. An equation such as \(\sin x=-1\) has a solution wherever the graph of \(y=\sin x\) meets the line \(y=-1\), and over a domain of 720° there are two such places. Always count the solutions on a sketch over the whole domain (Box 25).

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 5:the quadrants-and-related-angle method}}
The standard method — quadrants and the related acute angle (Year 11 Advanced, 5H)

Most trigonometric equations eventually come down to one or more equations such as

\(\sin x=-\tfrac12\), where \(-180^\circ\le x\le180^\circ\).

Provided that the angle is not a boundary angle, the method is:

27 The quadrants-and-related-angle method
  1. Draw a quadrant diagram, then draw a ray in each quadrant that the angle could be in.
  2. Find the related acute angle (only work with positive numbers here):
     a using special angles, or
     b using the calculator to find an approximation.
    Never enter a negative number into the calculator at this point.
  3. Mark the angles on the ends of the rays, taking account of any restrictions on \(x\), and write a conclusion.
Multiple solutions with a calculator (Maths Pathway, Solving Trigonometric Equations)

Calculators will only give one solution for a trigonometric function, even though there may be multiple solutions. You can use what you know about the unit circle to find any other solutions.

For example, solving the following equation with a calculator gives us a single solution:

\[\begin{aligned}\sin(x)&=\frac12\\ x&=\sin^{-1}\Big(\frac12\Big)\\&=30\end{aligned}\]

But looking at the unit circle, we can see two possible solutions.

So, \(x\) is actually equal to \(30^\circ\), \(150^\circ\), or any other equivalent angles.

Some trigonometric equations involve more than one trigonometric function. For example,

\(\sin x+\sqrt3\cos x=0\)

The general approach is to use trigonometric identities to produce an equation in only one trigonometric function.

Worked example 21From the text: keep for homework
Solve \(\sin x+\sqrt3\cos x=0\), where \(0^\circ\le x\le360^\circ\).
Solution
\[\begin{aligned}\sin x+\sqrt3\cos x&=0\\ \tan x+\sqrt3&=0\qquad(\div\cos x)\\ \tan x&=-\sqrt3,\ \text{where }0^\circ\le x\le360^\circ.\end{aligned}\]

Because \(\tan x\) is negative, \(x\) is in quadrants 2 or 4.
The tan of the related acute angle is \(\sqrt3\), so the related angle is 60°.
Hence  \(x=120^\circ\) or \(300^\circ\).

Steps the book skips: Worked example 21

Dividing by \(\cos x\) uses Box 20, \(\dfrac{\sin x}{\cos x}=\tan x\). It is safe: if \(\cos x=0\), the equation would say \(\sin x=0\) as well, but sin and cos are never both 0, so no solution is lost. Quadrant 2: \(180^\circ-60^\circ=120^\circ\); quadrant 4: \(360^\circ-60^\circ=300^\circ\).

{{sec:rose:key:Concept 7:extension for the Hard band: compound angles}}
Equations with compound angles (Year 11 Advanced, 5H)

In some equations, the angle is a function of \(x\) rather than simply \(x\) itself. For example,

\(\tan2x=\sqrt3\),  where \(0^\circ\le x\le360^\circ\),  or  \(\sin(x-250^\circ)=\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\),  where \(0^\circ\le x\le360^\circ\).

These equations are really trigonometric equations in the compound angles \(2x\) and \((x-250^\circ)\) respectively. The secret lies in solving for the compound angle, and in first calculating the domain for that compound angle.

29 Equations with compound angles
  1. Let \(u\) be the compound angle.
  2. Find the restrictions on \(u\) from the given restrictions on \(x\).
  3. Solve the trigonometric equation for \(u\).
  4. Hence solve for \(x\).
For the Hard band only

Compound angles go beyond the Year 10 course and appear only in the Hard band of this lesson. The new step is the domain: if \(0^\circ\le x\le360^\circ\), then \(0^\circ\le2x\le720^\circ\), so \(u=2x\) goes round the circle twice and there are twice as many solutions.
