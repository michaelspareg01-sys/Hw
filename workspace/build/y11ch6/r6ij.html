Naming the sides and angles

The usual statement of all three rules uses the convention that the vertices are named with upper-case letters, then each side takes as its name the lower-case letter of the opposite vertex: side \(a\) is opposite \(A\), side \(b\) is opposite \(B\), and side \(c\) is opposite \(C\).

31 The sine rule

In any triangle \(ABC\), \(\qquad\dfrac{a}{\sin A}=\dfrac{b}{\sin B}=\dfrac{c}{\sin C}.\)

‘The ratio of each side to the sine of the opposite angle is constant.’

32 Using the sine rule to find a side

In the AAS congruence situation: \(\qquad\dfrac{\text{unknown side}}{\text{sine of its opposite angle}}=\dfrac{\text{known side}}{\text{sine of its opposite angle}}.\)

Always place the unknown side at the top left of the equation.

If two angles of a triangle are known, so is the third, because the angles add to \(180^\circ\).

33 The area formula

In any triangle \(ABC\), \(\qquad\text{area }\triangle ABC=\tfrac12bc\sin A.\)

‘The area of a triangle is half the product of any two sides times the sine of the included angle.’

34 Using the area formula

In the SAS congruence situation: \(\qquad\text{area}=(\text{half the product of two sides})\times(\text{sine of the included angle}).\)

Substituting into the area formula when the area is known may allow an unknown side or angle to be found. When finding an angle, the formula will always give a single answer for \(\sin\theta\). There will be two solutions for \(\theta\), however, one acute and one obtuse.

35 Using the sine rule to find an angle

In the ambiguous ASS situation, where two sides and a non-included angle of the triangle are known,

\[\frac{\text{sine of unknown angle}}{\text{its opposite side}}=\frac{\text{sine of known angle}}{\text{its opposite side}}.\]

Always check the angle sum to see whether both answers are possible.

36 The cosine rule

In any triangle \(ABC\), \(\qquad a^2=b^2+c^2-2bc\cos A.\)

‘The square of any side of a triangle equals: the sum of the squares of the other two sides, minus twice the product of those sides and the cosine of their included angle.’

37 The cosine rule and Pythagoras’ theorem
  • When \(\angle A=90^\circ\), then \(\cos A=0\) and the cosine rule is Pythagoras’ theorem.
  • The last term is thus a correction to Pythagoras’ theorem when \(\angle A\ne90^\circ\).
  • When \(\angle A<90^\circ\), then \(\cos A\) is positive, so \(a^2<b^2+c^2\).
    When \(\angle A>90^\circ\), then \(\cos A\) is negative, so \(a^2>b^2+c^2\).
38 Using the cosine rule to find a side

In the SAS congruence situation:
(square of any side) \(=\) (sum of squares of other two sides)
\(\qquad-\) (twice the product of those sides) \(\times\) (cosine of their included angle).

39 Using the cosine rule to find an angle

In the SSS congruence situation:

  • Substitute into the cosine rule and solve for \(\cos\theta\).
40 The cosine rule with \(\cos A\) as subject

In any triangle \(ABC\), \(\qquad\cos A=\dfrac{b^2+c^2-a^2}{2bc}.\)

Notice that \(\cos\theta\) is positive when \(\theta\) is acute, and is negative when \(\theta\) is obtuse. Hence there is only ever one solution for the unknown angle, unlike the situation for the sine rule, where there are often two possible angles.
