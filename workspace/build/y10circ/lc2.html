Lesson C2

Angles at the centre and circumference

The angle in a semicircle is a special case of a much more general theorem: an angle at the circumference is half the angle at the centre standing on the same arc. From it come two key results: angles at the circumference on the same arc are equal, and the opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral are supplementary, with its exterior angle equal to the opposite interior angle. The converse gives a test for a quadrilateral to be cyclic. Two extensions show how circles prove that the altitudes of a triangle are concurrent and what the common ratio in the sine rule really is. The teaching follows the AMSI TIMES module Circle Geometry, pages 11 to 17.
{{sec:teal:key:Concept 1:the angle at the centre is twice the angle at the circumference}}
Angles at the centre and circumference (AMSI, page 11)

The angle-in-a-semicircle theorem can be generalised considerably. In each diagram below, \(AB\) is an arc of a circle with centre \(O\), and \(P\) is a point on the opposite arc. The arc \(AB\) subtends the angle \(\angle AOB\) at the centre. The arc also subtends the angle \(\angle APB\), called an angle at the circumference subtended by the arc \(AB\).

{{svg:c2_arcs1}}{{svg:c2_arcs2}}{{svg:c2_arcs3}}

In the middle diagram, where the arc is a semicircle, the angle at the centre is a straight angle, and by the previous theorem, the angle at the circumference is a right angle – exactly half. We shall show that this relationship holds also for the other two cases, when the arc is a minor arc (left-hand diagram) or a major arc (right-hand diagram). The proof uses isosceles triangles in a similar way to the proof of Thales’ theorem.

Theorem

An angle at the circumference of a circle is half the angle at the centre subtended by the same arc.

Worked proofFrom the text (AMSI, pages 11 and 12): keep for homework
Proof

Let \(AB\) be an arc of a circle with centre \(O\), and let \(P\) be any point on the opposite arc. We need to prove \(\angle AOB=2\angle APB\). The proof divides into three cases, depending on whether:

{{svg:c2_case1}}Case 1: \(O\) lies inside \(\triangle ABP\)
{{svg:c2_case2}}Case 2: \(O\) lies on \(\triangle APB\)
{{svg:c2_case3}}Case 3: \(O\) lies outside \(\triangle APB\).

Case 1: Join \(PO\) and produce \(PO\) to \(Q\). Then \(OA=OB=OP\) (radii), so we have two isosceles triangles \(OAP\) and \(OBP\).

Let\(\angle OAP=\alpha\) and \(\angle OBP=\beta\). Then\(\angle APO=\alpha\)(base angles of isosceles \(\triangle OAP\)) and\(\angle BPO=\beta\)(base angles of isosceles \(\triangle OBP\)). Hence\(\angle AOQ=2\alpha\)(exterior angle of \(\triangle OAP\)) and\(\angle BOQ=2\beta\) so(exterior angle of \(\triangle OBP\)), \(\angle AOB=2\alpha+2\beta=2(\alpha+\beta)=2\times\angle APB.\)
Steps the source skips: Case 1

The radius \(PO\) splits \(\angle APB\) into \(\angle APO+\angle BPO=\alpha+\beta\), and splits \(\angle AOB\) into \(\angle AOQ+\angle BOQ\). The exterior angle of \(\triangle OAP\) at \(O\) equals the sum of the two opposite interior angles, \(\alpha+\alpha=2\alpha\). Cases 2 and 3 are AMSI Exercise 6, Hard homework Question 12.

Reflex angles at the centre

If the angle at the circumference is obtuse, it stands on a major arc, and the angle at the centre on that arc is a reflex angle, more than \(180^\circ\). The theorem still holds: an angle of \(160^\circ\) at the circumference goes with a reflex angle of \(320^\circ\) at the centre. Angles in a revolution at the centre add to \(360^\circ\).

Worked exampleFrom the text (AMSI, page 12): keep for homework

A punter stands on the edge of a circular racing track. With his binoculars he is following a horse that is galloping around the track at one revolution a minute. Explain why the punter’s binoculars are rotating at a constant rate of half a revolution per minute.

Solution

As the horse moves from position \(A\) to position \(B\), the horse moves an angle \(\angle AOB=2\theta\) about the centre \(O\) of the track. The angle at the circumference is half the angle at the centre, so \(\angle APB=\theta\) which means that the punter’s binoculars rotate by an angle \(\theta\) the horse moves from \(A\) to \(B\). Hence the punter is rotating his binoculars at a constant rate that is half the rate at which the horse is rotating about the centre.

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 2:angles on the same arc}}
Angles subtended by the same arc (AMSI, page 12)

In the diagram to the right, the two angles \(\angle APB\) and \(\angle AQB\) are subtended by the same (minor) arc \(AB\). Each angle is half the angle \(\angle AOB\) at the centre subtended by the same arc, so \(\angle APB=\angle AQB\).

This corollary of the previous theorem is a particularly significant result about angles in circles:

{{svg:c2_samearc}}
Theorem

Two angles at the circumference subtended by the same arc are equal.

Thales’ theorem is a special case of this theorem.

Some alternative terminology (AMSI, page 13)

The last two theorems are often expressed in slightly different language, and some explanation is needed to avoid confusion.

  1. An angle subtended by an arc is often said to be standing on the arc. With this terminology, the two theorems become:
    • An angle at the circumference of a circle is half the angle at the centre standing on the same arc.
    • Two angles at the circumference standing on the same arc are equal.
    In the context of these two theorems, it is best to avoid the phrases ‘standing on a chord \(AB\)’ and ‘subtended by a chord \(AB\)’, because we need to distinguish between angles subtended by the major arc \(AB\) and angles subtended by the minor arc \(AB\).
  1. In the upper diagram to the right, the angle \(\angle APB\) is called an angle in the (major) segment. Notice that it actually stands on the minor arc \(AB\), which can be confusing. We have already used this terminology before in speaking about an ‘angle in a semicircle’. With this terminology, the last theorem becomes:

    Two angles in the same segment of a circle are equal.

    The situation is illustrated in the lower diagram to the right.

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 3:cyclic quadrilaterals}}
Cyclic quadrilaterals (AMSI, page 14)

In the module, Congruence, we showed how to draw the circumcircle through the vertices of any triangle. To do this, we showed that the perpendicular bisectors of its three sides are concurrent, and that their intersection, called the circumcentre of the triangle, is equidistant from each vertex.

No other circle passes through these three vertices. If we tried to take as centre a point \(P\) other than the circumcentre, then \(P\) would not lie on one of the perpendicular bisectors, so it could not be equidistant from the three vertices.

When there are four points, we can always draw a circle through any three of them (provided they are not collinear), but only in special cases will that circle pass through the fourth point. A cyclic quadrilateral is a quadrilateral whose vertices all lie on a circle. This is the last type of special quadrilateral that we shall consider.

{{svg:c2_circum}}{{svg:c2_cquad}}

Constructing the circumcircle of a cyclic quadrilateral. Suppose that we are given a quadrilateral that is known to be cyclic, but whose circumcentre is not shown (perhaps it has been rubbed out). The circumcentre of the quadrilateral is the circumcentre of the triangle formed by any three of its vertices, so the construction to the right will find its circumcentre.

{{svg:c2_constr}}

The opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral. The distinctive property of a cyclic quadrilateral is that its opposite angles are supplementary. The following proof uses the theorem that an angle at the circumference is half the angle at the centre standing on the same arc.

Theorem

The opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral are supplementary.

Worked proofFrom the text (AMSI, page 15): keep for homework
Proof

Let \(ABCD\) be a cyclic quadrilateral with \(O\) the centre of the circle.

Join the radii \(OB\) and \(OD\). Let \(\alpha\) and \(\gamma\) be the angles at the centre, as shown on the diagram.

{{svg:c2_opp}}

\(\begin{aligned}[t]\text{Then}\quad\alpha+\gamma&=360^\circ&&\text{(angles in a revolution at }O)\\\text{Also}\quad\angle A&=\tfrac12\alpha&&\text{(angles on the same arc }BCD)\\\text{and}\quad\angle C&=\tfrac12\gamma&&\text{(angles on the same arc }BAD)\\\text{so}\quad\angle A+\angle C&=\tfrac12\alpha+\tfrac12\gamma=180^\circ&&\end{aligned}\)

Hence also \(\angle ABC+\angle ADC=180^\circ\) (angle sum of quadrilateral \(ABCD\))

Here is an alternative proof using the fact that two angles in the same segment are equal. (AMSI Exercise 8, Medium homework Question 8.)

Steps the source skips: the opposite angles

\(\alpha\) is the angle at the centre standing on the arc \(BCD\), and \(\angle A\) stands on the same arc, so \(\angle A=\tfrac12\alpha\). Then \(\tfrac12\alpha+\tfrac12\gamma=\tfrac12(\alpha+\gamma)=\tfrac12\times360^\circ=180^\circ\). The angles of any quadrilateral add to \(360^\circ\), so the other pair also adds to \(360^\circ-180^\circ=180^\circ\).

Exterior angles of a cyclic quadrilateral (AMSI, page 15)

An exterior angle of a cyclic quadrilateral is supplementary to the adjacent interior angle, so is equal to the opposite interior angle. This gives us the corollary to the cyclic quadrilateral theorem:

Theorem

An exterior angle of a cyclic quadrilateral is equal to the opposite interior angle.

Worked proofFrom the text (AMSI, page 15): keep for homework
Proof

In the diagram to the right, \(BC\) is produced to \(P\) to form the exterior angle \(\angle PCD\). This exterior angle and \(\angle A\) are both supplementary to \(\angle BCD\), so they are equal.

{{svg:c2_ext}}
Steps the source skips: the exterior angle

\(\angle PCD+\angle BCD=180^\circ\) (angles on the straight line \(BCP\)) and \(\angle A+\angle BCD=180^\circ\) (opposite angles of the cyclic quadrilateral). So \(\angle PCD=180^\circ-\angle BCD=\angle A\).

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 4:a test for a cyclic quadrilateral}}
Extension – A test for a cyclic quadrilateral (AMSI, page 16)

The property of a cyclic quadrilateral proven earlier, that its opposite angles are supplementary, is also a test for a quadrilateral to be cyclic. That is the converse is true. This theorem completes the structure that we have been following – for each special quadrilateral, we establish its distinctive properties, and then establish tests for it.

The proof uses ‘proof by contradiction’, and is thus a little more difficult than other Year 10 material.

Theorem

If the opposite angles of a quadrilateral are supplementary, then the quadrilateral is cyclic.

Worked proofFrom the text (AMSI, page 16): keep for homework
Proof

Let \(ABCD\) be a quadrilateral with \(\angle A+\angle C=180^\circ\).

Construct the circle through \(A\), \(B\) and \(D\), and suppose, by way of contradiction, that the circle does not pass through \(C\).

Let \(DC\), produced if necessary, meet the circle again at \(X\), and join \(XB\).

Then \(\angle A\) and \(\angle BXD\) are supplementary because \(ABXD\) is a cyclic quadrilateral,

so the angles \(\angle DXB\) and \(\angle DCB\) are equal, so \(XB\parallel CB\)

Hence \(XB\) and \(CB\) are the same line, so \(C\) and \(X\) coincide, that is the circle does pass through \(C\).

{{svg:c2_conv}}
Steps the source skips: the converse

Both \(\angle DXB\) and \(\angle DCB\) are \(180^\circ-\angle A\). They are corresponding angles on the line \(DXC\), so \(XB\parallel CB\). Two parallel lines through the same point \(B\) must be the same line, which forces \(X=C\). Hard homework Question 10 (AMSI Exercise 11) proves the same test in exterior-angle form.

Extension – The sine rule and circle geometry (AMSI, page 17)

The sine rule states that for any triangle \(ABC\), the ratio of any side over the sine of its opposite angle is a constant,

\[\frac{a}{\sin A}=\frac{b}{\sin B}=\frac{c}{\sin C}\]

Each term is the ratio of a length over a pure number, so their common value seems to be a length. Thus it reasonable to ask, what is this common length? The answer is a surprise – the common length is the diameter of the circumcircle through the vertices of the triangle.

The proof of this result provides a proof of the sine rule that is independent of the proof given in the module, Further Trigonometry.

{{svg:c2_sine_t}}{{svg:c2_sine_c}}
Theorem

In any triangle \(ABC\), \(\dfrac{a}{\sin A}=\dfrac{b}{\sin B}=\dfrac{c}{\sin C}=2R\), where \(R\) is the radius of the circumcircle.

Worked proofFrom the text (AMSI, page 17): keep for homework
Proof

Let \(O\) be the centre of the circumcircle through \(A\), \(B\) and \(C\), and let \(\angle A=\alpha\).

It is sufficient to prove that \(\dfrac{a}{\sin A}\) is the diameter of the circumcircle.

There are three cases, as shown below.

{{svg:c2_sine1}}Case 1: \(A\) and \(O\) lie on the same side of \(BC\).
{{svg:c2_sine2}}Case 2: \(A\) and \(O\) lie on opposite sides of \(BC\).
{{svg:c2_sine3}}Case 3: \(O\) lies on \(BC\)

In cases 1 and 2, construct the diameter \(BOP\), and join \(PC\).

Finishing the proof

The three cases are completed in AMSI Exercise 13, Hard homework Question 15. In Case 1, \(\angle BCP=90^\circ\) (angle in a semicircle) and \(\angle P=\alpha\) (angles on the same arc \(BC\)), so in right-angled \(\triangle BCP\), \(\sin\alpha=\dfrac{a}{2R}\).
