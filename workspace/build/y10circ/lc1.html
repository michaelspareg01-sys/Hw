Lesson C1

Chords, radii and angles

This lesson sets up the language of circles: radius, chord, diameter, secant, arc, sector and segment, and what it means for an angle to be subtended. Then come the first theorems, each proved with isosceles and congruent triangles: the line from the centre to the midpoint of a chord is perpendicular to it, equal chords subtend equal angles at the centre, and an angle in a semicircle is a right angle. The teaching follows the AMSI TIMES module Circle Geometry, pages 5 to 11.
{{sec:teal:key:Concept 1:circles and their parts}}
Radii and chords (AMSI, page 5)

We begin by recapitulating the definition of a circle and the terminology used for circles. Throughout this module, all geometry is assumed to be within a fixed plane.

  • A circle is the set of all points in the plane that are a fixed distance (the radius) from a fixed point (the centre).
  • Any interval joining a point on the circle to the centre is called a radius. By the definition of a circle, any two radii have the same length. Notice that the word ‘radius’ is being used to refer both to these intervals and to the common length of these intervals.
{{svg:c1_terms}}
  • An interval joining two points on the circle is called a chord.
  • A chord that passes through the centre is called a diameter. Since a diameter consists of two radii joined at their endpoints, every diameter has length equal to twice the radius. The word ‘diameter’ is used to refer both to these intervals and to their common length.
  • A line that cuts a circle at two distinct points is called a secant. Thus a chord is the interval that the circle cuts off a secant, and a diameter is the interval cut off by a secant passing through the centre of a circle.
Chords, secants and tangents (Maths Pathway)
{{svg:c1_mp_chord}}

A chord is a line segment between two points on a circle

{{svg:c1_mp_secant}}

A secant is a line which meets the circle at two points, and extends beyond the circle

{{svg:c1_mp_tangent}}

A tangent is a line which intersects the circle at one point

Note: A chord which is extended into a full line is a secant.

Tangents come later

Lesson C3 studies tangents in detail. For now you only need to recognise one: a tangent touches the circle at exactly one point, while a secant cuts it at two.

Symmetries of a circle (AMSI, page 6)

Circles have an abundance of symmetries:

  • A circle has every possible rotation symmetry about its centre, in that every rotation of the circle about its centre rotates the circle onto itself.
  • If \(AOB\) is a diameter of a circle with centre \(O\), then the reflection in the line \(AOB\) reflects the circle onto itself. Thus every diameter of the circle is an axis of symmetry.
{{svg:c1_sym_a}}{{svg:c1_sym_b}}{{svg:c1_sym_c}}

As a result of these symmetries, any point \(P\) on a circle can be moved to any other point \(Q\) on the circle. This can be done by a rotation through the angle \(\theta=\angle POQ\) about the centre. It can also be done by a reflection in the diameter \(AOB\) bisecting \(\angle POQ\). Thus every point on a circle is essentially the same as every other point on the circle – no other figure in the plane has this property except for lines.

Congruence and similarity of circles (AMSI, page 6)

Any two circles with the same radius are congruent – if one circle is moved so that its centre coincides with the centre of the other circle, then it follows from the definition that the two circles will coincide.

More generally, any two circles are similar – move one circle so that its centre coincides with the centre of the other circle, then apply an appropriate enlargement so that it coincides exactly with the second circle.

{{svg:c1_cong}}

A circle forms a curve with a definite length, called the circumference, and it encloses a definite area. The similarity of any two circles is the basis of the definition of \(\pi\), the ratio of the circumference and the diameter of any circle. We saw in the module, The Circles that if a circle has radius \(r\), then

\[\text{circumference of the circle}=2\pi r\qquad\text{and}\qquad\text{area of the circle}=\pi r^2\]
Segments, and the word ‘subtend’ (AMSI, page 8)

A chord \(AB\) of a circle divides the circle into two segments. If \(AB\) is a diameter, the two congruent segments are called semicircles – the word ‘semicircle’ is thus used both for the semicircular arc, and for the segment enclosed by the arc and the diameter. Otherwise, the two segments are called a major segment and a minor segment.

The word ‘subtend’ literally means ‘holds under’, and is often used in geometry to describe an angle.

Suppose that we have an interval or arc \(AB\) and a point \(P\) not on \(AB\). Join the intervals \(AP\) and \(BP\) to form the angle \(\angle APB\). We say that the angle \(\angle APB\) is the angle subtended by the interval or arc \(AB\) at the point \(P\).

Our own picture: segments and a subtended angle

The chord \(AB\) cuts the circle into the shaded minor segment and the unshaded major segment. The angle \(\angle APB\), marked in blue, is the angle subtended by the chord \(AB\) at the point \(P\). Think of the chord \(AB\) as ‘held under’ the two arms \(PA\) and \(PB\).

Radii and chords (AMSI, page 7)

Let \(AB\) be a chord of a circle not passing through its centre \(O\). The chord and the two equal radii \(OA\) and \(BO\) form an isosceles triangle whose base is the chord. The angle \(\angle AOB\) is called the angle at the centre subtended by the chord.

In the module, Rhombuses, Kites and Trapezia we discussed the axis of symmetry of an isosceles triangle. Translating that result into the language of circles:

{{svg:c2_iso}}
Theorem

Let \(AB\) be a chord of a circle with centre \(O\). The following three lines coincide:

  • The bisector of the angle \(\angle AOB\) at the centre subtended by the chord.
  • The interval joining \(O\) and the midpoint of the chord \(AB\).
  • The perpendicular bisector of the chord \(AB\).
{{svg:c2_thm}}
Two theorems related to chords and radii (Maths Pathway)
{{svg:c2_mp1}}

If a radius is perpendicular to a chord, then this radius also bisects the chord.

{{svg:c2_mp2}}

If a radius bisects a chord, then this radius is also perpendicular to the chord.

Steps the source skips: proving the three lines are the same line

The AMSI module quotes this theorem from an earlier module, so here are the three short proofs. In each, \(M\) is a point of \(AB\), and \(\triangle OAM\) and \(\triangle OBM\) share the side \(OM\), with \(OA=OB\) (radii).

  1. Midpoint gives perpendicular. If \(AM=BM\), then \(\triangle OAM\equiv\triangle OBM\) (SSS). The worked example below finishes this proof.
  2. Perpendicular gives midpoint. If \(OM\perp AB\), then \(\angle OMA=\angle OMB=90^\circ\), so \(\triangle OAM\equiv\triangle OBM\) (RHS). So \(AM=BM\) (matching sides of congruent triangles).
  3. Angle bisector gives both. If \(\angle AOM=\angle BOM\), then \(\triangle OAM\equiv\triangle OBM\) (SAS). So \(AM=BM\), and \(\angle OMA=\angle OMB\), which are adjacent angles on the straight line \(AB\), so each is \(90^\circ\).

So each of the three lines passes through \(O\) and the midpoint of \(AB\). Only one line passes through two given points, so the three lines coincide.

Trigonometry and chords (AMSI, page 7)

Constructions with radii and chords give plenty of opportunity for using trigonometry.

This exercise shows that sine can be regarded as the length of the semichord \(AM\) in a circle of radius 1, and cosine as the perpendicular distance of the chord from the centre. Until modern times, tables of sines were compiled as tables of chords or semichords, and the name ‘sine’ is conjectured to have come in a complicated and confused way from the Indian word for semichord.

Which exercise?

‘This exercise’ is AMSI Exercise 2, Medium homework Question 9 below: in a circle of radius 1, \(AM=\sin\theta\) and \(OM=\cos\theta\). In a circle of radius \(r\), the same right-angled triangle \(OAM\) gives \(AM^2+OM^2=r^2\) by Pythagoras’ theorem, which finds a chord, a radius or the distance of a chord from the centre.

Arcs and sectors (AMSI, pages 7 and 8)

Let \(A\) and \(B\) be two different points on a circle with centre \(O\). These two points divide the circle into two opposite arcs. If the chord \(AB\) is a diameter, then the two arcs are called semicircles. Otherwise, one arc is longer than the other – the longer arc is called the major arc \(AB\) and the shorter arc is called the minor arc \(AB\).

{{svg:c3_arc}}

Now join the radii \(OA\) and \(OB\). The reflex angle \(\angle AOB\) is called the angle subtended by the major arc \(AB\). The non-reflex angle \(\angle AOB\) is called the angle subtended by the minor arc \(AB\), and is also the angle subtended by the chord \(AB\).

The two radii divide the circle into two sectors, called correspondingly the major sector \(OAB\) and the minor sector \(OAB\).

It is no surprise that equal chords and equal arcs both subtend equal angles at the centre of a fixed circle. The result for chords can be proven using congruent triangles, but congruent triangles cannot be used for arcs because they are not straight lines, so we need to identify the transformation involved.

Theorem

a Equal chords of a circle subtend equal angles at the centre.
b Equal arcs of a circle subtend equal angles at the centre.

Worked proofFrom the text (AMSI, page 8): keep for homework
Proof

a In the diagram to the right, \(\triangle AOB\equiv\triangle POQ\) (SSS)
so \(\angle AOB=\angle POQ\) (matching angles of congruent triangles)

b Rotate the circle so that the arc \(PQ\) coincides with the arc \(AB\) or \(BA\). Then the angles \(\angle POQ\) and \(\angle AOB\) coincide, and hence are equal.

{{svg:c3_proof}}
Steps the source skips: the SSS test in part a

The three pairs of equal sides are \(OA=OP\) (radii), \(OB=OQ\) (radii) and \(AB=PQ\) (the chords are given equal). Every radius of a circle has the same length, which is why two triangles with their apex at the centre are so often congruent.

Equal angles and equal chords (Maths Pathway)

Two theorems related to chords:

{{svg:c3_mp1}}

In a circle, if two chords are equal, then the angles they subtend at the centre are equal.

{{svg:c3_mp2}}

In a circle, if two angles subtended at the centre are equal, then the chords which subtend them are equal.

The second theorem is the converse

The second theorem swaps the ‘if’ part and the ‘then’ part of the first. Its proof uses the SAS test instead of SSS: two radii and the equal angle between them. You prove it in Hard homework Question 12.

Angles in a semicircle (AMSI, page 9)

Let \(AOB\) be a diameter of a circle with centre \(O\), and let \(P\) be any other point on the circle. The angle \(\angle APB\) subtended at \(P\) by the diameter \(AB\) is called an angle in a semicircle. This angle is always a right angle – a fact that surprises most people when they see the result for the first time.

{{svg:c4_semi}}
Theorem

An angle in a semicircle is a right angle.

Worked proofFrom the text (AMSI, page 9): keep for homework
Proof

Let \(AB\) be a diameter of a circle with centre \(O\), and let \(P\) be any other point on the circle.

Join the radius \(PO\), and let \(\alpha=\angle A\) and \(\beta=\angle B\).

The triangles \(AOP\) and \(BOP\) are isosceles because all radii are equal, so \(\angle APO=\alpha\) and \(\angle BPO=\beta\) (base angles of isosceles triangles \(AOP\) and \(BOP\)).

{{svg:c4_proof}}

Hence \(\alpha+\beta+(\alpha+\beta)=180^\circ\) (angle sum of \(\triangle APB\))

\(\begin{aligned}[t]2\alpha+2\beta&=180^\circ\\\alpha+\beta&=90^\circ,\end{aligned}\)

so \(\angle APB=90^\circ\), as required.

This famous theorem is traditionally ascribed to the Greek mathematician Thales, the first known Greek mathematician.

Steps the source skips: the proof of Thales’ theorem

The radius \(PO\) splits \(\angle APB\) into two parts, so \(\angle APB=\angle APO+\angle BPO=\alpha+\beta\). The three angles of \(\triangle APB\) are therefore \(\alpha\) at \(A\), \(\beta\) at \(B\) and \(\alpha+\beta\) at \(P\), which is where \(\alpha+\beta+(\alpha+\beta)\) comes from.

Angles subtended by the diameter (Maths Pathway)

Every angle subtended at a point on the circle by the diameter is \(90^\circ\).

Constructing a right angle at the endpoint of an interval (AMSI, page 10)

Thales’ theorem gives a quick way to construct a right angle at the endpoint of an interval \(AX\).

  1. Choose a point \(O\) above the interval \(AX\).
  2. Draw a circle with centre \(O\) through \(A\) crossing \(AX\) again at \(P\).
  3. Draw the radius \(PO\) and produce it to a diameter \(POQ\).
  4. Join \(AQ\), then \(\angle PAQ=90^\circ\).
{{svg:c4_constr}}
Why the construction works

\(PQ\) is a diameter and \(A\) is on the circle, so \(\angle PAQ\) is an angle in a semicircle.

The converse theorem (AMSI, page 10)

The angle in a semicircle theorem has a straightforward converse that is best expressed as a property of a right-angled triangle:

Theorem

The circle whose diameter is the hypotenuse of a right-angled triangle passes through all three vertices of the triangle.

Worked proofFrom the text (AMSI, page 10): keep for homework
Proof

Let \(\triangle ABC\) be right-angled at \(C\), and let \(M\) be the midpoint of the hypotenuse \(AB\).

We need to prove that \(MC=MA=MB\). Complete the rectangle \(ACBR\). Because \(ACBR\) is a rectangle, its diagonals bisect each other and are equal. Hence \(M\) is the midpoint of the other diagonal \(CR\), and \(AM=BM=CM=RM\).

{{svg:c4_conv}}

This is an excellent example of the way ideas in geometry fit together – a significant theorem about circles has been proven using a property of rectangles.

Steps the source skips: the converse

Since \(MA=MB=MC\), the circle with centre \(M\) and radius \(MA\) passes through \(A\), \(B\) and \(C\). Its diameter is \(AB\), the hypotenuse. A useful consequence: the midpoint of the hypotenuse is the same distance from all three vertices, so \(CM=\tfrac12AB\).

Two practical situations to illustrate the converse theorem (AMSI, page 10)

A set of points in the plane is often called a locus. The term is used particularly when the set of points is the curve traced out by a moving point. For example, a circle can be defined as the locus of a point that moves so that its distance from some fixed point is constant. The two examples below use the converse of the angle in a semicircle theorem to describe a locus.
