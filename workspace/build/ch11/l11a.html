Lesson 11A

Equations involving compound angles

Use compound-angle and double-angle identities to turn an equation into one trigonometric function of one angle, then solve.
{{sec:teal:key:Concept 1:sin²θ and cos²θ in terms of cos 2θ}}
Two new forms of the cos 2θ formula

Start from the second and third forms of \(\cos 2\theta\) and make the square the subject.

\[\begin{aligned} \cos 2\theta&=2\cos^2\theta-1 &\qquad\qquad \cos 2\theta&=1-2\sin^2\theta\\ 2\cos^2\theta&=1+\cos 2\theta & 2\sin^2\theta&=1-\cos 2\theta\\ \cos^2\theta&=\tfrac12+\tfrac12\cos 2\theta & \sin^2\theta&=\tfrac12-\tfrac12\cos 2\theta \end{aligned}\]

Check: adding the two gives \(\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta=1\), as it must. These forms are used again in Chapter 12 to integrate \(\sin^2 x\) and \(\cos^2 x\).

Why this helps
A squared sine or cosine becomes a single cosine wave \(y=\tfrac12\pm\tfrac12\cos 2x\), so you can read off its amplitude, period and range.

Solution
\[y=\sin^2 x=\tfrac12-\tfrac12\cos 2x\]

This is \(y=\cos 2x\) turned upside down, stretched vertically by the factor \(\tfrac12\), then shifted up \(\tfrac12\).

Period \(\pi\)  ·  amplitude \(\tfrac12\)  ·  it oscillates about \(y=\tfrac12\), so the range is \(0\le y\le 1\).

Equations with more than one trigonometric function
  • Use trigonometric identities (ratio and Pythagorean identities) to produce an equation in only one trigonometric function.
  • Many trigonometric equations can be solved by more than one method.
The danger of squaring

Squaring both sides can introduce extra solutions. For example, \(\sin x=1\) for \(0\le x\le 2\pi\) has one solution \(x=\tfrac{\pi}{2}\), but \(\sin^2x=1\) has two solutions, \(x=\tfrac{\pi}{2}\) and \(x=\tfrac{3\pi}{2}\).

If you square, you must check every answer in the original equation.

Worked exampleKeep for homework
Solve \(2\tan\theta=\sec\theta\) for \(0^\circ\le\theta\le 360^\circ\): a using the ratio identities, b by squaring both sides.
Solution

a

\[\begin{aligned} 2\tan\theta&=\sec\theta\\ \dfrac{2\sin\theta}{\cos\theta}&=\dfrac{1}{\cos\theta}\\ \sin\theta&=\tfrac12\\ \theta&=30^\circ\ \text{or}\ 150^\circ \end{aligned}\]

b Squaring, \(4\tan^2\theta=\sec^2\theta\)

\[\begin{aligned} 4\sec^2\theta-4&=\sec^2\theta\\ \sec^2\theta&=\tfrac43\\ \cos\theta&=\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\ \text{or}\ -\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\\ \theta&=30^\circ,150^\circ,210^\circ\ \text{or}\ 330^\circ \end{aligned}\]

Checking each solution in the original equation, only \(\theta=30^\circ\) or \(150^\circ\) work. (At \(210^\circ\), \(\tan\theta>0\) but \(\sec\theta<0\).)

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 3:equations involving different angles}}
Different angles in one equation

Use compound-angle identities to change all the trigonometric functions to functions of one angle.

General approach to any trigonometric equation
  1. First, try to get all the angles the same.
  2. Then try to get all the trigonometric functions the same.

Then factor, solve each factor, and keep track of the domain of every angle that appears (if \(0\le x\le 2\pi\) then \(0\le 2x\le 4\pi\)).

Worked exampleKeep for homework
Solve \(\tan 4x=-\tan 2x\) for \(0\le x\le \dfrac{\pi}{2}\), using the \(\tan 2\theta\) formula.
Solution
\[\begin{aligned} \tan 4x&=-\tan 2x\\ \dfrac{2\tan 2x}{1-\tan^2 2x}&=-\tan 2x\\ 2\tan 2x&=-\tan 2x+\tan^3 2x\\ \tan^3 2x-3\tan 2x&=0\\ \tan 2x\,(\tan^2 2x-3)&=0\\ \tan 2x=0,\quad \tan 2x&=\sqrt3\quad\text{or}\quad \tan 2x=-\sqrt3 \end{aligned}\]

The restriction on \(2x\) is \(0\le 2x\le\pi\), so \(2x=0\) or \(\pi\), or \(2x=\tfrac{\pi}{3}\), or \(2x=\tfrac{2\pi}{3}\).

\[x=0,\ \tfrac{\pi}{6},\ \tfrac{\pi}{3}\ \text{or}\ \tfrac{\pi}{2}\]

Alternatively, substitute \(t=\tan 2x\) after the second line.

Worked exampleTwo ways to make the angles the same
Solve \(\cos 2x=4\sin^2x-14\cos^2x\) for \(0\le x\le 2\pi\): a by changing all the angles to \(x\), b by changing all the angles to \(2x\).
Solution

a Using \(\cos 2x=\cos^2x-\sin^2x\):

\[\begin{aligned} \cos^2x-\sin^2x&=4\sin^2x-14\cos^2x\\ 15\cos^2x&=5\sin^2x\\ \tan^2x&=3\\ \tan x&=\sqrt3\ \text{or}\ -\sqrt3\\ x&=\tfrac{\pi}{3},\tfrac{2\pi}{3},\tfrac{4\pi}{3}\ \text{or}\ \tfrac{5\pi}{3} \end{aligned}\]

b Using Concept 1:

\[\begin{aligned} \cos 2x&=4\left(\tfrac12-\tfrac12\cos 2x\right)-14\left(\tfrac12+\tfrac12\cos 2x\right)\\ 10\cos 2x&=-5\\ \cos 2x&=-\tfrac12\quad (0\le 2x\le 4\pi)\\ 2x&=\tfrac{2\pi}{3},\tfrac{4\pi}{3},\tfrac{8\pi}{3}\ \text{or}\ \tfrac{10\pi}{3}\\ x&=\tfrac{\pi}{3},\tfrac{2\pi}{3},\tfrac{4\pi}{3}\ \text{or}\ \tfrac{5\pi}{3} \end{aligned}\]
{{sec:teal:key:Concept 4:homogeneous equations}}
Homogeneous equations
  • An equation is homogeneous in \(\sin x\) and \(\cos x\) if the sum of the indices of \(\sin x\) and \(\cos x\) in each term is the same.
  • To solve it, divide through by a suitable power of \(\cos x\) to produce an equation in \(\tan x\) alone.

The expansions of \(\sin 2x\) and \(\cos 2x\) are homogeneous of degree 2 in \(\sin x\) and \(\cos x\). Also, \(1=\sin^2x+\cos^2x\) can be regarded as homogeneous of degree 2.

Before dividing by \(\cos^2x\), check that \(\cos x=0\) is not a solution (substitute \(x=\tfrac{\pi}{2}\)), otherwise you would lose it.

Your turnSame method
Solve \(\sin^2x+\sin x\cos x-2\cos^2x=0\) for \(0\le x\le 2\pi\), giving answers correct to 3 decimal places where necessary. {{work:50}}
Worked exampleMake it homogeneous first
Solve \(\sin 2x+\cos 2x=\sin^2x+1\) for \(0\le x\le 2\pi\).
Solution

Expand, writing \(1\) as \(\sin^2x+\cos^2x\) so that every term has degree 2:

\[\begin{aligned} 2\sin x\cos x+(\cos^2x-\sin^2x)&=\sin^2x+(\sin^2x+\cos^2x)\\ 3\sin^2x-2\sin x\cos x&=0\\ 3\tan^2x-2\tan x&=0 \qquad(\div\cos^2x)\\ \tan x\,(3\tan x-2)&=0\\ \tan x=0\quad\text{or}\quad\tan x&=\tfrac23 \end{aligned}\]

Hence \(x=0,\ \pi\) or \(2\pi\), or \(x\approx 0.588\) or \(3.730\). (\(\cos x=0\) is not a solution: at \(x=\tfrac{\pi}{2}\), LHS \(=-1\), RHS \(=2\).)
