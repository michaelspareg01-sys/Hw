Lesson 13E

Applications of differential equations

Find unknown constants when the form of the solution is known, then model real situations with the natural growth DE, Newton's law of cooling and the logistic equation. The variable is usually \(t\) for time.
{{sec:teal:key:Concept 1:when the form of the solution is known}}
Equating coefficients

People who work with DEs learn to recognise them, so they often know what the solution looks like but not the values of the constants. Substitute the given form into the DE, then equate coefficients (of \(x\), of each exponential, and the constants) to find them.

Tutor demoEquate coefficients
a Find the values of \(a\) and \(b\) so that \(y=ax+b+2e^{-x}\) is a solution of \(y'+y=4x\).   b Find \(n\) so that \(y=3\sin nx\) is a solution of \(y''=-16y\). {{work:55}}
Worked example 1Keep for homework
a Find the values of \(b\) and \(c\) so that \(y=e^{3x}+bx+c\) is a solution of \(y'=3y-18x\).   b Find \(n\) so that \(y=10\cos nx\) is a solution of the second-order DE \(y''=-49y\).
Solution

a Substituting \(y=e^{3x}+bx+c\) into the DE,

\[\text{LHS}=3e^{3x}+b\qquad\text{RHS}=3e^{3x}+(3b-18)x+3c\]

Equating coefficients of \(x\): \(3b-18=0\). Equating constants: \(3c=b\). So \(b=6\) and \(c=2\), giving \(y=e^{3x}+6x+2\).

b Substituting \(y=10\cos nx\): LHS \(=-10n^2\cos nx\), RHS \(=-490\cos nx\).

Hence \(-10n^2=-490\), so \(n=7\) or \(n=-7\), giving \(y=10\cos7x\) and \(y=10\cos(-7x)\), which are the same function because cosine is even.

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 2:natural growth in a practical situation}}
Forming and solving the DE
  1. Translate the words into a DE: "the rate of increase of \(N\) is proportional to \(N\)" means \(\dfrac{dN}{dt}=kN\).
  2. Solve the DE, remembering any constant solution and the absolute values.
  3. Use the two given data points to find the arbitrary constant and \(k\).

The same steps handle "the rate of change of temperature is proportional to the difference from the surroundings" (Newton's law of cooling, \(\dfrac{dT}{dt}=k(T-R)\)).

Worked example 2Keep for homework
The rabbits on Bandicoot Island are increasing. Fifty years ago, 100 rabbits were released, and now there are 10 000 rabbits. Assume that the rate of increase of rabbits is proportional to the number of rabbits. a Find the equation for the population \(N\) at time \(t\) years after they were introduced. b Find how many rabbits there will be in another 25 years.
Solution

a As a DE, \(\dfrac{dN}{dt}=kN\). First, \(N=0\) is trivially a solution. Otherwise \(\dfrac1N\,dN=k\,dt\), and integrating, \(\log|N|=kt+C\), so \(N=Ae^{kt}\), where \(A\) can be any real number.

When \(t=0\), \(N=100\), so \(A=100\) and \(N=100e^{kt}\). When \(t=50\), \(N=10\,000\), so \(10\,000=100e^{50k}\) and \(k=\tfrac{1}{50}\log100\).

b Substituting \(t=75\): \(kt=\tfrac{1}{50}\log100\times75=1.5\log100\), so \(N=100e^{1.5\log100}=100\times100^{1.5}=100\,000\) rabbits.

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 3:the logistic equation as a limit to growth}}
Logistic growth

Populations cannot grow exponentially forever: food is limited and there may be predators. The simplest model of a limit is the logistic equation \(\dfrac{dN}{dt}=kN(P-N)\), where \(P\) is the carrying capacity (maximum population). Its solution between \(0\) and \(P\) is

\[N=\dfrac{P}{1+Be^{-Pkt}},\]

found using the partial fractions \(\dfrac{1}{N(P-N)}=\dfrac1P\left(\dfrac1N+\dfrac{1}{P-N}\right)\). The population rises rapidly, then levels off towards \(P\).

Worked example 3Keep for homework
The carrying capacity of Bandicoot Island is 20 000 rabbits. Using the data of Worked Example 2 (100 rabbits at \(t=0\), 10 000 at \(t=50\)), model the population by \(\dfrac{dN}{dt}=kN(P-N)\) with \(P=20\,000\). Find \(N\), and the population in another 25 years.
Solution

First, \(N=0\) and \(N=P\) are trivially solutions. Otherwise, using partial fractions,

\[\begin{aligned}\left(\dfrac1N+\dfrac{1}{P-N}\right)dN&=Pk\,dt\\\log|N|-\log|P-N|&=Pkt+C\\\dfrac{N}{P-N}&=Ae^{Pkt}\quad(A\text{ positive or negative})\\N&=\dfrac{P}{1+Be^{-Pkt}}\quad(B=A^{-1})\end{aligned}\]

When \(t=0\), \(N=100\): \(100=\dfrac{20\,000}{1+B}\), so \(B=199\). When \(t=50\), \(N=10\,000\): \(1+199e^{-1\,000\,000k}=2\), so \(k=\dfrac{\log199}{1\,000\,000}\).

When \(t=75\): \(-Pkt=-1.5\log199\), so \(N=\dfrac{20\,000}{1+199e^{-1.5\log199}}\approx18\,676\) rabbits, far fewer than the 100 000 predicted by exponential growth.
