Lesson 14A

Factorial notation

Counting problems keep producing products of descending whole numbers, such as \(5\times4\times3\times2\times1\). This lesson gives these products a name and a notation, \(n!\), defines them informally and recursively, explains why \(0!=1\), and shows how unrolling a factorial simplifies quotients, sums and differences of factorials.
{{sec:teal:key:Concept 1:the definition of n factorial}}
Where factorials come from

How many ways can five people form a queue at a bus stop? Fill the places one at a time.

  • Choose the person at the head of the queue in five ways.
  • Four people remain, so the person in second place can be chosen in four ways.
  • Three people remain, so the person in third place can be chosen in three ways.
  • Two people remain, so the person in fourth place can be chosen in two ways.
  • And there is now only one way to choose the person in the last place.
{{svg:queue5}}

So the number of possible queues is \(5\times4\times3\times2\times1\). The same product appears when \(y=x^5\) is differentiated five times: \(y^{(5)}=5\times4\times3\times2\times1\). This product is written \(5!\) and is called 5 factorial:

\[5!=5\times4\times3\times2\times1\qquad\text{(which evaluates to 120).}\]
Box 1  The definition of \(n\) factorial
  • Informally, for each whole number \(n\), the number \(n!\) (\(n\) factorial) is the product of all positive whole numbers from \(n\) down to 1: \[n!=n\times(n-1)\times(n-2)\times\cdots\times3\times2\times1.\]
  • The better definition of the function \(n!\) is recursive: \[\begin{cases}0!=1,\\ n!=n\times(n-1)!,\quad\text{for all whole numbers }n\ge1.\end{cases}\]

The recursive definition avoids the dots in the first formula, it gives \(0!\) a meaning, and it gives a better insight into how to manipulate factorial notation.

Why \(0!=1\), and the first few factorials
  • An empty product is regarded as being 1. If nothing has yet been multiplied, the register remains at 1, where it was originally set in preparation for multiplication.
  • In the same way, an empty sum is 0: if nothing has yet been added, the register remains at 0.

Using the recursive definition:

\(0!=1\)
\(1!=1\times0!=1\)
\(2!=2\times1!=2\)
\(3!=3\times2!=6\)		 \(4!=4\times3!=24\)
\(5!=5\times4!=120\)
\(6!=6\times5!=720\)
\(7!=7\times6!=5040\)		 \(8!=8\times7!=40\,320\)
\(9!=9\times8!=362\,880\)
\(10!=10\times9!=3\,628\,800\)
\(11!=11\times10!=39\,916\,800\)

Factorials increase very quickly indeed. Calculators have a factorial button labelled \(\boxed{x!}\) or \(\boxed{n!}\). It confirms that \(0!=1\), and it gives an error message if \(n\) is not a whole number: the domain of the function \(n!\) is the whole numbers \(0,1,2,\ldots\)

Solution

a The first place can be filled in 5 ways, the second in 4 ways, the third in 3 ways, the fourth in 2 ways and the last in 1 way. So

\[\text{number of queues}=5\times4\times3\times2\times1=5!=120.\]

b Differentiating five times,

\[\begin{aligned}y'&=5x^4\\ y''&=5\times4\times x^3\\ y'''&=5\times4\times3\times x^2\\ y^{(4)}&=5\times4\times3\times2\times x\\ y^{(5)}&=5\times4\times3\times2\times1=5!=120.\end{aligned}\]
{{sec:teal:key:Concept 2:unrolling factorials}}
Unrolling

The recursive definition is the key idea in many calculations. Successive applications of it can be thought of as unrolling the factorial further and further:

\[\begin{aligned}8!&=8\times7! &&\text{(unrolling once)}\\ &=8\times7\times6! &&\text{(unrolling twice)}\\ &=8\times7\times6\times5! &&\text{(unrolling three times)}\end{aligned}\]

and so on. This idea is vital when there are fractions involved.

Box 2  Calculating by unrolling factorials
  • A factorial can be unrolled one step at a time: \[7!=7\times6!=7\times6\times5!=7\times6\times5\times4!=\cdots\]
  • Cancel fractions with factorials by unrolling until the factorials cancel: \[\dfrac{7!}{4!}=\dfrac{7\times6\times5\times4!}{4!}=7\times6\times5=210.\]

Unroll the larger factorial down to the smaller one. With pronumerals, \((n+2)!=(n+2)(n+1)n!\) and \(n!=n(n-1)!\).

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 3:common denominators and common factors}}
Sums and differences of factorials
  • To add or subtract fractions with factorials in the denominators, use the largest factorial as the common denominator. Unroll it to see what each numerator must be multiplied by: \[\dfrac{1}{8!}=\dfrac{10\times9}{10\times9\times8!}=\dfrac{10\times9}{10!}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\dfrac{1}{n!}=\dfrac{n+1}{(n+1)!}.\]
  • Unrolling also lets you take out a common factor from a sum or difference of factorials. Unroll each term down to the smallest factorial: \[8!-7!=8\times7!-7!=(8-1)\times7!=7\times7!.\]
