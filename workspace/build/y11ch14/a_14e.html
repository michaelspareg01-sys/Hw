PP 1Sydney Boys High School, 2020, Year 12 Term 3 Trial HSC, Mathematics Extension 1, Q6

A group of 4 people is an unordered selection, so

\[\text{number of ways}={}^{20}\mathrm{C}_{4}=\dfrac{20\times19\times18\times17}{4\times3\times2\times1}=4845.\]

Answer A. Option C, \(116\,280={}^{20}\mathrm{P}_{4}\), counts ordered selections, so it counts each group \(4!=24\) times.

PP 2Sydney Grammar School, 2020, Trial Examination, Form VI Mathematics Extension 1, Q6

‘At least one of each sex’ is a warning, so consider the complement. With no restriction there are \({}^{10}\mathrm{C}_{3}=120\) committees. The committees that break the condition are all male, \({}^{5}\mathrm{C}_{3}=10\), or all female, \({}^{5}\mathrm{C}_{3}=10\). Hence

\[\text{number of committees}=120-10-10=100.\]

Check by cases: two men and one woman give \({}^{5}\mathrm{C}_{2}\times{}^{5}\mathrm{C}_{1}=50\), and one man and two women give another 50.

Answer (B).

PP 3Moriah College, 2023, Trial Higher School Certificate Examination, Mathematics Extension 1, Q3

The team has 5 members with at least two from each year, so it has either 2 from Year 11 and 3 from Year 12, or 3 from Year 11 and 2 from Year 12.

\[\begin{aligned}\text{number of teams}&={}^{10}\mathrm{C}_{2}\times{}^{15}\mathrm{C}_{3}+{}^{10}\mathrm{C}_{3}\times{}^{15}\mathrm{C}_{2}\\ &=45\times455+120\times105\\ &=33\,075.\end{aligned}\]

Answer C.

PP 4Killara High School, 2024, Year 12 Examination, Mathematics Extension 1 HSC Trial Examination, Q6

The captain and the vice captain have different roles, so choosing them is an ordered selection: the captain in \(n\) ways, then the vice captain in \(n-1\) ways. The other \(r-2\) members of the team have no special roles, so they are an unordered selection from the \(n-2\) students who are left: \(\binom{n-2}{r-2}\) ways. Hence

\[\text{number of ways}=n(n-1)\dbinom{n-2}{r-2}.\]

Answer D. Another correct method chooses the team first, in \(\binom{n}{r}\) ways, and then its captain and vice captain in \(r(r-1)\) ways. This gives \(r(r-1)\binom{n}{r}\). That form is not printed as an option, but it is equal to option D. Option C uses \(\binom{n-2}{r}\), which chooses too many other members.

With no restriction there are \({}^{10}\mathrm{C}_{5}=252\) committees. The committees that break the condition contain both Peter and May. For these, the other 3 members are chosen from the other 8 candidates in \({}^{8}\mathrm{C}_{3}=56\) ways. Hence

\[\text{number of committees}={}^{10}\mathrm{C}_{5}-{}^{8}\mathrm{C}_{3}=252-56=196.\]

PP 9Normanhurst Boys High School, 2023, Mathematics Extension 1, Year 12 Course Assessment Task 4 (Trial Examination), Q11(e)(i)(ii)

(i) Choosing 6 of the 12 people for one group, in \({}^{12}\mathrm{C}_{6}=924\) ways, leaves the other 6 as the second group. But the two groups have no names, so each division has been counted twice: once for each group being the one chosen first. Hence

\[\text{number of ways}=\dfrac{{}^{12}\mathrm{C}_{6}}{2}=\dfrac{924}{2}=462.\]

(ii) Put the two particular people in a group, and choose its other 4 members from the other 10 people. The rest form the second group. The group with the two particular people is now different from the other group, so nothing is counted twice:

\[\text{number of ways}={}^{10}\mathrm{C}_{4}=210.\]
PP 10Knox Grammar School, 2023, Higher School Certificate Trial Examination, Mathematics Extension 1, Q12(d)

The number of groups of three is \({}^{n}\mathrm{C}_{3}\) and the number of groups of two is \({}^{n}\mathrm{C}_{2}\), where \(n\ge3\). We are told that

\[\begin{aligned}{}^{n}\mathrm{C}_{3}&=3\times{}^{n}\mathrm{C}_{2}\\ \dfrac{n(n-1)(n-2)}{6}&=3\times\dfrac{n(n-1)}{2}.\end{aligned}\]

Since \(n\ge3\), \(n(n-1)\ne0\), so we may divide both sides by \(n(n-1)\):

\[\dfrac{n-2}{6}=\dfrac{3}{2},\qquad n-2=9,\qquad n=11.\]

There are 11 dancers. Check: \({}^{11}\mathrm{C}_{3}=165=3\times55=3\times{}^{11}\mathrm{C}_{2}\).

PP 11Abbotsleigh, 2024, Higher School Certificate Assessment 4 Trial Examination, Mathematics Extension 1, Q12(c)

Choose the 4 shaded tiles of the top row: \({}^{10}\mathrm{C}_{4}=210\) ways.

Choose which 2 of these 4 are above shaded tiles: \({}^{4}\mathrm{C}_{2}=6\) ways. The bottom tiles under these 2 are shaded. The bottom tiles under the other 2 top shaded tiles must not be shaded.

That leaves 4 more bottom tiles to shade, from the 6 bottom tiles that are not under a shaded top tile: \({}^{6}\mathrm{C}_{4}=15\) ways. Hence

\[\text{number of ways}={}^{10}\mathrm{C}_{4}\times{}^{4}\mathrm{C}_{2}\times{}^{6}\mathrm{C}_{4}=210\times6\times15=18\,900.\]
