PP 1Abbotsleigh, 2020, Higher School Certificate Assessment 4 Trial Examination, Mathematics Extension 1, Q1

The three girls form one group, and each of the 5 boys is a group on his own, so there are 6 groups. Order the 6 groups, then order the girls within their group (Box 8):

\[\text{number of ways}=6!\times3!=4320.\]

Answer C. Option A, \(40\,320=8!\), ignores the restriction. Option B, \(720=6!\), forgets to order the girls within their group.

PP 2James Ruse Agricultural High School, 2020, Year 12 Trial HSC Examination, Mathematics Extension 1, Q3

The seven letters O, L, Y, M, P, I, C are all different. Glue the C and the L together as one group. Then there are 6 groups (CL, O, Y, M, P, I), which can be ordered in \(6!\) ways, and the C and L can be ordered within their group in \(2!\) ways:

\[\text{number of arrangements}=6!\times2!=1440.\]

Answer (D). Option (B), \(720=6!\), forgets that the pair can be CL or LC.

PP 3Ascham School, 2023, Year 12 Trial Examination, Mathematics Extension 1, Q12(e)(ii)

The seven letters of EPSILON are all different, and the vowels are E, I and O. Glue the three vowels together as one group, order the 5 groups (the vowels, P, S, L, N) in \(5!\) ways, then order the vowels within their group in \(3!\) ways:

\[\text{number of arrangements}=5!\times3!=720.\]
PP 4Abbotsleigh, 2023, Higher School Certificate Assessment 4 Trial Examination, Mathematics Extension 1, Q9

Deal with the boys first: seat them in a row in \(3!\) ways. This leaves 4 gaps where a girl can sit, one at each end and one between each pair of boys (each \(\square\) below is a gap):

\[\square\quad\text{B}\quad\square\quad\text{B}\quad\square\quad\text{B}\quad\square\]

The girls sit apart exactly when no two girls are in the same gap. The first girl chooses a gap in 4 ways, the second in 3 ways and the third in 2 ways:

\[\text{number of ways}=3!\times4\times3\times2=144.\]

Answer (C). Option (A), \(72=2\times3!\times3!\), counts only the seatings in which the boys and girls alternate (GBGBGB and BGBGBG). It misses seatings such as GBBGBG.

PP 5Caringbah High School, 2020, Higher School Certificate Trial Examination, Mathematics Extension 1, Q10

The dice are different colours, so an outcome is an ordered triple of scores, and there are \(|S|=6^3=216\) equally likely outcomes. The only perfect squares among the scores are 1 and 4. Use cases based on how many of the three scores are equal. The cases do not overlap.

  • All three scores equal, \(a,a,a\): the product \(a^3\) is a square only for \(a=1\) and \(a=4\) (\(4^3=64=8^2\)). This gives 2 outcomes.
  • Exactly two scores equal, \(a,a,b\) with \(b\ne a\): the product \(a^2b\) is a square exactly when \(b\) is a square, so \(b=1\) or \(b=4\). For each \(b\) there are 5 choices of \(a\), and the odd score \(b\) can be on any of the 3 dice. This gives \(2\times5\times3=30\) outcomes.
  • All three scores different: the only such set of scores is \(\{2,3,6\}\), with product \(36=6^2\). (A single 5 makes the product a multiple of 5 but not of 25, so the scores come from 1, 2, 3, 4 and 6. Since 1 and 4 are squares, the product of the scores taken from 2, 3 and 6 must be a square. Three different scores cannot all come from 1 and 4, and of the products \(2,\ 3,\ 6,\ 2\times3,\ 2\times6,\ 3\times6,\ 2\times3\times6\) only \(2\times3\times6=36\) is a square.) The three scores can be placed on the dice in \(3!=6\) ways.
\[P(\text{perfect square})=\dfrac{2+30+6}{216}=\dfrac{38}{216}.\]

Answer (D).

PP 6North Sydney Girls High School, 2020, HSC Trial Examination, Mathematics Extension 1, Q11(d)

Without restriction the seven people can board in \(7!\) orders. Count the unacceptable orders, in which Melania gets on immediately following Donald. Glue Donald and Melania together, in the order DM only, so there are 6 groups to order, in \(6!\) ways. Subtract:

\[\begin{aligned}\text{number of ways}&=7!-6!\\ &=4320.\end{aligned}\]

Note that the pair is not multiplied by \(2!\) here: “Melania immediately following Donald” allows only the order DM.

PP 7Pymble Ladies’ College, 2024, HSC Trial Examination Term 3, Mathematics Extension 1, Q11(b)

Without restriction the 8 exams can be scheduled in \(8!\) ways. Count the complementary situation, where Extension 1 and Extension 2 are consecutive: glue them together, order the 7 groups in \(7!\) ways, and order the two exams within their group in \(2!\) ways. Subtract:

\[\begin{aligned}\text{number of ways}&=8!-2\times7!\\ &=30\,240.\end{aligned}\]
PP 8Blacktown Boys’ High School, 2023, Year 12 Trial Examination, Mathematics Extension 1, Q12(c)(i)(ii)

Think of the teacher choosing the four batteries one at a time: the 1st and 2nd go in the projector remote, and the 3rd and 4th go in the air-conditioner remote. The batteries are all different, so the number of equally likely ordered selections is

\[|S|=11\times10\times9\times8=7920.\]

This ordered model gives the same probabilities as unordered selection: each choice of an unordered pair for each remote matches exactly \(2!\times2!=4\) ordered selections, so each count of selections of all four batteries (including the total) is 4 times the unordered count, and the 4 cancels.

(i) Both remotes work when all four batteries are good. There are \(7\times6\times5\times4=840\) such selections, so

\[P(\text{both work})=\dfrac{840}{7920}=\dfrac{7}{66}.\]

(ii) The projector remote works when its two batteries are good: \(7\times6=42\) ways. Then 9 batteries are left, of which 5 are good. The air-conditioner remote does not work, so count the complementary situation: its two batteries can be chosen in \(9\times8=72\) ways, of which \(5\times4=20\) are both good. So there are \(72-20=52\) ways for it to fail. Hence

\[\begin{aligned}P(\text{only the projector works})&=\dfrac{42\times52}{7920}\\ &=\dfrac{2184}{7920}\\ &=\dfrac{91}{330}.\end{aligned}\]
