Past-Paper Homework - Worked Solutions
PP 1Normanhurst Boys High School, 2023, Mathematics Extension 1, Year 12 Course Assessment Task 4 (Trial Examination), Q3

A number with three digits or fewer is at most 432, so only four-digit numbers can be greater than 2100. Deal with the first digit first.

  • First digit 3 or 4: every such number is greater than 2100. There are \(2\times3\times2\times1=12\) of them.
  • First digit 2: the other digits are 1, 3 and 4 in some order, and the smallest of these numbers is 2134. So all \(3!=6\) of them are greater than 2100.
  • First digit 1: the number is less than 2000.
\[\text{number of numbers}=12+6=18.\]

Answer (B). Option (C), \(24=4!\), counts every four-digit number, including the six that begin with 1.

PP 2Moriah College, 2023, Trial Higher School Certificate Examination, Mathematics Extension 1, Q10(b)(ii)

The 8 people can stand in a line in \(8!\) ways. Swapping Lily and Ben turns each arrangement with Lily on the left of Ben into exactly one arrangement with Ben on the left of Lily, and back again. So exactly half of the arrangements have Lily somewhere to the left of Ben:

\[\text{number of ways}=\dfrac{8!}{2}=20\,160.\]
PP 3Gosford High School, 2023, Trial HSC examination, Mathematics Extension 1, Q5

Selecting 5 of the 6 people is the same as choosing the one person who is left out, so there are 6 selections. For each selection, deal with the restriction first: the oldest of the five goes at the left or the right end (2 ways), and the second oldest takes the other end (1 way). The other three people fill the middle three places in \(3!\) ways.

\[\text{number of ways}=6\times2\times1\times3!=72.\]

Answer C. Option A, \(720={}^{6}\mathrm{P}_{5}\), ignores the restriction on the ends.

PP 4Hurlstone Agricultural High School, 2021, Higher School Certificate Assessment Task 4, Mathematics Extension 1, Q11(b)

The eight letters C, O, N, S, I, D, E, R are all different, so there are \(|S|=8!\) equally likely arrangements. In the arrangements that begin with SI, the first two places are fixed and the other 6 letters fill the last 6 places, so \(|E|=6!\). Hence

\[P(\text{begins with SI})=\dfrac{6!}{8!}=\dfrac{1}{8\times7}=\dfrac{1}{56}.\]
PP 5Girraween High School, 2020, Trial Higher School Certificate Examination, Mathematics Extension 1, Q14(c)

The numbers may have from one to five digits. A number with three digits or fewer is less than 1000, so it is not counted.

Four-digit numbers. Deal with the first digit first: it must be 7 or 8 (2 ways). The other three places are then filled from the four digits left:

\[\text{four-digit numbers}=2\times4\times3\times2=48.\]

Five-digit numbers. Every five-digit number is greater than 6000, so all of them are counted:

\[\text{five-digit numbers}=5!=120.\]

Hence the number of numbers greater than 6000 is \(48+120=168\).

PP 6Ascham School, 2023, Year 12 Trial Examination, Mathematics Extension 1, Q12(e)(iii)

The seven letters of EPSILON are all different, so there are \(7!\) arrangements. Swapping N and L pairs each arrangement with N to the left of L with exactly one arrangement with L to the left of N. So half of the arrangements are counted:

\[\text{number of arrangements}=\dfrac{7!}{2}=2520.\]
PP 7Killara High School, 2024, Year 12 Examination, Mathematics Extension 1 HSC Trial Examination, Q12(d)

Let each of the three equal ratios be \(k\). Then \(a=\dfrac{{}^{n}P_{r-1}}{k}\), \(b=\dfrac{{}^{n}P_{r}}{k}\) and \(c=\dfrac{{}^{n}P_{r+1}}{k}\). Using \({}^{n}P_{r}=\dfrac{n!}{(n-r)!}\) and unrolling,

\[\begin{aligned}\dfrac{b}{a}&=\dfrac{{}^{n}P_{r}}{{}^{n}P_{r-1}}=\dfrac{n!}{(n-r)!}\times\dfrac{(n-r+1)!}{n!}=\dfrac{(n-r+1)!}{(n-r)!}=n-r+1,\\ \dfrac{c}{b}&=\dfrac{{}^{n}P_{r+1}}{{}^{n}P_{r}}=\dfrac{n!}{(n-r-1)!}\times\dfrac{(n-r)!}{n!}=\dfrac{(n-r)!}{(n-r-1)!}=n-r.\end{aligned}\]

Hence \(\dfrac{b}{a}=\dfrac{c}{b}+1\), that is,

\[\begin{aligned}\dfrac{b}{a}&=\dfrac{b+c}{b}\\ b^2&=a(b+c).\end{aligned}\]
PP 8North Sydney Girls High School, 2020, HSC Trial Examination, Mathematics Extension 1, Q13(c)(i)(ii)

The first digit is 1. Exactly one pair of identical digits means that one digit appears exactly twice and the other two digits are different from it and from each other. The digit 0 is allowed in the last three places.

(i) The repeated digit is 1, so there is one more 1. Deal with it first: it goes in one of the last 3 places (3 ways). The two remaining places hold two different digits other than 1, chosen in order from the 9 digits 0, 2, 3, …, 9:

\[\text{number of numbers}=3\times9\times8=216.\]

(ii) If the repeated digit is not 1, then the 1 appears only at the front. The last three places hold one digit once and another digit twice, and neither of them is 1. Place the digit that appears once first: its position (3 ways) and its value (9 ways, any digit except 1). The repeated digit fills the other two places, and it is not 1 and not the digit just placed (8 ways). So there are \(3\times9\times8=216\) numbers in this case. Adding the case from part (i),

\[\text{total}=216+216=432.\]
