Lesson 14C

Ordered selections and grouping

Lesson 14B gave one important counting principle: deal with the difficulties first. This lesson adds three more principles that are often useful in counting: grouping (order the groups, then order the individuals within each group), the complementary situation (count what is not wanted and subtract), and using cases (split the count into cases that do not overlap, or subtract the overlap).
{{sec:teal:key:Concept 1:grouping}}
A general counting principle: grouping

In some ordering problems, particular members must be grouped together. This produces a compound ordering, in which the various groups must first be ordered, and then the individuals ordered within each group.

For example, in Worked example 9 below, four boys and four girls form a queue. A couple (one girl and one boy) stand together, and the other three girls stand together. So the queue is made of five groups:

{{svg:groups9}}

The five groups can be ordered in \(5!\) ways. Then the couple can be ordered within their group in \(2!\) ways, and the three girls within their group in \(3!\) ways. A boy standing on his own is a group with only one member.

Box 8  Grouping
  • First order the groups, then order the individuals within each group.
  • A group may sometimes consist of a single individual.

Four boys and four girls form a queue at the bus stop. There is one couple who want to stand together, the other three girls want to stand together, but the other three boys don’t care where they stand. How many acceptable ways are there of forming the queue?
Solution

There are five groups: the couple, the group of three girls, and the three groups each consisting of one individual boy. First order the five groups, then order the couple, and the three girls.

order the 5 groupsorder the coupleorder the 3 girls
\(5!\)\(2!\)\(3!\)
\[\text{Hence number of ways}=5!\times2!\times3!=1440.\]
{{sec:teal:key:Concept 2:deal with the complementary situation}}
A general counting principle: the complementary situation

Many probability questions have already been solved using complementary events (see the revision page). The same principle applies to counting: count the unacceptable orderings, then subtract them from the total number of orderings. The word ‘not’ may or may not be there to prompt you.

A common use is with members that must be apart. Counting the orderings in which they are apart is hard, but counting the orderings in which they are together is easy, by grouping (Box 8). So subtract the ‘together’ orderings from the total.

Worked example 10Keep for homework
How many seven-letter words can be formed from the letters A, B, C, D, E, F, G if the vowels must be separated by at least one consonant?
Solution
\[\begin{aligned}\text{Number of orderings without restriction}&=7!\\ \text{Number of orderings with A and E together}&=2!\times6!\end{aligned}\]

(Order the A and E and glue them together, then order the 6 groups.)

\[\begin{aligned}\text{Hence number of orderings with A and E apart}&=7!-2\times6!\\ &=3600.\end{aligned}\]
{{sec:teal:key:Concept 3:using cases}}
A general counting principle: using cases

Sometimes the fiddly conditions of the problem mean that one set of boxes is not enough to solve it, and separate cases need to be considered. In this situation, the cases should not overlap, or if they do, the number in the overlap needs to be subtracted.

Box 9  Using cases in counting
  • The cases should not overlap.
  • If the cases do overlap, the overlap needs to be subtracted.
The same principle with sets

This is the same principle as the counting form of the addition rule on the revision page:

\[|A\cup B|=|A|+|B|-|A\cap B|.\]

When the two cases \(A\) and \(B\) do not overlap, \(|A\cap B|=0\) and the two counts are simply added.

Worked example 11Keep for homework
a How many whole numbers less than 1000 are odd and greater than 500, or multiples of 5 and less than 200?
b How many whole numbers less than 1000 are odd and greater than 200, or multiples of 5 and less than 500?
Solution

a There are 250 odd numbers between 500 and 1000, and there are 40 multiples of 5 less than 200 (including zero). The two cases do not overlap, so the total number is 290.

b There are 400 odd numbers between 200 and 1000, and there are 100 multiples of 5 less than 500 (including zero). But the two cases overlap: from 200 to 500 there are 30 odd numbers that are multiples of 5. Hence

\[\text{Total}=400+100-30=470.\]
