Probability basics

A short review of Advanced Year 11 probability (Chapter 12): sample spaces, equally likely outcomes, complements, the words “and”, “or” and “not”, and the product rule. Lesson 14F uses all of these, and the counting methods in 14A–14E are what let us find the sizes of the sample space and the event.
{{sec:violet:loop:Summary:keep this page open while you work}}
Sample spaces and equally likely outcomes (12A)
  • A sample space \(S\) is the set of possible outcomes of an experiment, where one and only one outcome occurs. It is uniform when all outcomes are equally likely (a fair die, a fair coin, a card chosen at random).
  • An event \(E\) is a subset of \(S\). Its outcomes are called favourable, and the set of favourable outcomes is the event space.
\[P(E)=\dfrac{|E|}{|S|}=\dfrac{\text{number of favourable outcomes}}{\text{number of possible outcomes}}\]

A standard pack has 52 cards in four suits (clubs ♣ and spades ♠ are black; diamonds ♦ and hearts ♥ are red). Each suit has 13 cards: A, 2, 3, …, 10, J, Q, K. The 12 jacks, queens and kings are the court cards.

Complementary events: the word “not” (12A)

The complement \(\overline E\) is the event “\(E\) does not occur”, and \(P(\overline E)=1-P(E)\). Consider the complement whenever a question says not or at least one: “at least one” is the complement of “none”.

Sets, “and” and “or” (12C, 12D)

“\(A\) and \(B\)” is the intersection \(A\cap B\). “\(A\) or \(B\)” is the union \(A\cup B\), and “or” always means “and/or”. For any two events, the addition rule is \[P(A\text{ or }B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\text{ and }B).\]

  • It comes from counting sets: \(|A\cup B|=|A|+|B|-|A\cap B|\).
  • Events are mutually exclusive (disjoint) when they cannot both occur. Then \(P(A\text{ or }B)=P(A)+P(B)\).
Multi-stage experiments and the product rule (12E)
  • If \(A\) and \(B\) are events in successive independent stages, then \(P(AB)=P(A)\times P(B)\). Independent means that one stage does not affect the probabilities of the other, as with repeated tosses of a coin.
  • For a selection without replacement the stages are not independent: after each draw the numbers left in the bag change, so use the new totals for the next stage.
  • The same idea counts outcomes. If one stage can be done in \(m\) ways and the next in \(n\) ways, the two stages can be done in \(m\times n\) ways. This multiplication principle is the start of Chapter 14.
