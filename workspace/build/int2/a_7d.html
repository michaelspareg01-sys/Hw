PP 1Sydney Grammar School, 2020, Trial Examination, Form VI Mathematics Extension 1, Q7

Write \(\tan x=\dfrac{\sin x}{\cos x}\). With \(u=\cos x\), \(u'=-\sin x\), so the top becomes the derivative of the bottom after balancing with \(-1\):

\[\begin{aligned}\int\tan x\,dx&=-\int\dfrac{-\sin x}{\cos x}\,dx\\&=-\ln|\cos x|+c.\end{aligned}\]

Write the constant as \(c=-\ln A\), where \(A\gt0\). Then \(-\ln|\cos x|-\ln A=-\big(\ln|\cos x|+\ln A\big)=-\ln|A\cos x|\).

The answer is (D). Option (A) is wrong because \(\sec^2x\) is the derivative of \(\tan x\), not a primitive. Option (B) is a primitive of \(\cot x\). Option (C) is the derivative of \(\tan^{-1}x\), and has nothing to do with \(\tan x\).

PP 2Caringbah High School, 2021, Year 12 Mathematics Extension 1 HSC Course Assessment Task 4, Q6

Let \(u=\tan\theta\). Then \(\dfrac{du}{d\theta}=\sec^2\theta\), and with \(n=2\), \(\displaystyle\int u^2\,\dfrac{du}{d\theta}\,d\theta=\dfrac{u^3}{3}\). So

\[\begin{aligned}\int\sec^2\theta\tan^2\theta\,d\theta&=\int(\tan\theta)^2\sec^2\theta\,d\theta\\&=\tfrac13\tan^3\theta+C.\end{aligned}\]

Check: \(\dfrac{d}{d\theta}\big(\tfrac13\tan^3\theta\big)=\tfrac13\times3\tan^2\theta\times\sec^2\theta=\sec^2\theta\tan^2\theta\). The answer is (B). Differentiating (C) gives the extra term \(\tan^4\theta\sec^2\theta\), and (A) and (D) do not differentiate back to the integrand either.

PP 3Cherrybrook Technology High School, 2020, Year 12 AP4, Mathematics Extension 1, Q5

Let \(u=\cos x\). Then \(u'=-\sin x\), and with \(n=3\), \(\displaystyle\int u^3\,\dfrac{du}{dx}\,dx=\dfrac{u^4}{4}\). So

\[\begin{aligned}y&=\int\sin x\cos^3x\,dx\\&=-\int(-\sin x)\cos^3x\,dx\\&=-\tfrac14\cos^4x+C.\end{aligned}\]

Check: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\big(-\tfrac14\cos^4x\big)=-\tfrac14\times4\cos^3x\times(-\sin x)=\sin x\cos^3x\). The answer is B. Option A has the wrong sign, and C and D do not differentiate back to \(\sin x\cos^3x\).

PP 5Fort Street High School, 2020, Trial Higher School Certificate Examination, Mathematics Extension 1, Q12(e)

Integrate \(y''=-\sec^2(2x)\), using \(\displaystyle\int\sec^2(ax+b)\,dx=\dfrac1a\tan(ax+b)+C\) with \(a=2\):

\[y'=-\tfrac12\tan2x+C.\]

The gradient is \(-1\) at \(x=\dfrac{\pi}{8}\), and \(2\times\dfrac{\pi}{8}=\dfrac{\pi}{4}\), so

\[\begin{aligned}-1&=-\tfrac12\tan\tfrac{\pi}{4}+C\\-1&=-\tfrac12\times1+C\\C&=-\tfrac12.\end{aligned}\]

Hence \(y'=-\tfrac12\tan2x-\tfrac12\). At \(x=\dfrac{\pi}{12}\), \(2\times\dfrac{\pi}{12}=\dfrac{\pi}{6}\), so

\[\begin{aligned}y'&=-\tfrac12\tan\tfrac{\pi}{6}-\tfrac12\\&=-\tfrac12\times\tfrac{1}{\sqrt3}-\tfrac12\\&=-\tfrac{\sqrt3}{6}-\tfrac36\\&=-\dfrac{3+\sqrt3}{6}.\end{aligned}\]

The exact gradient at \(x=\dfrac{\pi}{12}\) is \(-\dfrac{3+\sqrt3}{6}\). (The \(y\)-value \(2\) at the point is not needed for the gradient. It would be needed to find \(y\) itself.)

PP 6Hornsby Girls High School, 2022, Mathematics Extension 1, Year 12 Higher School Certificate Trial Examination Term 3, Q14(a)

Write \(\cot x=\dfrac{\cos x}{\sin x}\). The top is the derivative of the bottom, so

\[\begin{aligned}f(x)&=\int\Big(\dfrac{\cos x}{\sin x}+x\Big)\,dx\\&=\ln|\sin x|+\dfrac{x^2}{2}+C.\end{aligned}\]

Substituting \(f\Big(\dfrac{\pi}{2}\Big)=0\),

\[\begin{aligned}\ln\Big|\sin\tfrac{\pi}{2}\Big|+\dfrac{\big(\frac{\pi}{2}\big)^2}{2}+C&=0\\\ln1+\dfrac{\pi^2}{8}+C&=0\\C&=-\dfrac{\pi^2}{8},\end{aligned}\]

because \(\sin\frac{\pi}{2}=1\), \(\ln1=0\) and \(\big(\frac{\pi}{2}\big)^2\div2=\frac{\pi^2}{4}\div2=\frac{\pi^2}{8}\). Hence

\[f(x)=\ln|\sin x|+\dfrac{x^2}{2}-\dfrac{\pi^2}{8}.\]
PP 7Abbotsleigh, 2023, Higher School Certificate Assessment 4 Trial Examination, Mathematics Extension 1, Q11(d)(ii)

A primitive of \(2\sqrt3\cos x-2\sin x\) is \(2\sqrt3\sin x+2\cos x\), so

\[\begin{aligned}\int_0^k\big(2\sqrt3\cos x-2\sin x\big)\,dx&=\Big[2\sqrt3\sin x+2\cos x\Big]_0^k\\&=\big(2\sqrt3\sin k+2\cos k\big)-\big(2\sqrt3\sin0+2\cos0\big)\\&=2\sqrt3\sin k+2\cos k-2.\end{aligned}\]

Setting this equal to \(2\),

\[\begin{aligned}2\sqrt3\sin k+2\cos k-2&=2\\2\sqrt3\sin k+2\cos k&=4\\4\sin\big(k+\tfrac{\pi}{6}\big)&=4\qquad\text{(using part (i))}\\\sin\big(k+\tfrac{\pi}{6}\big)&=1.\end{aligned}\]

Since \(0\le k\le2\pi\), the angle \(k+\frac{\pi}{6}\) lies between \(\frac{\pi}{6}\) and \(\frac{13\pi}{6}\). In this interval, \(\sin\theta=1\) only at \(\theta=\frac{\pi}{2}\) (the next solution, \(\frac{5\pi}{2}\), is too large). So

\[k+\tfrac{\pi}{6}=\tfrac{\pi}{2},\qquad k=\tfrac{\pi}{2}-\tfrac{\pi}{6}=\tfrac{\pi}{3}.\]
PP 8Killara High School, 2020, Mathematics Extension 1 Trial HSC Examination, Q12(d)(ii)

By part (i), \(\operatorname{cosec}x+\cot x=\cot\dfrac{x}{2}=\dfrac{\cos\frac{x}{2}}{\sin\frac{x}{2}}\). Let \(u=\sin\dfrac{x}{2}\). Then \(u'=\dfrac12\cos\dfrac{x}{2}\), so balance with \(2\) outside:

\[\begin{aligned}\int_{\frac{\pi}{3}}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}(\operatorname{cosec}x+\cot x)\,dx&=\int_{\frac{\pi}{3}}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}\cot\tfrac{x}{2}\,dx\\&=2\int_{\frac{\pi}{3}}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}\dfrac{\frac12\cos\frac{x}{2}}{\sin\frac{x}{2}}\,dx\\&=2\Big[\ln\Big|\sin\tfrac{x}{2}\Big|\Big]_{\frac{\pi}{3}}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}\\&=2\Big(\ln\sin\tfrac{\pi}{4}-\ln\sin\tfrac{\pi}{6}\Big)\\&=2\Big(\ln\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}-\ln\tfrac12\Big)\\&=2\ln\Big(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\div\tfrac12\Big)\\&=2\ln\tfrac{2}{\sqrt2}\\&=2\ln\sqrt2\\&=\ln2.\end{aligned}\]

Here \(\frac{2}{\sqrt2}=\sqrt2\) and \(2\ln\sqrt2=\ln(\sqrt2)^2=\ln2\). The interval \(\frac{\pi}{3}\le x\le\frac{\pi}{2}\) does not contain a zero of \(\sin\frac{x}{2}\), so no asymptote is crossed.
