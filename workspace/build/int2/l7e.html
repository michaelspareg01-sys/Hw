Lesson 7E

Applications of integration

The trigonometric integrals from Lesson 7D can now be used to find areas in the usual way. We find the area between a curve such as \(y=\cos\tfrac12x\) and the \(x\)-axis, where the integral is split at every \(x\)-intercept, and the area between two curves, such as \(y=\sin x\) and \(y=\sin2x\), using the top curve minus the bottom curve.

Applications of integration

The trigonometric integrals can now be used to find areas in the usual way.

Finding areas by integration

As always, a sketch is essential, because areas below the \(x\)-axis are represented as a negative number by the definite integral.

It is best to evaluate the separate integrals first and then make a conclusion about areas.

The standard forms from Lesson 7D
  • \(\displaystyle\int\cos(ax+b)\,dx=\dfrac1a\sin(ax+b)+C\)
  • \(\displaystyle\int\sin(ax+b)\,dx=-\dfrac1a\cos(ax+b)+C\)
  • \(\displaystyle\int\sec^2(ax+b)\,dx=\dfrac1a\tan(ax+b)+C\)

For example, \(\displaystyle\int\cos\tfrac12x\,dx=\dfrac{1}{\frac12}\sin\tfrac12x+C=2\sin\tfrac12x+C\), because the reciprocal of \(\tfrac12\) is \(2\).

Sketching \(y=\cos nx\) and \(y=\sin nx\) (Year 11)

The curves \(y=\cos nx\) and \(y=\sin nx\), where \(n\gt0\), have amplitude \(1\) and period \(2\pi\div n\). The curve \(y=\cos nx\) crosses the \(x\)-axis where \(nx=\dfrac{\pi}{2},\ \dfrac{3\pi}{2},\ \dfrac{5\pi}{2},\ \dots\), and the curve \(y=\sin nx\) crosses it where \(nx=0,\ \pi,\ 2\pi,\ \dots\). The curve is above the \(x\)-axis and below it in turn between these intercepts.

The steps for finding an area
  • Sketch the curve in the given interval, and find every \(x\)-intercept inside it.
  • Evaluate a separate integral over each piece between the intercepts.
  • A piece below the \(x\)-axis gives a negative integral. Its area is the opposite of that integral.
  • Add the areas of the pieces, and write the conclusion in square units.

Worked example 24Keep for homework
a Sketch \(y=\cos\tfrac12x\) in the interval \(0\le x\le4\pi\), marking both \(x\)-intercepts.
b Hence find the area between the curve and the \(x\)-axis, for \(0\le x\le4\pi\).
Solution

a The curve \(y=\cos\tfrac12x\) has amplitude 1, and the period is \(2\pi\div\tfrac12=4\pi\).
The two \(x\)-intercepts in the interval are \(x=\pi\) and \(x=3\pi\).

b We must integrate separately over the three intervals \([0,\pi]\) and \([\pi,3\pi]\) and \([3\pi,4\pi]\).

{{svg:c7e_ex24}}

First,

\[\begin{aligned}\int_0^\pi\cos\tfrac12x\,dx&=\Big[2\sin\tfrac12x\Big]_0^\pi\\&=2\sin\tfrac{\pi}{2}-2\sin0\\&=2-0\\&=2,\end{aligned}\]

which is positive, because the curve is above the \(x\)-axis for \(0\le x\le\pi\).

Secondly,

\[\begin{aligned}\int_\pi^{3\pi}\cos\tfrac12x\,dx&=\Big[2\sin\tfrac12x\Big]_\pi^{3\pi}\\&=2\sin\tfrac{3\pi}{2}-2\sin\tfrac{\pi}{2}\\&=-2-2\\&=-4,\end{aligned}\]

which is negative, because the curve is below the \(x\)-axis for \(\pi\le x\le3\pi\).

Thirdly,

\[\begin{aligned}\int_{3\pi}^{4\pi}\cos\tfrac12x\,dx&=\Big[2\sin\tfrac12x\Big]_{3\pi}^{4\pi}\\&=2\sin2\pi-2\sin\tfrac{3\pi}{2}\\&=0-(-2)\\&=2,\end{aligned}\]

which is positive, because the curve is above the \(x\)-axis for \(3\pi\le x\le4\pi\).

Hence

\[\begin{aligned}\text{total area}&=2+4+2\\&=8\text{ square units.}\end{aligned}\]
Reading Worked example 24, line by line

In part a, the curve crosses the \(x\)-axis where \(\tfrac12x=\dfrac{\pi}{2}\) or \(\tfrac12x=\dfrac{3\pi}{2}\), that is, where \(x=\pi\) or \(x=3\pi\). In part b, the primitive \(2\sin\tfrac12x\) comes from the first standard form with \(a=\tfrac12\). At the limits, \(\tfrac12\times\pi=\dfrac{\pi}{2}\), \(\tfrac12\times3\pi=\dfrac{3\pi}{2}\) and \(\tfrac12\times4\pi=2\pi\). The values are \(\sin0=0\), \(\sin\dfrac{\pi}{2}=1\), \(\sin\dfrac{3\pi}{2}=-1\) and \(\sin2\pi=0\). The middle integral is \(-4\), so the middle piece has area \(4\), and the total area is \(2+4+2=8\).

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 2:areas between curves}}
Finding areas between curves

The next worked example uses the principle that if \(y=f(x)\) is above \(y=g(x)\) throughout some interval \(a\le x\le b\), then the area between the curves is given by the formula

\[\text{area between the curves}=\int_a^b\big(f(x)-g(x)\big)\,dx.\]
Top curve minus bottom curve
  • Find where the curves meet. These \(x\)-values are usually the limits of the integral.
  • Decide which curve is on top by testing one \(x\)-value between the limits.
  • Integrate the top curve minus the bottom curve. The formula still works when part of the region lies below the \(x\)-axis, because \(f(x)-g(x)\) is positive throughout the interval.

To show that two curves meet at a given \(x\)-value, substitute it into both equations and show that the \(y\)-values are equal.

Worked example 25Keep for homework
a Show that the curves \(y=\sin x\) and \(y=\sin2x\) intersect when \(x=\dfrac{\pi}{3}\).
b Sketch these curves in the interval \([0,\pi]\).
c Find the area contained between the curves in the interval \(\Big[0,\dfrac{\pi}{3}\Big]\).
Solution

a The curves intersect at \(x=\dfrac{\pi}{3}\) because \(\sin\dfrac{\pi}{3}=\sin\dfrac{2\pi}{3}=\tfrac12\sqrt3\).

b The curves are sketched to the right.

c In the interval \(0\le x\le\dfrac{\pi}{3}\), the curve \(y=\sin2x\) is always above \(y=\sin x\),

{{svg:c7e_ex25}}
\[\begin{aligned}\text{so area between}&=\int_0^{\frac{\pi}{3}}(\sin2x-\sin x)\,dx\\&=\Big[-\tfrac12\cos2x+\cos x\Big]_0^{\frac{\pi}{3}}\\&=\Big(-\tfrac12\cos\tfrac{2\pi}{3}+\cos\tfrac{\pi}{3}\Big)-\Big(-\tfrac12\cos0+\cos0\Big).\end{aligned}\]

Because \(\cos0=1\) and \(\cos\dfrac{\pi}{3}=\tfrac12\) and \(\cos\dfrac{2\pi}{3}=-\tfrac12\),

\[\begin{aligned}\text{area}&=\big(\tfrac14+\tfrac12\big)-\big(-\tfrac12+1\big)\\&=\tfrac14\text{ square units.}\end{aligned}\]
Reading Worked example 25, line by line

In part a, \(\sin2x\) at \(x=\dfrac{\pi}{3}\) is \(\sin\dfrac{2\pi}{3}\). The angle \(\dfrac{2\pi}{3}\) is in quadrant 2 with related angle \(\dfrac{\pi}{3}\), and sine is positive there, so \(\sin\dfrac{2\pi}{3}=\sin\dfrac{\pi}{3}=\tfrac12\sqrt3\). In part c, a test value shows which curve is on top: at \(x=\dfrac{\pi}{4}\), \(\sin2x=\sin\dfrac{\pi}{2}=1\) and \(\sin x=\tfrac12\sqrt2\), which is less than \(1\). The primitive of \(\sin2x\) is \(-\tfrac12\cos2x\), and the primitive of \(-\sin x\) is \(\cos x\). In the last lines, \(-\tfrac12\times\big(-\tfrac12\big)=\tfrac14\) and \(-\tfrac12\times1+1=\tfrac12\), so the area is \(\tfrac34-\tfrac12=\tfrac14\).

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 3:two curves that cross twice}}
Where \(y=\sin x\) and \(y=\cos x\) meet

To solve \(\sin x=\cos x\), divide both sides by \(\cos x\). This is allowed, because \(\cos x\ne0\) at any solution: if \(\cos x=0\), then \(\sin x=\pm1\), and the two sides are not equal. Dividing gives \(\tan x=1\). In each interval of length \(2\pi\) there are two solutions, \(\pi\) apart, one in quadrant 1 and one in quadrant 3.

The curves then cross twice, and the region between the crossings lies partly above and partly below the \(x\)-axis. Top curve minus bottom curve still gives its area in one integral.
