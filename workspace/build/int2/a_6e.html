C1
Tutor demo.

a Move the graph of \(y=e^x\) down \(e^2\) units.
When \(x=0\), \(\ y=e^0-e^2=1-e^2\).
When \(y=0\), \(\ e^x=e^2\), so \(x=2\).
The horizontal asymptote moves down to \(y=-e^2\).

{{svg:a6e_c1d}}

b The region is below the \(x\)-axis, from \(x=0\) to \(x=2\).

\[\begin{aligned}\int_0^2(e^x-e^2)\,dx&=\Big[e^x-e^2x\Big]_0^2\\&=(e^2-2e^2)-(e^0-0)\\&=-e^2-1.\end{aligned}\]

The integral is negative because the region is below the \(x\)-axis. Hence the area is \(e^2+1\) square units.

Past-Paper Homework - Worked Solutions
PP 1Pymble Ladies' College, 2023, Mathematics Extension 1 HSC Trial Examination Term 3, Q13(d)

(i) When \(x=e+1\), \(\ y=\log_e(e+1-1)=\log_ee=1\).

(ii) The curve meets the \(x\)-axis where \(\log_e(x-1)=0\), that is, \(x-1=1\), so \(x=2\). The shaded region runs from \(y=0\) to \(y=1\), with the curve on the left and the line \(x=e+1\) on the right, so integrate with respect to \(y\).

From \(y=\log_e(x-1)\), \(\ x-1=e^y\), so \(x=e^y+1\). Using the right-hand boundary minus the left-hand boundary,

\[\begin{aligned}\text{area}&=\int_0^1\Big((e+1)-(e^y+1)\Big)\,dy\\&=\int_0^1\big(e-e^y\big)\,dy\\&=\Big[ey-e^y\Big]_0^1\\&=(e\times1-e^1)-(e\times0-e^0)\\&=0-(-1)\\&=1.\end{aligned}\]

The shaded area is \(1\) square unit.

PP 2Fort Street High School, 2020, Trial Higher School Certificate Examination, Mathematics Extension 1, Q11(c)

(i) When \(x=0\), \(\ f(0)=e^0+1=2\), so the graph crosses the \(y\)-axis at \((0,2)\).

Differentiating, \(\ f'(x)=\tfrac12e^{\frac{x}{2}}\), so \(f'(0)=\tfrac12e^0=\tfrac12\). The gradient of the tangent at \((0,2)\) is \(\tfrac12\), so the gradient of the normal is \(-2\) (since \(\tfrac12\times(-2)=-1\)).

\[\begin{aligned}y-2&=-2(x-0)\\y&=-2x+2.\end{aligned}\]

The normal is \(y=-2x+2\), or \(2x+y-2=0\).

(ii) The normal meets the \(x\)-axis where \(0=-2x+2\), that is, at \(x=1\). The shaded region runs from \(x=0\) to \(x=1\) (the vertical edge in the diagram is the line \(x=1\), through the point where the normal meets the \(x\)-axis), with the curve on top and the normal below. Top minus bottom:

\[\begin{aligned}\text{area}&=\int_0^1\Big(\big(e^{\frac{x}{2}}+1\big)-\big(-2x+2\big)\Big)\,dx\\&=\int_0^1\big(e^{\frac{x}{2}}+2x-1\big)\,dx\\&=\Big[2e^{\frac{x}{2}}+x^2-x\Big]_0^1\\&=\big(2e^{\frac12}+1-1\big)-\big(2e^0+0-0\big)\\&=2\sqrt e-2.\end{aligned}\]

Here \(\displaystyle\int e^{\frac12x}\,dx=\dfrac{1}{\frac12}\,e^{\frac12x}=2e^{\frac12x}\). The shaded area is \(2\sqrt e-2\) square units.

PP 3Caringbah High School, 2020, Higher School Certificate Trial Examination, Mathematics Extension 1, Q14(c)(i)(ii)

(i) Using the product rule with \(u=x\) and \(v=\ln x\), so \(u'=1\) and \(v'=\dfrac1x\),

\[\begin{aligned}\dfrac{d}{dx}\big(x\ln x\big)&=u'v+v'u\\&=1\times\ln x+\dfrac1x\times x\\&=\ln x+1,\end{aligned}\]

which is \(1+\ln x\), as required.

(ii) The inverse function: from \(y=1+\ln x\), swap \(x\) and \(y\) to get \(x=1+\ln y\). Then \(\ln y=x-1\), so \(f^{-1}(x)=e^{x-1}\).

The two curves meet on the line \(y=x\) at \((1,1)\), since \(f(1)=1+\ln1=1\). The curve \(y=f(x)\) meets the \(x\)-axis where \(1+\ln x=0\), that is, \(x=e^{-1}\). The curve \(y=f^{-1}(x)\) meets the \(y\)-axis at \(y=e^{-1}\).

So the shaded region lies under \(y=e^{x-1}\) from \(x=0\) to \(x=1\), with the region under \(y=f(x)\) from \(x=e^{-1}\) to \(x=1\) taken away:

\[\text{area}=\int_0^1e^{x-1}\,dx-\int_{e^{-1}}^1(1+\ln x)\,dx.\]

By part (i), a primitive of \(1+\ln x\) is \(x\ln x\). Hence

\[\begin{aligned}\text{area}&=\Big[e^{x-1}\Big]_0^1-\Big[x\ln x\Big]_{e^{-1}}^1\\&=\big(e^0-e^{-1}\big)-\big(1\times\ln1-e^{-1}\ln e^{-1}\big)\\&=\big(1-e^{-1}\big)-\big(0-e^{-1}\times(-1)\big)\\&=1-e^{-1}-e^{-1}\\&=1-\dfrac2e.\end{aligned}\]

The shaded area is \(1-\dfrac2e\) square units.

PP 4Saint Ignatius' College, Riverview, 2024, Mathematics Assessment Task, Year 12 Mathematics (Extension One) Task 4, Trial HSC Examination, Q13(d)(ii)

(ii) The curve and the line meet at the point of part (i), where \(x=\dfrac{3e^2}{2}\) and \(y=\ln\Big(\dfrac23\times\dfrac{3e^2}{2}\Big)=\ln e^2=2\).

The line meets the \(x\)-axis where \(\ 0=-\dfrac{3e^2x}{2}+\dfrac{9e^4}{4}+2\), so \(\dfrac{3e^2x}{2}=\dfrac{9e^4}{4}+2\), and multiplying by \(\dfrac{2}{3e^2}\), \(\ x=\dfrac{3e^2}{2}+\dfrac{4}{3e^2}\).

The curve meets the \(x\)-axis where \(\ln\dfrac{2x}{3}=0\), that is, \(\dfrac{2x}{3}=1\), so \(x=\dfrac32\).

Let \(O=(0,0)\), \(A=\Big(\dfrac{3e^2}{2}+\dfrac{4}{3e^2},\,0\Big)\), \(B=\Big(\dfrac{3e^2}{2},\,2\Big)\) and \(C=(0,2)\). The enclosed region is the trapezium \(OABC\) with the region between the curve and the \(y\)-axis, from \(y=0\) to \(y=2\), taken away.

The trapezium has parallel sides \(OA\) and \(CB\) and height \(2\):

\[\begin{aligned}\text{area of }OABC&=\tfrac12\Big(\dfrac{3e^2}{2}+\dfrac{4}{3e^2}+\dfrac{3e^2}{2}\Big)\times2\\&=3e^2+\dfrac{4}{3e^2}.\end{aligned}\]

For the curve, \(y=\ln\dfrac{2x}{3}\) gives \(e^y=\dfrac{2x}{3}\), so \(x=\tfrac32e^y\). Integrating with respect to \(y\),

\[\begin{aligned}\int_0^2\tfrac32e^y\,dy&=\tfrac32\Big[e^y\Big]_0^2\\&=\tfrac32\big(e^2-e^0\big)\\&=\tfrac32e^2-\tfrac32.\end{aligned}\]

Hence the enclosed area is

\[\begin{aligned}3e^2+\dfrac{4}{3e^2}-\Big(\tfrac32e^2-\tfrac32\Big)&=\tfrac32e^2+\tfrac32+\dfrac{4}{3e^2}\text{ square units.}\end{aligned}\]
