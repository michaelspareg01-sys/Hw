Applications of integration of \(1/x\)

The usual applications of integration can now be applied to the reciprocal function, whose primitive was previously unavailable.

The standard forms from Lesson 6I
  • \(\displaystyle\int\dfrac1x\,dx=\log_e|x|+C\)
  • \(\displaystyle\int\dfrac{1}{ax+b}\,dx=\dfrac1a\log_e|ax+b|+C\)
  • \(\displaystyle\int\dfrac{f'(x)}{f(x)}\,dx=\log_e|f(x)|+C\)

Remember that \(\log_e1=0\) and \(\log_ee=1\). When \(x\gt0\), \(|x|=x\), so \(\log_e|x|\) may be written as \(\log_ex\).

The steps for finding an area
  • Sketch the curve, showing the intercepts and the asymptotes. Curves such as \(y=\dfrac kx\) have the vertical asymptote \(x=0\). A definite integral must never run across an asymptote.
  • Write down and evaluate the definite integral between the required \(x\)-values.
  • If the region is below the \(x\)-axis, the integral is negative, and the area is its opposite. If the region crosses the \(x\)-axis, split the integral at the \(x\)-intercept and find each part separately.
  • Simplify with the log laws: \(\log_ea+\log_eb=\log_eab\),  \(\log_ea-\log_eb=\log_e\dfrac ab\)  and  \(n\log_ea=\log_ea^n\).
  • Write a conclusion, giving the area in square units.
Regions to the left of the \(y\)-axis

For \(x\lt0\), the primitive \(\log_e|x|\) still works, because \(|x|\) is positive. For example, the curve \(y=\dfrac1x\) is below the \(x\)-axis for \(x\lt0\), and

\[\begin{aligned}\int_{-e}^{-1}\dfrac1x\,dx&=\Big[\log_e|x|\Big]_{-e}^{-1}\\&=\log_e|-1|-\log_e|-e|\\&=\log_e1-\log_ee\\&=-1.\end{aligned}\]

The integral is negative because the region is below the \(x\)-axis, so the area between \(y=\dfrac1x\) and the \(x\)-axis, from \(x=-e\) to \(x=-1\), is \(1\) square unit.

Finding areas by integration

The next worked example involves finding the area between two given curves.

Top curve minus bottom curve (from Lesson 6E)

If a curve \(y=f(x)\) is always above \(y=g(x)\) in an interval \(a\le x\le b\), then the area of the region between the curves is \(\displaystyle\int_a^b\big(f(x)-g(x)\big)\,dx\).

  • Find where the curves meet by solving their equations simultaneously. These \(x\)-values are the limits of the integral.
  • Decide which curve is on top by testing one \(x\)-value between the limits.
The hyperbola \(xy=k\)

When \(k\gt0\), the hyperbola \(xy=k\) is the curve \(y=\dfrac kx\). Both axes are asymptotes, and its two branches lie in the first and third quadrants. In the first quadrant, it passes through \((1,k)\) and \((k,1)\).

Worked example 38Keep for homework
a Find where the hyperbola \(xy=2\) meets the line \(x+y=3\).
b Sketch the situation.
c Find the area of the region between the two curves, in exact form, and then correct to three decimal places.
Solution

a Substituting the line \(y=3-x\) into the hyperbola,

\(x(3-x)\)\(=2\)
\(x^2-3x+2\)\(=0\)
\((x-1)(x-2)\)\(=0\)
\(x\)\(=1\ \text{ or }\ 2,\)

so the curves meet at \((1,2)\) and \((2,1)\).

b The hyperbola \(xy=2\) has both axes as asymptotes.
The line \(x+y=3\) has \(x\)-intercept \((3,0)\) and \(y\)-intercept \((0,3)\).

{{svg:c6j_ex38}}

c

\[\begin{aligned}\text{Area}&=\int_1^2(\text{top curve}-\text{bottom curve})\,dx\\&=\int_1^2\Big((3-x)-\dfrac2x\Big)\,dx\\&=\Big[3x-\tfrac12x^2-2\log_e|x|\Big]_1^2\\&=\big(6-2-2\log_e2\big)-\big(3-\tfrac12-2\log_e1\big)\\&=\big(4-2\log_e2\big)-\big(2\tfrac12-0\big)\\&=\big(1\tfrac12-2\log_e2\big)\text{ square units}\\&\doteq0.114\text{ square units.}\end{aligned}\]

Note: In part a, expanding \(x(3-x)=2\) gives \(3x-x^2=2\), and moving every term to the right gives \(x^2-3x+2=0\). In part c, the hyperbola is \(y=\dfrac2x\), and the line \(y=3-x\) is on top: at \(x=1\tfrac12\), the line gives \(1\tfrac12\) and the hyperbola gives \(1\tfrac13\). In the fourth line, \(3\times2=6\), \(\tfrac12\times2^2=2\), \(3\times1=3\) and \(\tfrac12\times1^2=\tfrac12\). In the fifth line, \(\log_e1=0\). Finally, \(1\tfrac12-2\log_e2\doteq1.5-1.3863=0.1137\).
