Tutor demo.

a The curve \(y=\cos\tfrac13x\) has amplitude \(1\), and the period is \(2\pi\div\tfrac13=6\pi\).
It crosses the \(x\)-axis where \(\tfrac13x=\dfrac{\pi}{2}\) or \(\tfrac13x=\dfrac{3\pi}{2}\), so the two \(x\)-intercepts in the interval are \(x=\dfrac{3\pi}{2}\) and \(x=\dfrac{9\pi}{2}\).

{{svg:a7e_c1d}}

b Integrate separately over \(\Big[0,\dfrac{3\pi}{2}\Big]\), \(\Big[\dfrac{3\pi}{2},\dfrac{9\pi}{2}\Big]\) and \(\Big[\dfrac{9\pi}{2},6\pi\Big]\). The primitive of \(\cos\tfrac13x\) is \(\dfrac{1}{\frac13}\sin\tfrac13x=3\sin\tfrac13x\).

\[\begin{aligned}\int_0^{\frac{3\pi}{2}}\cos\tfrac13x\,dx&=\Big[3\sin\tfrac13x\Big]_0^{\frac{3\pi}{2}}\\&=3\sin\tfrac{\pi}{2}-3\sin0\\&=3-0\\&=3,\end{aligned}\]

which is positive, because the curve is above the \(x\)-axis there.

\[\begin{aligned}\int_{\frac{3\pi}{2}}^{\frac{9\pi}{2}}\cos\tfrac13x\,dx&=\Big[3\sin\tfrac13x\Big]_{\frac{3\pi}{2}}^{\frac{9\pi}{2}}\\&=3\sin\tfrac{3\pi}{2}-3\sin\tfrac{\pi}{2}\\&=-3-3\\&=-6,\end{aligned}\]

which is negative, because the curve is below the \(x\)-axis there.

\[\begin{aligned}\int_{\frac{9\pi}{2}}^{6\pi}\cos\tfrac13x\,dx&=\Big[3\sin\tfrac13x\Big]_{\frac{9\pi}{2}}^{6\pi}\\&=3\sin2\pi-3\sin\tfrac{3\pi}{2}\\&=0-(-3)\\&=3,\end{aligned}\]

which is positive, because the curve is above the \(x\)-axis there. At the limits, \(\tfrac13\times\dfrac{3\pi}{2}=\dfrac{\pi}{2}\), \(\tfrac13\times\dfrac{9\pi}{2}=\dfrac{3\pi}{2}\) and \(\tfrac13\times6\pi=2\pi\).

\[\begin{aligned}\text{Hence total area}&=3+6+3\\&=12\text{ square units.}\end{aligned}\]

a The curve \(y=\cos2x\) has amplitude \(1\), and the period is \(2\pi\div2=\pi\).
It crosses the \(x\)-axis where \(2x=\dfrac{\pi}{2}\) or \(2x=\dfrac{3\pi}{2}\), so the two \(x\)-intercepts in the interval are \(x=\dfrac{\pi}{4}\) and \(x=\dfrac{3\pi}{4}\).

{{svg:a7e_c1y}}

b Integrate separately over \(\Big[0,\dfrac{\pi}{4}\Big]\), \(\Big[\dfrac{\pi}{4},\dfrac{3\pi}{4}\Big]\) and \(\Big[\dfrac{3\pi}{4},\pi\Big]\). The primitive of \(\cos2x\) is \(\tfrac12\sin2x\).

\[\begin{aligned}\int_0^{\frac{\pi}{4}}\cos2x\,dx&=\Big[\tfrac12\sin2x\Big]_0^{\frac{\pi}{4}}\\&=\tfrac12\sin\tfrac{\pi}{2}-\tfrac12\sin0\\&=\tfrac12-0\\&=\tfrac12,\end{aligned}\]

which is positive, because the curve is above the \(x\)-axis there.

\[\begin{aligned}\int_{\frac{\pi}{4}}^{\frac{3\pi}{4}}\cos2x\,dx&=\Big[\tfrac12\sin2x\Big]_{\frac{\pi}{4}}^{\frac{3\pi}{4}}\\&=\tfrac12\sin\tfrac{3\pi}{2}-\tfrac12\sin\tfrac{\pi}{2}\\&=-\tfrac12-\tfrac12\\&=-1,\end{aligned}\]

which is negative, because the curve is below the \(x\)-axis there.

\[\begin{aligned}\int_{\frac{3\pi}{4}}^{\pi}\cos2x\,dx&=\Big[\tfrac12\sin2x\Big]_{\frac{3\pi}{4}}^{\pi}\\&=\tfrac12\sin2\pi-\tfrac12\sin\tfrac{3\pi}{2}\\&=0-\big(-\tfrac12\big)\\&=\tfrac12,\end{aligned}\]

which is positive, because the curve is above the \(x\)-axis there.

\[\begin{aligned}\text{Hence total area}&=\tfrac12+1+\tfrac12\\&=2\text{ square units.}\end{aligned}\]

PP 1Fort Street High School, 2020, Trial Higher School Certificate Examination, Mathematics Extension 1, Q10

The points \(A\) and \(B\) are where the curves meet. Put \(\sin x=\cos x\). Then \(\tan x=1\), so \(x=\dfrac{\pi}{4}\) or \(x=\dfrac{5\pi}{4}\). These are the limits of the integral.

Between \(A\) and \(B\), the curve \(y=\sin x\) is on top: at \(x=\dfrac{\pi}{2}\), \(\sin x=1\) and \(\cos x=0\). The area is the integral of the top curve minus the bottom curve, \(\displaystyle\int_{\frac{\pi}{4}}^{\frac{5\pi}{4}}\big(\sin(x)-\cos(x)\big)\,dx\).

Option A is the bottom curve minus the top curve, so it gives the opposite of the area. Option C adds the two functions instead of subtracting them. Option D has the wrong limits. Answer (B).

PP 2Caringbah High School, 2021, Year 12 Mathematics Extension 1 HSC Course Assessment Task 4, Q12(c)

(i) When \(x=0\),

\[\begin{aligned}y&=2\cos^{-1}0\\&=2\times\dfrac{\pi}{2}\\&=\pi.\end{aligned}\]

The \(y\) intercept is \(\pi\).

(ii) The domain of \(\cos^{-1}\) is \(-1\le\dfrac{x}{3}\le1\). Multiplying by \(3\), the domain of \(f\) is \(-3\le x\le3\).

The range of \(\cos^{-1}\) is \(0\le\cos^{-1}\dfrac{x}{3}\le\pi\). Multiplying by \(2\), the range of \(f\) is \(0\le y\le2\pi\).

(iii) The region lies under the curve between \(x=0\) and \(x=3\). At \(x=3\), \(y=2\cos^{-1}1=0\), and at \(x=0\), \(y=\pi\). So the region is bounded by the curve, the \(x\)-axis and the \(y\)-axis, and it runs from \(y=0\) to \(y=\pi\). Make \(x\) the subject, and integrate with respect to \(y\):

\[\begin{aligned}y&=2\cos^{-1}\dfrac{x}{3}\\\dfrac{y}{2}&=\cos^{-1}\dfrac{x}{3}\\\dfrac{x}{3}&=\cos\dfrac{y}{2}\\x&=3\cos\dfrac{y}{2}.\end{aligned}\]

For \(0\le y\le\pi\), \(\ x=3\cos\dfrac{y}{2}\) is positive, so the region lies between the \(y\)-axis and this curve.

\[\begin{aligned}\text{Area}&=\int_0^\pi3\cos\dfrac{y}{2}\,dy\\&=\Big[6\sin\dfrac{y}{2}\Big]_0^\pi\\&=6\sin\dfrac{\pi}{2}-6\sin0\\&=6\times1-0\\&=6.\end{aligned}\]

The area is \(6\) square units. (The primitive of \(\cos\tfrac12y\) is \(\dfrac{1}{\frac12}\sin\tfrac12y=2\sin\tfrac12y\), and \(3\times2=6\).)

PP 3Northern Beaches Secondary College Manly Selective Campus, 2020, Year 12 Trial Examination, Mathematics Extension 1, Q11(c)

The curve \(y=\tan^{-1}x\) passes through the origin and meets the lines \(y=\pm\dfrac{\pi}{4}\) at \(x=\pm1\), because \(\tan^{-1}1=\dfrac{\pi}{4}\) and \(\tan^{-1}(-1)=-\dfrac{\pi}{4}\). The region has two parts, one in the first quadrant and one in the third quadrant, each between the curve and the \(y\)-axis.

{{svg:a7e_manly}}

Make \(x\) the subject: from \(y=\tan^{-1}x\), \(\ x=\tan y\), for \(-\dfrac{\pi}{2}\lt y\lt\dfrac{\pi}{2}\).

The function \(\tan^{-1}x\) is odd, so the two parts have equal area. In the first quadrant, the region runs from \(y=0\) to \(y=\dfrac{\pi}{4}\), between the \(y\)-axis and \(x=\tan y\). Using \(\displaystyle\int\tan y\,dy=-\log_e|\cos y|+C\) (Lesson 7D),

\[\begin{aligned}\text{Area}&=2\int_0^{\frac{\pi}{4}}\tan y\,dy\\&=2\Big[-\log_e\cos y\Big]_0^{\frac{\pi}{4}}\\&=2\Big(-\log_e\cos\tfrac{\pi}{4}+\log_e\cos0\Big)\\&=2\Big(-\log_e\dfrac{1}{\sqrt2}+\log_e1\Big)\\&=2\big(\log_e\sqrt2+0\big)\\&=2\times\tfrac12\log_e2\\&=\log_e2.\end{aligned}\]

The area is \(\ln2\) square units. Here \(\cos y\gt0\) for \(0\le y\le\dfrac{\pi}{4}\), so \(|\cos y|=\cos y\); also \(-\log_e\dfrac{1}{\sqrt2}=\log_e\sqrt2\) and \(\log_e\sqrt2=\log_e2^{\frac12}=\tfrac12\log_e2\).

PP 4Hornsby Girls High School, 2023, Year 12 Higher School Certificate Trial Examination Term 3, Mathematics Extension 1, Q14(b)

The shaded region is bounded by the curve and the two axes.

When \(x=0\), \(\ y=2\cos^{-1}\tfrac12=2\times\dfrac{\pi}{3}=\dfrac{2\pi}{3}\).
When \(y=0\), \(\ \cos^{-1}\big(x+\tfrac12\big)=0\), so \(x+\tfrac12=1\) and \(x=\tfrac12\).

So the region runs from \(y=0\) to \(y=\dfrac{2\pi}{3}\). Make \(x\) the subject:

\[\begin{aligned}y&=2\cos^{-1}\big(x+\tfrac12\big)\\\dfrac{y}{2}&=\cos^{-1}\big(x+\tfrac12\big)\\\cos\dfrac{y}{2}&=x+\tfrac12\\x&=\cos\dfrac{y}{2}-\tfrac12.\end{aligned}\]

The region lies between the \(y\)-axis and this curve, so integrate with respect to \(y\):

\[\begin{aligned}A&=\int_0^{\frac{2\pi}{3}}\Big(\cos\dfrac{y}{2}-\tfrac12\Big)\,dy\\&=\Big[2\sin\dfrac{y}{2}-\tfrac12y\Big]_0^{\frac{2\pi}{3}}\\&=\Big(2\sin\dfrac{\pi}{3}-\tfrac12\times\dfrac{2\pi}{3}\Big)-\Big(2\sin0-0\Big)\\&=2\times\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2}-\dfrac{\pi}{3}\\&=\sqrt3-\dfrac{\pi}{3}.\end{aligned}\]

The shaded area is \(\Big(\sqrt3-\dfrac{\pi}{3}\Big)\) square units. (At the upper limit, \(\tfrac12\times\dfrac{2\pi}{3}=\dfrac{\pi}{3}\).)

PP 5Pymble Ladies' College, 2024, Mathematics Extension 1 HSC Trial Examination Term 3, Q13(d)

(i) The curve \(y=-\tan^{-1}x\) is the reflection of \(y=\tan^{-1}x\) in the \(x\)-axis. It passes through the origin, it is decreasing, and its horizontal asymptotes are \(y=\dfrac{\pi}{2}\) (to the left) and \(y=-\dfrac{\pi}{2}\) (to the right).

When \(x=1\), \(\ y=-\tan^{-1}1=-\dfrac{\pi}{4}\), so the labelled point is \(\Big(1,-\dfrac{\pi}{4}\Big)\).

{{svg:a7e_plc}}

(ii) The region is below the \(x\)-axis, from \(x=0\) to \(x=1\), and it runs from \(y=-\dfrac{\pi}{4}\) to \(y=0\). Make \(x\) the subject: from \(y=-\tan^{-1}x\), \(\ \tan^{-1}x=-y\), so \(x=\tan(-y)=-\tan y\).

At each height \(y\), the region runs from the curve \(x=-\tan y\) on the left to the line \(x=1\) on the right. Using the right-hand boundary minus the left-hand boundary,

\[\begin{aligned}\text{Area}&=\int_{-\frac{\pi}{4}}^{0}\big(1-(-\tan y)\big)\,dy\\&=\int_{-\frac{\pi}{4}}^{0}\big(1+\tan y\big)\,dy\\&=\Big[y-\log_e\cos y\Big]_{-\frac{\pi}{4}}^{0}\\&=\big(0-\log_e\cos0\big)-\Big(-\tfrac{\pi}{4}-\log_e\cos\big(-\tfrac{\pi}{4}\big)\Big)\\&=(0-0)-\Big(-\tfrac{\pi}{4}-\log_e\dfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\Big)\\&=\dfrac{\pi}{4}+\log_e\dfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\\&=\dfrac{\pi}{4}-\tfrac12\log_e2.\end{aligned}\]

The area is \(\Big(\dfrac{\pi}{4}-\tfrac12\ln2\Big)\) square units. Here \(\cos y\gt0\) for \(-\dfrac{\pi}{4}\le y\le0\), so \(|\cos y|=\cos y\), and \(\log_e\dfrac{1}{\sqrt2}=\log_e2^{-\frac12}=-\tfrac12\log_e2\).
