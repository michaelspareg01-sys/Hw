Lesson 6I

Integration of the reciprocal function

The usual rule for integrating powers of \(x\) fails for \(\dfrac1x=x^{-1}\), because it divides by zero. Reversing the standard form \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\log_ex=\dfrac1x\) gives the missing primitive, \(\displaystyle\int\dfrac1x\,dx=\log_e|x|+C\). We extend it to both sides of the origin, learn the three standard forms of Box 10, evaluate definite integrals without crossing an asymptote, find a function from its derivative, and find a primitive of \(\log_ex\).
{{sec:teal:key:Concept 1:the primitive of the reciprocal function}}
Why the power rule fails for \(y=\dfrac1x\)

The reciprocal function \(y=\dfrac1x\) is an important function. We have seen that it is required whenever two quantities are inversely proportional to each other. So far, however, it has not been possible to integrate the reciprocal function, because the usual rule for integrating powers of \(x\) gives nonsense:

\[\text{When }n=-1,\quad\int x^n\,dx=\dfrac{x^{n+1}}{n+1}\quad\text{gives}\quad\int x^{-1}\,dx=\dfrac{x^0}{0},\]

which is nonsense because of the division by zero.

Yet the graph of \(y=\dfrac1x\) to the right shows that there should be no problem with definite integrals involving \(\dfrac1x\), provided that the integral does not cross the discontinuity at \(x=0\). For example, the diagram shows the integral \(\displaystyle\int_1^2\dfrac1x\,dx\), which the little rectangles show has a value between \(\tfrac12\) and \(1\).

{{svg:c6i_rect}}
Integration of the reciprocal function

Reversing the standard form for differentiating \(\log x\) will now give the necessary standard forms for integrating \(\dfrac1x\).

Reversing\(\dfrac{d}{dx}\log_ex=\dfrac1x\)gives\(\displaystyle\int\dfrac1x\,dx=\log_ex+C.\)

This is a new standard form for integrating the reciprocal function. The only qualification is that \(x\gt0\), otherwise \(\log_ex\) is undefined, so we have

\[\int\dfrac1x\,dx=\log_ex+C,\ \text{ for some constant }C,\text{ provided that }x\gt0.\]
A characterisation of \(e\)

Integrating the reciprocal function from \(1\) to \(e\) gives an amazingly simple result:

\[\begin{aligned}\int_1^e\dfrac1x\,dx&=\Big[\log_ex\Big]_1^e\\&=\log_ee-\log_e1\\&=1-0\\&=1.\end{aligned}\]
{{svg:c6i_e}}

The integral is sketched to the right. The example is very important because it characterises \(e\) as the real number satisfying \(\displaystyle\int_1^e\dfrac1x\,dx=1\). In other expositions of the theory, this integral is taken as the definition of \(e\).

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 2:the primitive on both sides of the origin}}
The primitive of \(y=\dfrac1x\) on both sides of the origin

So far our primitive is restricted by the condition \(x\gt0\), meaning that we can only deal with definite integrals on the right-hand side of the origin. The full graph of the reciprocal function \(y=\dfrac1x\), however, is a hyperbola, with two disconnected branches separated by the discontinuity at \(x=0\).

There is no reason why we should not integrate over a closed interval such as \(-4\le x\le-1\) on the left-hand side of the origin. We can take any definite integrals of \(\dfrac1x\), provided only that we do not work across the asymptote at \(x=0\). If \(x\) is negative, then \(\log(-x)\) is well defined, and using our previous standard forms,

\[\dfrac{d}{dx}\log(-x)=-\Big(\dfrac{1}{-x}\Big)=\dfrac1x.\]
{{svg:c6i_both}}

Reversing this, \(\log(-x)\) is a primitive of \(\dfrac1x\) when \(x\) is negative,

\[\int\dfrac1x\,dx=\log_e(-x)+C,\ \text{ provided that }x\lt0.\]

The absolute value function is designed for just these situations. We can combine the two results into one standard form for the whole reciprocal function,

\[\int\dfrac1x\,dx=\log_e|x|+C,\ \text{ provided that }x\ne0.\]

Question 16 of Exercise 6G gives more detail about this standard form.

The derivative of \(\log_e(-x)\) in full

The line \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\log(-x)=-\Big(\dfrac{1}{-x}\Big)\) uses the chain rule. Written out:

Let\(y=\log_e(-x),\)where \(x\lt0\).
Let\(u=-x,\)so \(y=\log_eu\).
Then\(\dfrac{du}{dx}=-1\ \text{ and }\ \dfrac{dy}{du}=\dfrac1u.\)
Hence\(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=\dfrac{dy}{du}\times\dfrac{du}{dx}=\dfrac{1}{-x}\times(-1)=\dfrac1x.\)
Each branch may have its own constant of integration

Careful readers will realise that because \(y=\dfrac1x\) has two disconnected branches, there can be different constants of integration in the two branches. So the general primitive of \(\dfrac1x\) is

\[\int\dfrac1x\,dx=\begin{cases}\log_ex+A,&\text{for }x\gt0,\\\log(-x)+B,&\text{for }x\lt0,\end{cases}\qquad\text{where }A\text{ and }B\text{ are constants.}\]

If a boundary condition is given for one branch, this has no implication at all for the constant of integration in the other branch.

In any physical interpretation, however, the function would normally have meaning in only one of the two branches, so the complication discussed here is rarely needed, and the over-simplified forms in Box 10 below are standard and generally used. The qualification is understood and taken account of when necessary.

Three standard forms

As always, reversing the other standard forms for differentiation gives two more standard forms.

Where the second and third forms come from

Reversing each derivative, as for \(\dfrac1x\):

Reversing\(\dfrac{d}{dx}\log_e(ax+b)=\dfrac{a}{ax+b}\)
gives\(\displaystyle\int\dfrac{a}{ax+b}\,dx=\log_e|ax+b|,\)
and dividing through by \(a\),\(\displaystyle\int\dfrac{1}{ax+b}\,dx=\dfrac1a\log_e|ax+b|+C.\)
Reversing\(\dfrac{d}{dx}\log_eu=\dfrac{u'}{u}\)
gives\(\displaystyle\int\dfrac{u'}{u}\,dx=\log_e|u|+C.\)
10 Standard forms for integrating reciprocal functions
  • \(\displaystyle\int\dfrac1x\,dx=\log_e|x|+C\)
  • \(\displaystyle\int\dfrac{1}{ax+b}\,dx=\dfrac1a\log_e|ax+b|+C\)
  • \(\displaystyle\int\dfrac{u'}{u}\,dx=\log_e|u|+C\qquad\text{OR}\qquad\int\dfrac{f'(x)}{f(x)}\,dx=\log_e|f(x)|+C\)

No calculation involving these primitives may cross an asymptote.

Never cross an asymptote

The final warning always applies to the primitive of any function, but it is mentioned here because it is such an obvious issue.

Checking for an asymptote

Before substituting the limits, find where the denominator is zero. If that \(x\)-value lies between the limits, the definite integral is meaningless.

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 3:using the third standard form}}
Using the third standard form

The vital point in using the third standard form,

\[\int\dfrac{u'}{u}\,dx=\log_e|u|\qquad\text{OR}\qquad\int\dfrac{f'(x)}{f(x)}\,dx=\log_e|f(x)|,\]

is that the top must be the derivative of the bottom. Choose whichever form of the reverse chain rule you are most comfortable with.

Steps for the third standard form
  • Let \(u\) (or \(f(x)\)) be the bottom, and find its derivative \(u'\).
  • If the top is \(u'\), the primitive is \(\log_e|u|\).
  • If the top is a constant multiple of \(u'\), first make the top the derivative of the bottom: multiply the top by a constant, and multiply outside the integral by its reciprocal (Worked example 35b).

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 4:given the derivative, find the function}}
Given the derivative, find the function

Finding the function from the derivative involves a constant that can be found if the value of \(y\) is known for some value of \(x\).

Steps for finding the function
  • Take the primitive of the derivative, including the constant \(C\).
  • Substitute the known value to find \(C\).
  • Write the function with the value of \(C\) in it, then use it. Check that the known point and the \(x\)-value you use are on the same side of any asymptote.

A primitive of \(\log_ex\)

The next worked example is more difficult, but it is important because it produces a primitive of \(\log_ex\), which the theory has not yielded so far. There is no need to memorise the result.

How the method works

The method reverses a derivative, as in Section 6D: differentiate a function whose derivative contains \(\log_ex\), then reverse the result.
