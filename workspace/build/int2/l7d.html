Lesson 7D

Integrating the trigonometric functions

Reversing the standard derivatives of \(\sin x\), \(\cos x\) and \(\tan x\) gives three new standard integrals, and replacing \(x\) by \(ax+b\) extends them. With these we find areas and definite integrals, find the primitives of \(\tan x\) and \(\cot x\), find a function from its derivative and one known value, and reverse chain-rule derivatives to find new primitives.
{{sec:teal:key:Concept 1:the standard forms for integrating the trigonometric functions}}
The standard forms for integrating the trigonometric functions

As always, the standard forms for differentiation can be reversed to give standard forms for integration.

When the standard forms for differentiating \(\sin x\), \(\cos x\) and \(\tan x\) are reversed, they give three new standard integrals.

First,\(\dfrac{d}{dx}\sin x=\cos x,\)and reversing this,\(\displaystyle\int\cos x\,dx=\sin x.\)
Secondly,\(\dfrac{d}{dx}\cos x=-\sin x,\)and reversing this,\(\displaystyle\int(-\sin x)\,dx=\cos x\)
\(\times(-1)\)\(\displaystyle\int\sin x\,dx=-\cos x.\)
Thirdly,\(\dfrac{d}{dx}\tan x=\sec^2x,\)and reversing this,\(\displaystyle\int\sec^2x\,dx=\tan x.\)

This gives three new standard integrals. These three standard forms should be carefully memorised — pay attention to the signs in the first two standard forms.

8 Standard trigonometric integrals
  • \(\displaystyle\int\cos x\,dx=\sin x+C\), for some constant \(C\)
  • \(\displaystyle\int\sin x\,dx=-\cos x+C\), for some constant \(C\)
  • \(\displaystyle\int\sec^2x\,dx=\tan x+C\), for some constant \(C\)

No calculation involving a primitive may cross an asymptote.

Where the minus sign comes from

The box \(\times(-1)\) means: multiply both sides of \(\displaystyle\int(-\sin x)\,dx=\cos x\) by \(-1\). This gives \(\displaystyle\int\sin x\,dx=-\cos x\). Check it by differentiating: \(\tfrac{d}{dx}(-\cos x)=-(-\sin x)=\sin x\).

The asymptotes in this lesson belong to \(\tan x\) and to \(\sec^2x\), the reciprocal of \(\cos^2x\). They are the lines \(x=\tfrac{\pi}{2}\), \(x=\tfrac{3\pi}{2}\), \(x=-\tfrac{\pi}{2}\), and so on, where \(\cos x=0\).

Solution

Because the region is entirely above the \(x\)-axis,

\[\begin{aligned}\text{area}&=\int_0^\pi\sin x\,dx\\&=\Big[-\cos x\Big]_0^\pi\\&=-\cos\pi+\cos0\\&=-(-1)+1\end{aligned}\]

(the graph of \(y=\cos x\) shows that \(\cos\pi=-1\))
\(\phantom{(}=2\) square units.

{{svg:c7d_ex16}}
The substitution in Worked example 16

Substituting the upper limit and then the lower limit into \(-\cos x\), and subtracting,

\[\Big[-\cos x\Big]_0^\pi=(-\cos\pi)-(-\cos0)=-\cos\pi+\cos0.\]

Then \(\cos\pi=-1\) and \(\cos0=1\), so the area is \(-(-1)+1=1+1=2\) square units.

Note

This simple answer confirms again that radians are the right units to use for calculus with trigonometric functions. Similar simple results were obtained earlier when \(e\) was used as the base for powers. For example, Question 39c of the Chapter 6 Review gathered together three remarkably simple results:

\[\int_1^e\dfrac1x\,dx=\int_1^e\log_ex\,dx=\int_0^1xe^x\,dx=1\]

Why part c is meaningless

The integrand \(\sec^2x\) is the reciprocal of \(\cos^2x\), so it is undefined at \(x=\tfrac{\pi}{2}\), because \(\cos\tfrac{\pi}{2}=0\), and \(\tfrac{\pi}{2}\) lies between the limits \(\tfrac{\pi}{4}\) and \(\tfrac{3\pi}{4}\). The primitive \(\tan x\) has an asymptote there. If the asymptote were ignored, the calculation would give \(\tan\tfrac{3\pi}{4}-\tan\tfrac{\pi}{4}=-1-1=-2\), a negative number from a function \(\sec^2x\) that is never negative. This shows that such a calculation cannot be trusted.

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 2:replacing x by ax + b}}
Replacing \(x\) by \(ax+b\)

Reversing the standard forms for derivatives in Section 7B gives a further set of standard forms. Again, the constants of integration have been ignored until the boxed statement of the standard forms.

First,\(\dfrac{d}{dx}\sin(ax+b)=a\cos(ax+b),\)
so\(\displaystyle\int a\cos(ax+b)\,dx=\sin(ax+b)\)
and dividing by \(a\),\(\displaystyle\int\cos(ax+b)\,dx=\dfrac1a\sin(ax+b).\)
Secondly,\(\dfrac{d}{dx}\cos(ax+b)=-a\sin(ax+b),\)
so\(\displaystyle\int-a\sin(ax+b)\,dx=\cos(ax+b)\)
and dividing by \(-a\),\(\displaystyle\int\sin(ax+b)\,dx=-\dfrac1a\cos(ax+b).\)
Thirdly,\(\dfrac{d}{dx}\tan(ax+b)=a\sec^2(ax+b),\)
so\(\displaystyle\int a\sec^2(ax+b)\,dx=\tan(ax+b).\)
and dividing by \(a\),\(\displaystyle\int\sec^2(ax+b)\,dx=\dfrac1a\tan(ax+b).\)

The result is extended forms of the three standard integrals. These extended standard forms should also be carefully memorised.

9 Standard integrals for functions of \(ax+b\)
  • \(\displaystyle\int\cos(ax+b)\,dx=\dfrac1a\sin(ax+b)+C\), for some constant \(C\)
  • \(\displaystyle\int\sin(ax+b)\,dx=-\dfrac1a\cos(ax+b)+C\), for some constant \(C\)
  • \(\displaystyle\int\sec^2(ax+b)\,dx=\dfrac1a\tan(ax+b)+C\), for some constant \(C\)
Reading off \(a\) and \(b\)

Compare the angle with \(ax+b\). For \(\sin\tfrac14x\), the angle is \(\tfrac14x+0\), so \(a=\tfrac14\), \(b=0\) and \(-\tfrac1a=-4\). For \(\sec^2(2x+\pi)\), \(a=2\) and \(b=\pi\), so \(\tfrac1a=\tfrac12\). For \(\cos(7-5x)\), the angle is \(-5x+7\), so \(a=-5\). The \(\tfrac1a\) can be checked by differentiating the answer.

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 3:the primitives of tan x and cot x}}
The primitives of \(\tan x\) and \(\cot x\)

The primitives of \(\tan x\) and \(\cot x\) can be found by using the ratio formulae \(\tan x=\dfrac{\sin x}{\cos x}\) and \(\cot x=\dfrac{\cos x}{\sin x}\) and then applying the standard form from the previous chapter, in either one of its two versions,

\[\int\dfrac{u'}{u}\,dx=\log_e|u|+C\qquad\text{or}\qquad\int\dfrac{f'(x)}{f(x)}\,dx=\log_e|f(x)|+C.\]

Worked example 19Keep for homework
Find primitives of these functions.
a\(\cot x\)b\(\tan x\)
Solution

a

\[\begin{aligned}&\int\cot x\,dx\\&\quad=\int\dfrac{\cos x}{\sin x}\,dx\\&\quad=\log_e|\sin x|+C.\end{aligned}\]
Let \(u=\sin x.\)ORLet \(f(x)=\sin x.\)
Then \(u'=\cos x.\)Then \(f'(x)=\cos x.\)
\(\displaystyle\int\dfrac{u'}{u}\,dx=\log_e|u|\)\(\displaystyle\int\dfrac{f'(x)}{f(x)}\,dx=\log_e|f(x)|\)

b

\[\begin{aligned}&\int\tan x\,dx\\&\quad=\int\dfrac{\sin x}{\cos x}\,dx\\&\quad=-\int\dfrac{-\sin x}{\cos x}\,dx\\&\quad=-\log_e|\cos x|+C.\end{aligned}\]
Let \(u=\cos x.\)ORLet \(f(x)=\cos x.\)
Then \(u'=-\sin x.\)Then \(f'(x)=-\sin x.\)
\(\displaystyle\int\dfrac{u'}{u}\,dx=\log_e|u|\)\(\displaystyle\int\dfrac{f'(x)}{f(x)}\,dx=\log_e|f(x)|\)

(This can also be written as \(\log_e|\sec x|\).)

Note

Do not run across a zero of \(\sin x\) when using part a, or a zero of \(\cos x\) when using part b.

The steps in Worked example 19

In part b, the top \(\sin x\) is not yet the derivative of the bottom \(\cos x\), which is \(-\sin x\). Multiplying the top by \(-1\) and the outside by \(-1\) balances it:

\[\dfrac{\sin x}{\cos x}=-\dfrac{-\sin x}{\cos x}.\]

The last form uses a log law:

\[-\log_e|\cos x|=\log_e|\cos x|^{-1}=\log_e\Big|\dfrac{1}{\cos x}\Big|=\log_e|\sec x|.\]

When the angle is \(ax\), balance the constant in the same way. For example, the derivative of \(\sin2x\) is \(2\cos2x\), so

\[\cot2x=\dfrac{\cos2x}{\sin2x}=\dfrac12\times\dfrac{2\cos2x}{\sin2x}.\]

Worked example 22Keep for homework
a Use the chain rule to differentiate \(\cos^5x\).
b Hence find \(\displaystyle\int_0^\pi\sin x\cos^4x\,dx\).
Solution

a

Let\(y=\cos^5x.\)
By the chain rule,\(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=\dfrac{dy}{du}\times\dfrac{du}{dx}\)
\(\phantom{\dfrac{dy}{dx}}=-5\sin x\cos^4x.\)

Let \(u=\cos x\).
Then \(y=u^5\).
Hence \(\dfrac{du}{dx}=-\sin x\)
and \(\dfrac{dy}{du}=5u^4\).

b

From part a,\(\dfrac{d}{dx}(\cos^5x)=-5\sin x\cos^4x.\)
Reversing this,\(\displaystyle\int(-5\sin x\cos^4x)\,dx=\cos^5x.\)
\(\div(-5)\)\(\displaystyle\int\sin x\cos^4x\,dx=-\tfrac15\cos^5x.\)

Hence

\[\begin{aligned}\int_0^\pi\sin x\cos^4x\,dx&=-\tfrac15\Big[\cos^5x\Big]_0^\pi\\&=-\tfrac15(-1-1)\\&=\tfrac25\end{aligned}\]
The steps in Worked example 22

In part a, \(\tfrac{dy}{du}\times\tfrac{du}{dx}=5u^4\times(-\sin x)=-5\sin x\cos^4x\), after putting back \(u=\cos x\).

In part b, the box \(\div(-5)\) means: divide both sides by \(-5\). For the limits, \(\cos\pi=-1\), so \(\cos^5\pi=(-1)^5=-1\), and \(\cos0=1\), so \(\cos^50=1^5=1\). Hence \(-\tfrac15\Big[\cos^5x\Big]_0^\pi=-\tfrac15(-1-1)=-\tfrac15\times(-2)=\tfrac25\).

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 6:using a formula for the reverse chain rule}}
Using a formula for the reverse chain rule

The integral in the worked example above could have been done using the reverse chain rule for powers of \(u\) or \(f(x)\).

The formula from Section 5I

The reverse chain rule for powers has two forms:

\[\int u^n\,\dfrac{du}{dx}\,dx=\dfrac{u^{n+1}}{n+1}+C\qquad\text{OR}\qquad\int\big(f(x)\big)^n\,f'(x)\,dx=\dfrac{\big(f(x)\big)^{n+1}}{n+1}+C,\]

where \(n\ne-1\). Let \(u\) be the function that is raised to the power, such as \(u=\cos x\) in \(\sin x\cos^4x\). Its derivative \(u'\) must appear in the integrand, apart from a constant factor. Insert that constant inside the integral and balance it with its reciprocal outside.

Worked example 22 by the formula
\[\begin{aligned}&\int_0^\pi\sin x\cos^4x\,dx\\&\quad=-\int_0^\pi(-\sin x)\cos^4x\,dx\\&\quad=-\Big[\tfrac15\cos^5x\Big]_0^\pi\\&\quad=-\tfrac15\big(\cos^5\pi-\cos^50\big)\\&\quad=-\tfrac15(-1-1)\\&\quad=\tfrac25\end{aligned}\]

Let \(u=\cos x\).
Then \(u'=-\sin x\).

\[\int u^4\,\dfrac{du}{dx}\,dx=\dfrac{u^5}{5}\]

The second line inserts the factor \(-1\) to make \(u'=-\sin x\), and balances it with \(-1\) outside.
