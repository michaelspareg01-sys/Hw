Lesson 6D

Integration of exponential functions

Finding primitives is the reverse of differentiation. Reversing the standard forms for differentiating \(e^x\) and \(e^{ax+b}\) gives the standard forms for integrating them: \(\int e^x\,dx=e^x+C\) and \(\int e^{ax+b}\,dx=\tfrac1a\,e^{ax+b}+C\). With these we evaluate definite integrals, find a function from its derivative and one known value, reverse a known derivative to find a new primitive, and use the reverse chain rule formula from Section 5I.
{{sec:teal:key:Concept 1:standard forms for integration}}
Reversing the standard forms for differentiation

Reversing the standard forms for differentiating exponential functions gives the standard forms for integrating them.

Reversing\(\dfrac{d}{dx}\,e^x=e^x\)gives\(\displaystyle\int e^x\,dx=e^x+C,\)for some constant \(C\).
Reversing\(\dfrac{d}{dx}\,e^{ax+b}=a\,e^{ax+b}\)gives\(\displaystyle\int a\,e^{ax+b}\,dx=e^{ax+b},\)
and dividing through by \(a\),\(\displaystyle\int e^{ax+b}\,dx=\dfrac1a\,e^{ax+b}+C,\)for some constant \(C\).
Where the constant goes

The second line has no \(C\). \(C\) is added in the last line, after dividing by \(a\), because \(C\) divided by \(a\) is just another constant.

4 Standard forms for integration
  • \(\displaystyle\int e^x\,dx=e^x+C\), for some constant \(C\)
  • \(\displaystyle\int e^{ax+b}\,dx=\dfrac1a\,e^{ax+b}+C\), for some constant \(C\)
The reverse chain rule for exponential functions (for reference)

There is also an associated formula for the reverse chain rule, but it is not included in the course. For reference, this formula has the usual two forms:

\[\int e^u\,\dfrac{du}{dx}\,dx=e^u+C\qquad\text{OR}\qquad\int e^{f(x)}f'(x)\,dx=e^{f(x)}+C.\]
When the index is not linear

The reference formula above is the reverse of the chain-rule result \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\,e^{f(x)}=e^{f(x)}f'(x)\). Exercise 6D Q17 uses it.

The standard form \(\int e^{ax+b}\,dx=\tfrac1a\,e^{ax+b}+C\) needs an index of the form \(ax+b\), so that its derivative \(a\) is a constant. Check every answer by differentiating it. For an index such as \(x^2\), first differentiate \(e^{x^2}\) with the chain rule and then reverse the result (Concept 4).

Definite integrals

Definite integrals are evaluated in the usual way by finding the primitive and substituting.

Substituting the limits

Substitute the upper limit, then the lower limit, and subtract. Remember that \(e^0=1\) and \(e^{-1}=\tfrac1e\). Answers are usually left in exact form, and a negative index is often rewritten as a fraction.

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 3:given the derivative, find the function}}
Given the derivative, find the function

As before, if the derivative of a function is known, and the value of the function at one point is also known, then the whole function can be determined.

The three steps
  • Take the primitive of the derivative, including the constant \(C\).
  • Substitute the known value to find \(C\).
  • Write the function with the value of \(C\) in it, then use it.

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 4:given a derivative, find an integral}}
Given a derivative, find an integral

The result of any differentiation can be reversed. This often allows a new primitive to be found.

Reversing a product-rule derivative

In symbols: if \(F'(x)=f(x)\), then \(\int f(x)\,dx=F(x)+C\) and \(\int_a^bf(x)\,dx=\big[F(x)\big]_a^b\).

Sometimes the derivative has two terms and only one of them is the integrand we want (Exercise 6D Q14). For example, by the product rule with \(u=x\) and \(v=e^{2x}\),

\[\dfrac{d}{dx}\big(xe^{2x}\big)=1\times e^{2x}+x\times2e^{2x}=e^{2x}+2xe^{2x}.\]

Reversing this, \(\ \displaystyle\int_0^1\big(e^{2x}+2xe^{2x}\big)\,dx=\Big[xe^{2x}\Big]_0^1\). Split the left-hand side into two integrals and move the known one across:

\[\begin{aligned}\int_0^12xe^{2x}\,dx&=\Big[xe^{2x}\Big]_0^1-\int_0^1e^{2x}\,dx\\&=\big(1\times e^2-0\times e^0\big)-\tfrac12\Big[e^{2x}\Big]_0^1\\&=e^2-\tfrac12\big(e^2-1\big)\\&=\tfrac12\big(e^2+1\big),\end{aligned}\]

so, dividing by 2, \(\ \displaystyle\int_0^1xe^{2x}\,dx=\tfrac14\big(e^2+1\big)\).

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 5:using a formula for the reverse chain rule}}
Using a formula for the reverse chain rule

There are some situations where the reverse chain rule formula from Section 5I can be used.

The formula from Section 5I
\[\int u^n\,\dfrac{du}{dx}\,dx=\dfrac{u^{n+1}}{n+1}+C\qquad\text{OR}\qquad\int\big(f(x)\big)^nf'(x)\,dx=\dfrac{\big(f(x)\big)^{n+1}}{n+1}+C,\]

where \(n\ne-1\). Choose \(u\) (or \(f(x)\)) to be the bracket that is raised to a power. Its derivative \(u'\) must appear in the integrand, apart from a constant factor. Insert that constant inside the integral and balance it with its reciprocal outside.
