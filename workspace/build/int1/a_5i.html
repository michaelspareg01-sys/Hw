a \(\begin{aligned}[t]\text{Let }y&=(x^4-3)^5.\\\text{Then }\frac{dy}{dx}&=\frac{dy}{du}\times\frac{du}{dx}\\&=5(x^4-3)^4\times4x^3\\&=20x^3(x^4-3)^4.\end{aligned}\)
\(\begin{aligned}[t]\text{Let }u&=x^4-3.\\\text{Then }y&=u^5.\\\text{Hence }\frac{du}{dx}&=4x^3\\\text{and }\frac{dy}{du}&=5u^4.\end{aligned}\)

c \(x^3(x^4-3)^4\) is \(20x^3(x^4-3)^4\) divided by 20. Dividing by 20, \(\displaystyle\int x^3(x^4-3)^4\,dx=\tfrac{1}{20}(x^4-3)^5+C\).

a \(u=x^3+1\), \(\dfrac{du}{dx}=3x^2\), \(n=4\): \[\begin{aligned}\int_{-1}^{1}x^2(x^3+1)^4\,dx&=\tfrac13\int_{-1}^{1}3x^2(x^3+1)^4\,dx\\&=\tfrac13\times\tfrac15\Big[(x^3+1)^5\Big]_{-1}^{1}\\&=\tfrac{1}{15}\big((1+1)^5-(-1+1)^5\big)=\tfrac{1}{15}(32-0)=\tfrac{32}{15}\end{aligned}\]
b \(u=5x^2+1\), \(\dfrac{du}{dx}=10x\), \(n=-3\): \[\begin{aligned}\int_0^1\frac{x}{(5x^2+1)^3}\,dx&=\tfrac{1}{10}\int_0^1 10x(5x^2+1)^{-3}\,dx\\&=\tfrac{1}{10}\times\Big(-\tfrac12\Big)\Big[(5x^2+1)^{-2}\Big]_0^1\\&=-\tfrac{1}{20}\big(6^{-2}-1^{-2}\big)=-\tfrac{1}{20}\Big(\tfrac{1}{36}-1\Big)\\&=-\tfrac{1}{20}\times\Big(-\tfrac{35}{36}\Big)=\tfrac{35}{720}=\tfrac{7}{144}\end{aligned}\]

a \(\sqrt{x^2-1}\) needs \(x^2-1\ge0\), so the domain is \(x\le-1\) or \(x\ge1\).
b By the product rule, with \(\dfrac{d}{dx}(x^2-1)^{\frac12}=\tfrac12(x^2-1)^{-\frac12}\times2x\), \[\begin{aligned}f'(x)&=1\times\sqrt{x^2-1}+x\times\tfrac12(x^2-1)^{-\frac12}\times2x\\&=\sqrt{x^2-1}+\frac{x^2}{\sqrt{x^2-1}}\\&=\frac{(x^2-1)+x^2}{\sqrt{x^2-1}}\\&=\frac{2x^2-1}{\sqrt{x^2-1}}\end{aligned}\] \(f'(x)=0\) only when \(2x^2-1=0\), that is, \(x=\pm\dfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\). These values are not in the domain, because \(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt2}<1\). (At \(x=\pm1\), \(f'(x)\) is undefined.) So there are no stationary points.
c \(f(-x)=(-x)\sqrt{(-x)^2-1}=-x\sqrt{x^2-1}=-f(x)\), so \(f\) is odd and its graph has point symmetry about the origin.
For \(x\ge1\), \(f(x)\ge0\), with \(f(1)=0\), and \(f'(x)>0\) for \(x>1\) (since \(2x^2-1>0\)), so the curve rises. The branch for \(x\le-1\) is the rotation of this branch through \(180^\circ\) about the origin.

d On \(1\le x\le3\), \(f(x)\ge0\), and \(8^{\frac32}=8\sqrt8=8\times2\sqrt2=16\sqrt2\). \(u=x^2-1\), \(\dfrac{du}{dx}=2x\), \(n=\tfrac12\). \[\begin{aligned}\text{Area}&=\int_1^3x\sqrt{x^2-1}\,dx=\tfrac12\int_1^3 2x(x^2-1)^{\frac12}\,dx\\&=\tfrac12\times\tfrac23\Big[(x^2-1)^{\frac32}\Big]_1^3\\&=\tfrac13\Big(8^{\frac32}-0^{\frac32}\Big)=\tfrac13\times16\sqrt2=\tfrac{16\sqrt2}{3}\text{ u}^2\end{aligned}\]

a Intercepts: \(y=0\) when \(x=0\) or \(7-x^2=0\), that is, at \((0,0)\), \((\sqrt7,0)\) and \((-\sqrt7,0)\). By the product rule, \[\begin{aligned}y'&=1\times(7-x^2)^3+x\times3(7-x^2)^2\times(-2x)\\&=(7-x^2)^2\big((7-x^2)-6x^2\big)\\&=(7-x^2)^2(7-7x^2)\\&=7(7-x^2)^2(1-x^2)\end{aligned}\] So \(y'=0\) at \(x=\pm1\) and \(x=\pm\sqrt7\). At \(x=1\), \(y=1\times6^3=216\); at \(x=-1\), \(y=-216\).
Since \((7-x^2)^2\ge0\), \(y'\) has the sign of \(1-x^2\): positive for \(-1<x<1\), and negative for \(|x|>1\) (apart from the zeroes at \(\pm\sqrt7\)).
So \((1,216)\) is a maximum turning point and \((-1,-216)\) is a minimum turning point. At \(x=\pm\sqrt7\), \(y'\) is negative on both sides, so \((\sqrt7,0)\) and \((-\sqrt7,0)\) are horizontal points of inflection.

b The curve is odd: \(y\ge0\) for \(0\le x\le\sqrt7\) and \(y\le0\) for \(-\sqrt7\le x\le0\), so the two parts have equal area. \(u=7-x^2\), \(\dfrac{du}{dx}=-2x\), \(n=3\). \[\begin{aligned}\text{Area}&=2\int_0^{\sqrt7}x(7-x^2)^3\,dx\\&=2\times\Big(-\tfrac12\Big)\int_0^{\sqrt7}(-2x)(7-x^2)^3\,dx\\&=-\tfrac14\Big[(7-x^2)^4\Big]_0^{\sqrt7}\\&=-\tfrac14\big(0^4-7^4\big)=\tfrac14\times2401=\tfrac{2401}{4}=600\tfrac14\text{ u}^2\end{aligned}\]

The derivative of \(1-x^2\) is \(-2x\), and \(x=-\tfrac12\times(-2x)\). \(u=1-x^2\), \(\dfrac{du}{dx}=-2x\), \(n=-\tfrac12\). \[\begin{aligned}\int\frac{x}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}\,dx&=-\tfrac12\int(-2x)(1-x^2)^{-\frac12}\,dx\\&=-\tfrac12\times\frac{(1-x^2)^{\frac12}}{\frac12}+c\\&=-\tfrac12\times2(1-x^2)^{\frac12}+c\\&=-\sqrt{1-x^2}+c\end{aligned}\]

The answer is D. Option C is half of D, so its derivative is \(\dfrac{x}{2\sqrt{1-x^2}}\), half the integrand. Options A and B are not \(-\sqrt{1-x^2}\) plus a constant.

PP 2Sydney Boys High School, 2020, Year 12 Term 3 Trial HSC, Mathematics Extension 1, Q101 mark
The derivative of \(9-x^2\) is \(-2x\), and \(x=-\tfrac12\times(-2x)\). \(u=9-x^2\), \(\dfrac{du}{dx}=-2x\), \(n=-\tfrac12\). \[\begin{aligned}\int\frac{x}{\sqrt{9-x^2}}\,dx&=-\tfrac12\int(-2x)(9-x^2)^{-\frac12}\,dx\\&=-\tfrac12\times\frac{(9-x^2)^{\frac12}}{\frac12}+C\\&=-\tfrac12\times2(9-x^2)^{\frac12}+C\\&=-\sqrt{9-x^2}+C\end{aligned}\]

The answer is A. Check: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\Big(-\sqrt{9-x^2}\Big)=-\tfrac12(9-x^2)^{-\frac12}\times(-2x)=\dfrac{x}{\sqrt{9-x^2}}\). Option B differentiates to twice the integrand, option D to \(-2\) times it, and option C to the opposite of the integrand.
