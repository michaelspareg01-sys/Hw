Lesson 5I

The reverse chain rule

When the chain rule is used to differentiate a composite function, the result is a product of two terms. This lesson reverses a chain-rule differentiation to find a primitive. A formula for the reverse chain rule is then developed in two notations, using \(u\) or using \(f(x)\). The vital step is to identify \(u\) or \(f(x)\) and its derivative in the function that is being integrated. The lesson ends with the Chapter 5 review exercise.
{{sec:teal:key:Concept 1:reversing a chain-rule differentiation}}
The reverse chain rule

When we use the chain rule to differentiate a composite function, the result is a product of two terms. For example, in the first worked example below,

\[\frac{d}{dx}(x^3+5)^4=4(x^3+5)^3\times3x^2.\]

This section deals with the problem of reversing a chain-rule differentiation.

Reversing a chain-rule differentiation

Finding primitives is the reverse process of differentiation. Thus once any differentiation has been performed, the process can then be reversed to give a primitive.

The chain rule (from Year 11)

The revision pages at the start of this pack set out the chain rule. To differentiate \(y=(x^3+5)^4\), work on the right first: let \(u=x^3+5\), so that \(y=u^4\). Then \(\dfrac{du}{dx}=3x^2\) and \(\dfrac{dy}{du}=4u^3\). On the left, multiply them, and put \(u=x^3+5\) back:

\[\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{dy}{du}\times\frac{du}{dx}=4u^3\times3x^2=4(x^3+5)^3\times3x^2.\]

The two terms of the product are the derivative of the outer power, \(4(x^3+5)^3\), and the derivative of the inside function, \(3x^2\).

Reading a derivative backwards

If \(\dfrac{d}{dx}F(x)=g(x)\), then \(F(x)\) is a primitive of \(g(x)\), so \(\displaystyle\int g(x)\,dx=F(x)+C\). If the function to be integrated is \(g(x)\) divided by a number \(k\), then its primitive is \(F(x)\) divided by \(k\):

\[\int\frac{g(x)}{k}\,dx=\frac{F(x)}{k}+C,\qquad\text{because}\qquad\frac{d}{dx}\Big(\frac{F(x)}{k}\Big)=\frac{g(x)}{k}.\]

aDifferentiate \((x^3+5)^4\) with full setting-out of the chain rule. bHence find a primitive of \(12x^2(x^3+5)^3\). cHence find the primitive of \(x^2(x^3+5)^3\).
Solution
a \(\begin{aligned}[t]\text{Let }y&=(x^3+5)^4.\\\text{Then }\frac{dy}{dx}&=\frac{dy}{du}\times\frac{du}{dx}\\&=4(x^3+5)^3\times3x^2\\&=12x^2(x^3+5)^3.\end{aligned}\)
\(\begin{aligned}[t]\text{Let }u&=x^3+5.\\\text{Then }y&=u^4.\\\text{Hence }\frac{du}{dx}&=3x^2\\\text{and }\frac{dy}{du}&=4u^3.\end{aligned}\)

b By part a, \(\quad\dfrac{d}{dx}(x^3+5)^4=12x^2(x^3+5)^3.\)

Reversing this, \(\quad\displaystyle\int 12x^2(x^3+5)^3\,dx=(x^3+5)^4.\)

c Dividing by 12, \(\quad\displaystyle\int x^2(x^3+5)^3\,dx=\tfrac{1}{12}(x^3+5)^4+C,\qquad\text{for some constant }C.\)

Adding the constant

Note: It is best not to add the arbitrary constant until the last line, because it would be pointless to divide \(C\) by 12 as well.

Steps the book skips: Worked example 36

a On the right, \(\dfrac{dy}{du}=4u^3\). Putting \(u=x^3+5\) back gives \(4u^3=4(x^3+5)^3\), and this is the factor used on the left. Then \(4(x^3+5)^3\times3x^2=4\times3\times x^2(x^3+5)^3=12x^2(x^3+5)^3\).

b Reversing the line in part a means that \((x^3+5)^4\) is a primitive of \(12x^2(x^3+5)^3\). Any constant could be added; the book leaves it until part c.

c The function \(x^2(x^3+5)^3\) is \(12x^2(x^3+5)^3\) divided by 12, so its primitive is \((x^3+5)^4\) divided by 12: \(\displaystyle\int x^2(x^3+5)^3\,dx=\tfrac{1}{12}\int 12x^2(x^3+5)^3\,dx=\tfrac{1}{12}(x^3+5)^4+C\). Check: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\Big(\tfrac{1}{12}(x^3+5)^4\Big)=\tfrac{1}{12}\times12x^2(x^3+5)^3=x^2(x^3+5)^3\).

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 2:reversing with a negative index}}
A fraction is a negative power

To differentiate a fraction such as \(\dfrac{1}{1+x^2}\) by the chain rule, write it as \((1+x^2)^{-1}\). Let \(u=1+x^2\), so that \(y=u^{-1}\) and \(\dfrac{dy}{du}=-u^{-2}\). The answer is written as a fraction again: \(-(1+x^2)^{-2}=-\dfrac{1}{(1+x^2)^2}\).

When the derivative is reversed, the number to divide by may be negative. Dividing by \(-2\) is the same as multiplying by \(-\tfrac12\).

21 Reversing a chain-rule differentiation

Once a chain-rule differentiation, or any differentiation, has been performed, the result can be written down in reverse as an indefinite integral.

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 3:a formula for the reverse chain rule}}
A formula for the reverse chain rule

There is a formula for the reverse chain rule. Start with the formula for differentiating a function using the chain rule — we gave the formula in two forms:

\[\frac{d}{dx}(u^n)=nu^{n-1}\frac{du}{dx}\qquad\qquad\frac{d}{dx}\big(f(x)\big)^n=n\big(f(x)\big)^{n-1}f'(x)\]

and we can reverse both forms of the formula,

\[\int u^{n-1}\frac{du}{dx}\,dx=\frac{u^n}{n}\qquad\qquad\int\big(f(x)\big)^{n-1}f'(x)\,dx=\frac{\big(f(x)\big)^n}{n}.\]

Then replacing \(n-1\) by \(n\) and \(n\) by \(n+1\),

\[\int u^n\frac{du}{dx}\,dx=\frac{u^{n+1}}{n+1}\qquad\text{OR}\qquad\int\big(f(x)\big)^nf'(x)\,dx=\frac{\big(f(x)\big)^{n+1}}{n+1}.\]

Take your pick which formula you prefer to use. The difficult part is recognising what \(u\) or \(f(x)\) should be in the function that you are integrating.

We shall do Worked example 36c again using the formula.

Why the formula works

Differentiate the right-hand side by the chain rule: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\Big(\dfrac{u^{n+1}}{n+1}\Big)=\dfrac{(n+1)u^n}{n+1}\times\dfrac{du}{dx}=u^n\dfrac{du}{dx}\), which is the function inside the integral. The index on the right is one more than the index on the left, and we divide by that new index. So the index \(n=-1\) cannot be used, because then \(n+1=0\) (as in Lesson 5E).

Using the formula, step by step
  1. Choose \(u\) (or \(f(x)\)): the function inside the power.
  2. Find \(\dfrac{du}{dx}\) (or \(f'(x)\)) on the right-hand side.
  3. If the other factor is a multiple of \(\dfrac{du}{dx}\), multiply inside the integral by a number and outside by its reciprocal, so that \(\dfrac{du}{dx}\) appears exactly.
  4. Write the standard form with the particular value of \(n\), then simplify the numbers in front.

Use the formula for the reverse chain rule to find \(\displaystyle\int x^2(x^3+5)^3\,dx\).
Solution

The key to finding this integral is to realise that \(x^2\) is a multiple of the derivative of \(x^3+5\). At this point, we can put \(u=x^3+5\) or \(f(x)=x^3+5\), and everything works smoothly after that. Here are two strong recommendations:

  • Show working identifying \(u\) or \(f(x)\) and its derivative on the right-hand side.
  • Write down the standard form above substituting the particular value of \(n\).

Using the first formula,

\(\begin{aligned}[t]\int x^2(x^3+5)^3\,dx&=\tfrac13\int 3x^2(x^3+5)^3\,dx\\&=\tfrac13\times\tfrac14(x^3+5)^4+C,\\&\quad\text{for some constant }C,\\&=\tfrac{1}{12}(x^3+5)^4+C.\end{aligned}\)
\(\begin{aligned}[t]\text{Let }u&=x^3+5.\\\text{Then }\frac{du}{dx}&=3x^2.\end{aligned}\)
\(\begin{aligned}[t]&\text{Here }\int u^3\frac{du}{dx}\,dx\\&\quad=\tfrac14u^4.\end{aligned}\)

Using the second formula,

\(\begin{aligned}[t]\int x^2(x^3+5)^3\,dx&=\tfrac13\int 3x^2(x^3+5)^3\,dx\\&=\tfrac13\times\tfrac14(x^3+5)^4+C,\\&\quad\text{for some constant }C,\\&=\tfrac{1}{12}(x^3+5)^4+C.\end{aligned}\)
\(\begin{aligned}[t]\text{Let }f(x)&=x^3+5.\\\text{Then }f'(x)&=3x^2.\end{aligned}\)
\(\begin{aligned}[t]&\text{Here }\int\big(f(x)\big)^3f'(x)\,dx\\&\quad=\tfrac14\big(f(x)\big)^4.\end{aligned}\)
Two notations

Notice that the two notations differ only in the working in the right-hand column.

Steps the book skips: Worked example 38

First line. The derivative of \(x^3+5\) is \(3x^2\), and \(x^2=\tfrac13\times3x^2\). Multiplying inside the integral by 3 and outside by \(\tfrac13\) does not change it, because \(\tfrac13\times3=1\): \(\displaystyle\int x^2(x^3+5)^3\,dx=\tfrac13\int 3x^2(x^3+5)^3\,dx\).

Second line. Here \(n=3\), so the formula gives \(\dfrac{u^{3+1}}{3+1}=\tfrac14u^4\), and \(u^4=(x^3+5)^4\). Third line. \(\tfrac13\times\tfrac14=\tfrac{1}{12}\). This is the answer to Worked example 36c.

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 4:fractional indices, and where the integrand is defined}}
A square root is a power

Write \(\sqrt{1-x^2}=(1-x^2)^{\frac12}\), so that \(n=\tfrac12\) and the new index is \(n+1=\tfrac32\). Dividing by \(\tfrac32\) is the same as multiplying by \(\tfrac23\).

A definite integral needs the integrand to be defined at every point of the interval. Since \(\sqrt{1-x^2}\) needs \(1-x^2\ge0\), it is defined only for \(-1\le x\le1\). For a definite integral, the primitive goes in square brackets, with no constant, and the limits are substituted (Lesson 5B).

\(\begin{aligned}[t]\int x\sqrt{1-x^2}\,dx&=-\tfrac12\int(-2x)\times(1-x^2)^{\frac12}\,dx\\&=-\tfrac12\times\tfrac23(1-x^2)^{\frac32}+C,\\&\quad\text{for some constant }C,\\&=-\tfrac13(1-x^2)^{\frac32}+C.\end{aligned}\)

Steps the book skips: Worked example 39

a The derivative of \(1-x^2\) is \(-2x\), and \(x=-\tfrac12\times(-2x)\), so multiply inside by \(-2\) and outside by \(-\tfrac12\). Here \(n=\tfrac12\), so the formula gives \(\dfrac{u^{\frac32}}{\frac32}=\tfrac23u^{\frac32}\). Then \(-\tfrac12\times\tfrac23=-\tfrac13\).

b The interval \(0\le x\le2\) includes the values \(1<x\le2\), where \(1-x^2<0\). For example, at \(x=2\), \(1-x^2=1-4=-3\), which has no square root.

22 A formula for the reverse chain rule
  • The reversing of the chain rule can be written as a formula in two ways:
    \[\int u^n\frac{du}{dx}\,dx=\frac{u^{n+1}}{n+1}+C\quad\text{OR}\quad\int\big(f(x)\big)^nf'(x)\,dx=\frac{\big(f(x)\big)^{n+1}}{n+1}+C.\]
  • The vital step in using this formula is to identify \(u\) or \(f(x)\) and its derivative.
Writing the integrand as a product

To use the formula, we have to write the integrand as a product. One factor is a power of a function \(f(x)\) or \(u\). The other factor is the derivative of that function.
