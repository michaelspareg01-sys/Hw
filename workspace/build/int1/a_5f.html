Lesson Examples
C1
Tutor demo. The curve meets the \(x\)-axis where \(9-x^2=0\), at \((3,0)\) and \((-3,0)\). The region lies entirely above the \(x\)-axis, and the relevant integral is \[\begin{aligned}\int_{-3}^{3}(9-x^2)\,dx&=\Big[9x-\frac{x^3}{3}\Big]_{-3}^{3}\\&=\big(27-9\big)-\big(-27+9\big)\\&=18-(-18)\\&=36,\end{aligned}\] which is positive. Hence the required area is \(36\) square units.
Your turn. The curve meets the \(x\)-axis where \(1-x^2=0\), at \((1,0)\) and \((-1,0)\). The region lies entirely above the \(x\)-axis, and \[\begin{aligned}\int_{-1}^{1}(1-x^2)\,dx&=\Big[x-\frac{x^3}{3}\Big]_{-1}^{1}\\&=\big(1-\tfrac13\big)-\big(-1+\tfrac13\big)\\&=\tfrac23-\big(-\tfrac23\big)\\&=1\tfrac13.\end{aligned}\] Hence the required area is \(1\tfrac13\) square units.
C2
Tutor demo. The curve meets the \(x\)-axis where \(x^2-4=0\), at \((2,0)\) and \((-2,0)\). The region lies entirely below the \(x\)-axis, and \[\begin{aligned}\int_{-2}^{2}(x^2-4)\,dx&=\Big[\frac{x^3}{3}-4x\Big]_{-2}^{2}\\&=\big(\tfrac83-8\big)-\big(-\tfrac83+8\big)\\&=-\tfrac{16}{3}-\tfrac{16}{3}\\&=-10\tfrac23,\end{aligned}\] which is negative, because the curve lies below the \(x\)-axis. Hence the required area is \(10\tfrac23\) square units.
Your turn. The curve meets the \(x\)-axis at \((3,0)\) and \((-3,0)\), and the region lies entirely below the \(x\)-axis. \[\begin{aligned}\int_{-3}^{3}(x^2-9)\,dx&=\Big[\frac{x^3}{3}-9x\Big]_{-3}^{3}\\&=(9-27)-(-9+27)\\&=-18-18\\&=-36,\end{aligned}\] so the required area is \(36\) square units.
C3
Tutor demo.
a The \(x\)-intercepts are \(x=-1\), \(x=0\) and \(x=3\). The cubic has a positive leading coefficient, so it is above the axis for \(-1<x<0\) and below it for \(0<x<3\).
b Expanding, \(y=x(x^2-3x+x-3)=x(x^2-2x-3)=x^3-2x^2-3x\). For the region above the \(x\)-axis, \[\begin{aligned}\int_{-1}^{0}(x^3-2x^2-3x)\,dx&=\Big[\frac{x^4}{4}-\frac{2x^3}{3}-\frac{3x^2}{2}\Big]_{-1}^{0}\\&=(0-0-0)-\big(\tfrac14+\tfrac23-\tfrac32\big)\\&=-\big(\tfrac{3}{12}+\tfrac{8}{12}-\tfrac{18}{12}\big)\\&=\tfrac{7}{12},\end{aligned}\] so area above \(=\tfrac{7}{12}\) square units. For the region below the \(x\)-axis, \[\begin{aligned}\int_{0}^{3}(x^3-2x^2-3x)\,dx&=\Big[\frac{x^4}{4}-\frac{2x^3}{3}-\frac{3x^2}{2}\Big]_{0}^{3}\\&=\big(\tfrac{81}{4}-18-\tfrac{27}{2}\big)-(0-0-0)\\&=\tfrac{81}{4}-\tfrac{72}{4}-\tfrac{54}{4}\\&=-11\tfrac14,\end{aligned}\] so area below \(=11\tfrac14\) square units. Total area \(=\tfrac{7}{12}+11\tfrac14=\tfrac{7}{12}+\tfrac{135}{12}=\tfrac{142}{12}=11\tfrac56\) square units.
c \[\begin{aligned}\int_{-1}^{3}x(x+1)(x-3)\,dx&=\Big[\frac{x^4}{4}-\frac{2x^3}{3}-\frac{3x^2}{2}\Big]_{-1}^{3}\\&=-11\tfrac14+\tfrac{7}{12}\\&=-\tfrac{135}{12}+\tfrac{7}{12}\\&=-10\tfrac23.\end{aligned}\] This integral is the difference \(11\tfrac14-\tfrac{7}{12}=10\tfrac23\) of the two areas, and is negative because the area below is larger than the area above.
Your turn.
a The \(x\)-intercepts are \(x=-1\), \(x=0\) and \(x=4\); the curve is above the axis for \(-1<x<0\) and below it for \(0<x<4\).
b \(y=x(x^2-3x-4)=x^3-3x^2-4x\), with primitive \(\tfrac{x^4}{4}-x^3-2x^2\). \[\begin{aligned}\int_{-1}^{0}(x^3-3x^2-4x)\,dx&=\Big[\frac{x^4}{4}-x^3-2x^2\Big]_{-1}^{0}\\&=0-\big(\tfrac14+1-2\big)\\&=\tfrac34,\end{aligned}\] \[\begin{aligned}\int_{0}^{4}(x^3-3x^2-4x)\,dx&=\Big[\frac{x^4}{4}-x^3-2x^2\Big]_{0}^{4}\\&=(64-64-32)-0\\&=-32,\end{aligned}\] so the areas are \(\tfrac34\) and \(32\), and the total area is \(32\tfrac34\) square units.
c \(\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{4}x(x+1)(x-4)\,dx=-32+\tfrac34=-31\tfrac14\). It is the difference \(32-\tfrac34\) of the two areas, negative because the area below is larger.
C4
Tutor demo.
a Let \(f(x)=x^3-9x\). Then \(f(-x)=(-x)^3-9(-x)=-x^3+9x=-f(x)\), so \(f(x)\) is odd.
b \(y=x(x^2-9)=x(x-3)(x+3)\), so the \(x\)-intercepts are \(x=-3\), \(x=0\) and \(x=3\). The two regions have equal areas because the function is odd. \[\begin{aligned}\int_0^3(x^3-9x)\,dx&=\Big[\frac{x^4}{4}-\frac{9x^2}{2}\Big]_0^3\\&=\big(\tfrac{81}{4}-\tfrac{81}{2}\big)-(0-0)\\&=-\tfrac{81}{4},\end{aligned}\] so area below the \(x\)-axis \(=\tfrac{81}{4}\) square units. Doubling, total area \(=\tfrac{81}{2}=40\tfrac12\) square units.
Your turn.
a \(f(-x)=(-x)^3-4(-x)=-x^3+4x=-f(x)\), so \(f(x)=x^3-4x\) is odd.
b \(y=x(x-2)(x+2)\), with \(x\)-intercepts \(-2\), \(0\) and \(2\). \[\begin{aligned}\int_0^2(x^3-4x)\,dx&=\Big[\frac{x^4}{4}-2x^2\Big]_0^2\\&=(4-8)-(0-0)\\&=-4,\end{aligned}\] so area below \(=4\) square units, and doubling, total area \(=8\) square units.
C5
Tutor demo.
a The lines meet where \(2x+1=7\), that is, at \((3,7)\). The line \(y=2x+1\) crosses the \(y\)-axis at \((0,1)\). The region is the triangle with vertices \((0,1)\), \((0,7)\) and \((3,7)\).
b \(y=2x+1\), so \(2x=y-1\) and \(x=\tfrac12y-\tfrac12\).
c \[\begin{aligned}\int_1^7\big(\tfrac12y-\tfrac12\big)\,dy&=\Big[\frac{y^2}{4}-\frac{y}{2}\Big]_1^7\\&=\Big(\frac{49}{4}-\frac72\Big)-\Big(\frac14-\frac12\Big)\\&=\frac{35}{4}-\Big(-\frac14\Big)\\&=9,\end{aligned}\] which is positive, because the region is to the right of the \(y\)-axis. The area is \(9\ \text{u}^2\).
d Area of triangle \(=\tfrac12\times3\times6=9\ \text{u}^2\) (base \(3-0=3\) along \(y=7\), height \(7-1=6\)).
Your turn.
a The lines meet where \(x+2=5\), at \((3,5)\); the line crosses the \(y\)-axis at \((0,2)\).
b \(x=y-2\).
c \[\begin{aligned}\int_2^5(y-2)\,dy&=\Big[\frac{y^2}{2}-2y\Big]_2^5\\&=\Big(\frac{25}{2}-10\Big)-(2-4)\\&=\tfrac52+2\\&=4\tfrac12,\end{aligned}\] so the area is \(4\tfrac12\ \text{u}^2\).
d Area of triangle \(=\tfrac12\times3\times3=4\tfrac12\ \text{u}^2\).
C6
Tutor demo.
a \(y=x^3\), so \(x^3=y\) and \(x=y^{\frac13}\).
b For the region to the right of the \(y\)-axis, \[\begin{aligned}\int_0^{27}y^{\frac13}\,dy&=\tfrac34\Big[y^{\frac43}\Big]_0^{27}\\&=\tfrac34\times(81-0)\qquad\big(27^{\frac43}=3^4=81\big)\\&=60\tfrac34,\end{aligned}\] so area \(=60\tfrac34\) square units. For the region to the left of the \(y\)-axis, \[\begin{aligned}\int_{-8}^{0}y^{\frac13}\,dy&=\tfrac34\Big[y^{\frac43}\Big]_{-8}^{0}\\&=\tfrac34\times(0-16)\qquad\big((-8)^{\frac43}=(-2)^4=16\big)\\&=-12,\end{aligned}\] so area \(=12\) square units.
c Total area \(=60\tfrac34+12=72\tfrac34\) square units.
Your turn.
a \(x^5=y\), so \(x=y^{\frac15}\).
b A primitive of \(y^{\frac15}\) is \(\dfrac{y^{\frac65}}{\frac65}=\tfrac56y^{\frac65}\). \[\begin{aligned}\int_0^{1}y^{\frac15}\,dy&=\tfrac56\Big[y^{\frac65}\Big]_0^{1}\\&=\tfrac56\times(1-0)\\&=\tfrac56,\end{aligned}\] \[\begin{aligned}\int_{-1}^{0}y^{\frac15}\,dy&=\tfrac56\Big[y^{\frac65}\Big]_{-1}^{0}\\&=\tfrac56\times(0-1)\\&=-\tfrac56,\end{aligned}\] using \((-1)^{\frac65}=(-1)^6=1\). The areas are \(\tfrac56\) and \(\tfrac56\) square units.
c Total area \(=\tfrac56+\tfrac56=1\tfrac23\) square units.

The region is bounded by the \(y\)-axis and the horizontal lines \(y=1\) and \(y=2\), so integrate with respect to \(y\), with limits \(y=1\) and \(y=2\) (Box 16). Write \(x\) as a function of \(y\):

\[\begin{aligned}y&=x^3-1\\x^3&=y+1\\x&=\sqrt[3]{y+1}.\end{aligned}\]

For \(1\le y\le2\), \(y+1\) is positive, so \(x=\sqrt[3]{y+1}\) is positive and the region lies to the right of the \(y\)-axis. Hence the integral is the area itself:

\[\text{area}=\int_1^2\sqrt[3]{y+1}\,dy.\]

Answer (B). Option (A) still has the integrand written in terms of \(x\). Options (C) and (D) are not the inverse of \(y=x^3-1\): for example, (D) uses \(x=y+1\), which is the inverse of \(y=x-1\).
