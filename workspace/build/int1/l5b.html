Lesson 5B

The fundamental theorem of calculus

There is a remarkably simple formula for evaluating definite integrals, based on taking the primitive of the function. The formula is called the fundamental theorem of calculus, because the whole of calculus depends on it. This lesson states the theorem and uses it: write down a primitive, substitute the upper and lower limits, and subtract. Expanding brackets, splitting fractions and negative indices prepare the integrand first. The challenging proof is delayed until Lesson 5D, when the usefulness of the theorem will have been established.
{{sec:teal:key:Concept 1:the fundamental theorem of calculus}}
Primitives

Let us first review from Lesson 4J what primitives are, and the first step in finding them.

3 Primitives
  • A function \(F(x)\) is called a primitive or anti-derivative of a function \(f(x)\) if its derivative is \(f(x)\): \[F(x)\text{ is a primitive of }f(x)\text{ if }F'(x)=f(x).\]
  • To find the general primitive of a power \(x^n\), where \(n\ne-1\): \[\text{If }\frac{dy}{dx}=x^n,\ \text{ then }y=\frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1}+C,\ \text{ for some constant }C.\]

‘Increase the index by 1 and divide by the new index.’

Statement of the fundamental theorem

The fundamental theorem says that a definite integral can be evaluated by writing down any primitive \(F(x)\) of \(f(x)\), then substituting the upper and lower limits into it and subtracting.

4 The fundamental theorem of calculus

Let \(f(x)\) be a function that is continuous in a closed interval \([a,b]\). Then

\[\int_a^b f(x)\,dx=F(b)-F(a),\quad\text{where }F(x)\text{ is any primitive of }f(x).\]
Areas and tangents are inverse processes

This result is extraordinary because it says that taking areas and taking tangents are inverse processes, which is not obvious. The definite integral was defined in Lesson 5A as an area. Its value comes from a primitive, that is, from reversing differentiation, and differentiation is about gradients of tangents.

Using the fundamental theorem to evaluate an integral

The conventional way to set out these calculations is to enclose the primitive in square brackets, writing the lower and upper limits as subscript and superscript respectively:

\[\int_a^b f(x)\,dx=\Big[F(x)\Big]_a^b=F(b)-F(a).\]
Why any primitive will do

Every primitive of \(f(x)\) has the form \(F(x)+C\). With the constant included,

\[\Big[F(x)+C\Big]_a^b=\big(F(b)+C\big)-\big(F(a)+C\big)=F(b)-F(a),\]

because the two constants cancel. So the constant is left out when evaluating a definite integral.

Setting out
  1. Write the primitive in square brackets, with the limits at the right-hand bracket.
  2. Substitute the upper limit, then substitute the lower limit, and subtract: (value at \(b\)) \(-\) (value at \(a\)).
  3. Simplify the arithmetic one line at a time.

a\(\displaystyle\int_0^2 2x\,dx\)b\(\displaystyle\int_2^4(2x-3)\,dx\)
Solution

a \(x^2\) is a primitive of \(2x\), because \(\dfrac{d}{dx}x^2=2x\).

\[\begin{aligned}\int_0^2 2x\,dx&=\Big[x^2\Big]_0^2\\&=2^2-0^2\qquad\text{(substitute 2, then substitute 0 and subtract)}\\&=4-0\\&=4.\end{aligned}\]

Using areas: the region is a triangle with base \(2-0=2\) and height \(2\times2=4\) (the value of \(2x\) at \(x=2\)).

\[\begin{aligned}\text{area of triangle}&=\tfrac12\times2\times4\\&=4.\end{aligned}\]
{{svg:c5b_e2a}}

b Take the primitive of each term: \(x^2\) for \(2x\), and \(-3x\) for \(-3\).

\[\begin{aligned}\int_2^4(2x-3)\,dx&=\Big[x^2-3x\Big]_2^4\\&=\big(4^2-3\times4\big)-\big(2^2-3\times2\big)\qquad\text{(substitute 4, then 2)}\\&=(16-12)-(4-6)\\&=4-(-2)\\&=6.\end{aligned}\]

Using areas: the region is a trapezium with width \(4-2=2\) and parallel sides \(2\times2-3=1\) and \(2\times4-3=5\).

\[\begin{aligned}\text{area of trapezium}&=\frac{1+5}{2}\times2\\&=3\times2\\&=6.\end{aligned}\]

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 2:primitives with several terms}}
Brackets are needed

Note: Whenever the primitive has two or more terms, brackets are needed when substituting the upper and lower limits of integration.

The minus sign between the brackets subtracts the whole value at the lower limit. For example, \((16-12)-(4-6)=4-(-2)=6\), whereas leaving the brackets out would give \(16-12-4-6=-6\), which is wrong.

Never omit a substitution of 0

When the lower limit is 0, still write the bracket \((0-0)\) for it. Many primitives give 0 at \(x=0\), but not all of them do. For example, using Lesson 4J, a primitive of \((x+1)^2\) is \(\tfrac13(x+1)^3\), and

\[\Big[\tfrac13(x+1)^3\Big]_0^1=\tfrac13\times2^3-\tfrac13\times1^3=\tfrac83-\tfrac13=\tfrac73,\]

where the value at 0 is \(\tfrac13\), not 0.

Powers of negative numbers and of fractions

Put each negative number in brackets before taking a power: \((-2)^2=4\), \((-2)^3=-8\), \((-1)^4=1\). For fractions, take the power of the numerator and of the denominator: \(\big(\tfrac23\big)^3=\tfrac{8}{27}\).

Evaluate these definite integrals.
a\(\displaystyle\int_0^1 5x^2\,dx\)b\(\displaystyle\int_0^4(25-x^2)\,dx\)
Solution

a Increase the index from 2 to 3, then divide by 3.

\[\begin{aligned}\int_0^1 5x^2\,dx&=\Big[\tfrac53x^3\Big]_0^1\\&=\tfrac53\times1^3-\tfrac53\times0^3\qquad\text{(substitute 1, then substitute 0 and subtract)}\\&=\tfrac53-0\\&=\tfrac53.\end{aligned}\]

b The primitive of \(25\) is \(25x\), and the primitive of \(x^2\) is \(\tfrac13x^3\).

\[\begin{aligned}\int_0^4(25-x^2)\,dx&=\Big[25x-\tfrac13x^3\Big]_0^4\\&=\big(25\times4-\tfrac13\times4^3\big)-\big(25\times0-\tfrac13\times0^3\big)\\&=\big(100-\tfrac13\times64\big)-(0-0)\qquad\text{(never omit a substitution of 0)}\\&=100-\tfrac{64}{3}\\&=\tfrac{300}{3}-\tfrac{64}{3}\\&=\tfrac{236}{3}\\&=78\tfrac23.\end{aligned}\]
{{sec:teal:key:Concept 3:expanding brackets in the integrand}}
Expand first

As with differentiation, it is often necessary to expand the brackets in the integrand before finding a primitive. With integration, there is no ‘product rule’ that could avoid the expansion.

Do not integrate each factor separately

The primitive of a product is not the product of the primitives. For example, \(x(x+1)\) is not integrated by multiplying \(\tfrac12x^2\) by \(\tfrac12x^2+x\). Expand to \(x^2+x\) first, then take the primitive of each term.

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 4:writing the integrand as separate fractions}}
Split the fraction first

If the integrand is a fraction with two or more terms in the numerator, it should normally be written as separate fractions, as with differentiation. With integration, there is no ‘quotient rule’ that could avoid this.

\[\frac{3x^4-2x^2}{x^2}=\frac{3x^4}{x^2}-\frac{2x^2}{x^2}=3x^2-2.\]
Dividing powers

Divide each term of the numerator by the denominator, subtracting the indices: \(\dfrac{x^4}{x^2}=x^{4-2}=x^2\), \(\dfrac{x^3}{x^3}=x^0=1\), \(\dfrac{x^4}{x^3}=x\). Do not divide only the first term.

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 5:negative indices, and asymptotes}}
Negative indices

The fundamental theorem also works when the indices are negative. Care is needed when converting between negative powers of \(x\) and fractions.

\[\frac{1}{x^4}=x^{-4},\qquad\frac{x^{-3}}{-3}=-\frac{1}{3x^3}\quad(\text{the 3 stays with }x^3\text{ in the denominator}).\]
The index \(-1\) cannot be handled by this rule

Note: The negative index \(-1\) cannot be handled by this rule, because it would generate division by 0, which is nonsense:

\[\int_1^2\frac1x\,dx=\int_1^2x^{-1}\,dx=\Big[\frac{x^0}{0}\Big]_1^2=\text{nonsense}.\]

Chapter 6 on exponential and logarithmic functions will handle this integral.

Warning: do not integrate across an asymptote

The following calculation seems just as valid as part b of Worked example 5 below:

\[\begin{aligned}\int_{-1}^{1}x^{-4}\,dx&=\Big[\frac{x^{-3}}{-3}\Big]_{-1}^{1}\\&=-\frac{1}{3\times1^3}-\Big(-\frac{1}{3\times(-1)^3}\Big)\\&=-\frac13-\frac13\\&=-\frac23.\end{aligned}\]
{{svg:c5b_asym}}

But in fact the calculation is nonsense. The function has an asymptote at \(x=0\), so it is not even defined there. (And the function \(y=x^{-4}\) is always positive, so how could it give a negative answer for an integral?) You cannot integrate across an asymptote, and you always need to be on the lookout for such meaningless integrals.

Box 4 needs \(f(x)\) to be continuous in the whole closed interval \([a,b]\). Before using it, check that the denominator is never zero anywhere from \(a\) to \(b\).

Use negative indices to evaluate these definite integrals.
a\(\displaystyle\int_1^5 x^{-2}\,dx\)b\(\displaystyle\int_1^2\frac{1}{x^4}\,dx\)
Solution

a Increase the index from \(-2\) to \(-1\), and divide by \(-1\).

\[\begin{aligned}\int_1^5 x^{-2}\,dx&=\Big[\frac{x^{-1}}{-1}\Big]_1^5\\&=\Big[-\frac1x\Big]_1^5\\&=-\frac15-\Big(-\frac11\Big)\\&=-\frac15-(-1)\\&=-\frac15+1\\&=\frac45\end{aligned}\]

b Increase the index from \(-4\) to \(-3\), and divide by \(-3\).

\[\begin{aligned}\int_1^2\frac{1}{x^4}\,dx&=\int_1^2x^{-4}\,dx\\&=\Big[\frac{x^{-3}}{-3}\Big]_1^2\\&=\Big[-\frac{1}{3x^3}\Big]_1^2\\&=-\frac{1}{3\times8}-\Big(-\frac{1}{3\times1}\Big)\\&=-\frac{1}{24}-\Big(-\frac13\Big)\\&=-\frac{1}{24}+\frac{8}{24}\\&=\frac{7}{24}\end{aligned}\]
{{sec:teal:key:Concept 6:the area of a circle}}
An old limiting argument

In earlier years, the formula \(A=\pi r^2\) for the area of a circle was developed. Because the boundary is a curve, some limiting process had to be used in its proof. For comparison with the notation for the definite integral explained in Lesson 5A, here is the most common version of that argument. It is a little rough in its logic, but very quick. It involves dissecting the circle into infinitesimally thin sectors and then rearranging them into a rectangle.

{{svg:c5b_circle}}

The height of the rectangle in the diagram is \(r\), the radius. Because the circumference \(2\pi r\) is divided equally between the top and bottom sides, the length of the rectangle is \(\pi r\). Hence the rectangle has area

\[\text{length}\times\text{height}=\pi r\times r=\pi r^2,\]

which is therefore the area of the circle.

Where the rough logic is

With finitely many sectors, the top and bottom of the shape are made of small arcs, not straight lines, and the sides slope a little. As the sectors become thinner, the shape becomes closer and closer to a rectangle. This is a limiting process, just like the strips of Lesson 5A.
