Lesson 5D

Proving the fundamental theorem

This lesson proves the fundamental theorem of calculus, which was stated and used in Lessons 5B and 5C. The key idea is to let the upper limit of a definite integral vary. This gives a new function, the signed area function \(A(x)=\int_a^x f(t)\,dt\). The differential form of the theorem says that \(A'(x)=f(x)\). It is proven by trapping a thin strip between a lower and an upper rectangle, as in Lesson 5A. The integral form \(\int_a^b f(x)\,dx=F(b)-F(a)\) then follows, because any two primitives differ by a constant. The lesson ends with a note on continuous functions.

The definite integral as a function of its upper limit

The value of a definite integral \(\displaystyle\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\) changes when the value of \(b\) changes. This means that it is a function of its upper limit \(b\). When we want to emphasise the functional relationship with the upper limit, we usually replace the letter \(b\) by the letter \(x\), which is conventionally the variable of a function.

In turn, the original letter \(x\) needs to be replaced by some other letter, usually \(t\). Then the definite integral is clearly represented as a function of its upper limit \(x\), as in the first diagram above. This function is called the signed area function for \(f(x)\) starting at \(x=a\), and is defined by

\[A(x)=\int_a^x f(t)\,dt.\]

The function \(A(x)\) is always zero at \(x=a\). For the function sketched above, \(A(x)\) increases at an increasing rate (see the second sketch). \(A(x)\) is also defined to the left of \(a\), where it is negative because the integrals run backwards.

{{svg:c5d_fa}}{{svg:c5d_Aa}}
Reading the two sketches line by line
  • At \(x=a\): \(A(a)=\displaystyle\int_a^a f(t)\,dt=0\), because the region from \(a\) to \(a\) has zero width.
  • To the right of \(a\): when \(x\) moves a little to the right, a thin new strip of height about \(f(x)\) is added to the shaded region. Here \(f(x)\) is positive, so \(A(x)\) increases. Also \(f(x)\) gets larger as \(x\) moves right, so each new strip is taller than the one before, and \(A(x)\) increases at an increasing rate.
  • To the left of \(a\): when \(x\lt a\), the integral runs backwards, from \(a\) down to \(x\). Reversing the limits changes the sign (Lesson 5C), so \[A(x)=\int_a^x f(t)\,dt=-\int_x^a f(t)\,dt.\] The integral \(\displaystyle\int_x^a f(t)\,dt\) is a positive area, because the curve is above the \(t\)-axis. Hence \(A(x)\) is negative.
The signed area function

The function in the sketch above was never negative. But the definite integral is the signed area under the curve, meaning that a negative sign is attached to areas of regions below the \(x\)-axis (provided that the integral is not running backwards).

The upper graph of \(f(t)\) to the right is a parabola with axis of symmetry \(x=b\). The four areas marked \(B\) are all equal. Here are some properties of the signed area function

\[A(x)=\int_a^x f(t)\,dt.\]
  • \(A(a)=0\), as always.
  • In the interval \([a,b]\), \(f(t)\) is negative and decreasing, so \(A(x)\) decreases at an increasing rate.
  • In the interval \([b,c]\), \(f(t)\) is negative and increasing, so \(A(x)\) decreases at a decreasing rate.
  • In the interval \([c,\infty)\), \(f(t)\) is positive and increasing, so \(A(x)\) increases at an increasing rate.
  • \(A(b)=-B\) and \(A(c)=-2B\) and \(A(d)=-B\).
  • \(A(x)\) is also defined in the interval \((-\infty,a]\) to the left of \(a\), where it is negative because the integrals run backwards and the curve is above the \(x\)-axis.

The lower diagram above is a sketch of the signed area function \(A(x)\) of \(f(x)\).

{{svg:c5d_fp}}{{svg:c5d_Ap}}
Where the values and the rates come from

The region from \(a\) to \(b\) is below the axis and has area \(B\), so its signed area is \(-B\). Add the regions one at a time:

\[\begin{aligned}A(b)&=\int_a^b f(t)\,dt=-B,\\A(c)&=\int_a^b f(t)\,dt+\int_b^c f(t)\,dt=-B+(-B)=-2B,\\A(d)&=\int_a^c f(t)\,dt+\int_c^d f(t)\,dt=-2B+B=-B.\end{aligned}\]

Think of the thin strips again. In \([a,b]\) each new strip has a negative signed area, and the strips get deeper because \(f(t)\) is decreasing, so \(A(x)\) goes down faster and faster. In \([b,c]\) the strips are still below the axis, but they get shallower, so \(A(x)\) goes down more and more slowly. At \(c\) the curve crosses the axis, so \(A(x)\) stops decreasing and starts increasing: it has a local minimum \(A(c)=-2B\).

The fourth area \(B\), to the left of \(a\), is the mirror image in the axis of symmetry of the region from \(c\) to \(d\). It is above the axis, and the integral from \(a\) back to its left-hand end runs backwards, so at that end \(A(x)=-B\) again.

Stating the differential form

We can now state and prove the differential form of the fundamental theorem of calculus, from which we will derive the integral form used already in Sections 5B and 5C.

The fundamental theorem: differential form

Theorem: If \(f(x)\) is continuous, then the signed area function for \(f(x)\) is a primitive of \(f(x)\). That is,

\[A'(x)=\frac{d}{dx}\int_a^x f(t)\,dt=f(x).\]
Proof

Proof: Because the theorem is so fundamental, its proof must begin with the definition of the derivative as a limit,

\[A'(x)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{A(x+h)-A(x)}{h}.\]
{{svg:c5d_ftc}}

Subtracting areas in the diagram above,

\[A(x+h)-A(x)=\int_x^{x+h}f(t)\,dt,\] \[\text{so}\qquad A'(x)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac1h\int_x^{x+h}f(t)\,dt.\]

This limit is handled by means of a clever sandwiching technique.
Suppose that \(f(t)\) is increasing in the interval \([x,x+h]\), as in the diagram above.
Then the lower rectangle on the interval \([x,x+h]\) has height \(f(x)\),
and the upper rectangle on the interval \([x,x+h]\) has height \(f(x+h)\),

\[\text{so using areas,}\qquad h\times f(x)\le\int_x^{x+h}f(t)\,dt\le h\times f(x+h)\] \[\div\,h\qquad\qquad f(x)\le\frac1h\int_x^{x+h}f(t)\,dt\le f(x+h).\qquad\qquad(1)\]

Thus the middle expression is ‘sandwiched’ between \(f(x)\) and \(f(x+h)\).
Because \(f(x)\) is continuous, \(f(x+h)\to f(x)\) as \(h\to0\),

\[\text{so by (1),}\qquad\lim_{h\to0}\frac1h\int_x^{x+h}f(t)\,dt=f(x),\]

meaning that \(A'(x)=f(x)\), as required.

If \(f(x)\) is decreasing in the interval \([x,x+h]\), the same argument applies, but with the inequalities reversed.

Note: This theorem shows that the signed area function \(A(x)=\displaystyle\int_a^x f(t)\,dt\) is a primitive of \(f(x)\). It is therefore often written as \(F(x)\) rather than \(A(x)\).

The steps of the proof, filled in
  • Subtracting areas. The region from \(a\) to \(x+h\) is the region from \(a\) to \(x\) together with the thin strip from \(x\) to \(x+h\): \[\begin{aligned}A(x+h)&=\int_a^{x+h}f(t)\,dt\\&=\int_a^x f(t)\,dt+\int_x^{x+h}f(t)\,dt\\&=A(x)+\int_x^{x+h}f(t)\,dt.\end{aligned}\] Subtracting \(A(x)\) from both sides gives \(A(x+h)-A(x)=\displaystyle\int_x^{x+h}f(t)\,dt\).
  • The rectangles. Both rectangles have width \(h\). The blue lower rectangle lies inside the strip under the curve from \(x\) to \(x+h\), and the strip lies inside the upper rectangle (blue and amber together). Area of a rectangle \(=\) width \(\times\) height, which gives \(h\times f(x)\) and \(h\times f(x+h)\).
  • Dividing by \(h\). In the diagram \(h\gt0\), so dividing by \(h\) keeps the inequality signs the same way round. (When \(h\lt0\), the strip runs from \(x+h\) to \(x\), and the same sandwich applied to that strip gives the same limit.)
  • The sandwich. As \(h\to0\), the left-hand side \(f(x)\) stays fixed and the right-hand side \(f(x+h)\) approaches \(f(x)\). The middle expression is trapped between two quantities that both approach \(f(x)\), so it approaches \(f(x)\) too. This is the same reasoning as step D in Lesson 5A.
  • Decreasing \(f\). Then the lower rectangle has height \(f(x+h)\) and the upper one has height \(f(x)\), so \(f(x+h)\le\dfrac1h\displaystyle\int_x^{x+h}f(t)\,dt\le f(x)\), with the same limit \(f(x)\).
  • The lower limit \(a\) does not matter. Starting at another number \(a_1\) instead of \(a\) gives \(\displaystyle\int_{a_1}^x f(t)\,dt=\int_{a_1}^a f(t)\,dt+\int_a^x f(t)\,dt\). This differs from \(A(x)\) by a constant, and the derivative of a constant is zero.

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 3:the fundamental theorem, integral form}}
The integral form

The integral form of the fundamental theorem is the familiar form that we have been using in Sections 5B and 5C.

The fundamental theorem: integral form

Theorem: Suppose that \(f(x)\) is continuous in the closed interval \([a,b]\), and that \(F(x)\) is a primitive of \(f(x)\). Then

\[\int_a^b f(x)\,dx=F(b)-F(a).\]
Proof

Proof: We now know that \(F(x)\) and \(\displaystyle\int_a^x f(t)\,dt\) are both primitives of \(f(x)\).
Because any two primitives differ only by a constant,

\[\int_a^x f(t)\,dt=F(x)+C,\qquad\text{for some constant }C.\] \[\text{Substituting }x=a,\qquad\int_a^a f(t)\,dt=F(a)+C,\]

but \(\displaystyle\int_a^a f(t)\,dt=0\), because the area in this definite integral has zero width,

\[\begin{aligned}\text{so}\qquad0&=F(a)+C\\C&=-F(a).\end{aligned}\] \[\text{Thus}\qquad\int_a^x f(t)\,dt=F(x)-F(a),\]

and changing letters from \(x\) to \(b\) and from \(t\) to \(x\) gives

\[\int_a^b f(x)\,dx=F(b)-F(a),\qquad\text{as required.}\]
The reasons in the proof
  • \(\displaystyle\int_a^x f(t)\,dt\) is a primitive of \(f(x)\) by the differential form, proven above.
  • ‘Any two primitives differ only by a constant’ is Box 14B in Lesson 4J.
  • The line ‘Thus …’ comes from putting \(C=-F(a)\) into the first line, \(\displaystyle\int_a^x f(t)\,dt=F(x)+C\).
The change of letters

Putting \(x=b\) into \(\displaystyle\int_a^x f(t)\,dt=F(x)-F(a)\) gives \(\displaystyle\int_a^b f(t)\,dt=F(b)-F(a)\). The letter \(t\) inside the integral only names the horizontal axis: \(\displaystyle\int_a^b f(t)\,dt\) and \(\displaystyle\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\) are the same area, so \(t\) may be changed back to \(x\). In the examples below, the primitive is written in square brackets, \(\big[F(t)\big]_a^x=F(x)-F(a)\), exactly as in Lesson 5B.

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 4:super challenge, continuous functions}}
Continuous at a point, in an interval, and as a function

In Section 9K of the Year 11 volume, we defined continuity at a point: you can draw the curve through the point without lifting the pencil off the paper.

Then throughout this chapter, we have been using the phrase, ‘continuous in the closed interval \([a,b]\)’. This idea is also straightforward, and informally means that you can place the pencil on the left-hand endpoint \(\big(a,f(a)\big)\) and draw the curve to the right-hand endpoint \(\big(b,f(b)\big)\) without lifting the pencil off the paper.

There is, however, a global notion of a continuous function:

A continuous function is a function continuous at every value in its domain.

This may look obvious, like so many definitions in mathematics, but it is not. It means, for example, that the reciprocal function \(y=\dfrac1x\) is a continuous function. This is because \(x=0\) is not in its domain, so the function is continuous at every value of \(x\) in its domain.

Thus \(y=\dfrac1x\) is a ‘continuous function with a discontinuity’. (Or perhaps the word ‘discontinuity’ is redefined.) We recommend avoiding the concept completely unless some question specifically requires it.

{{svg:c5d_recip}}
How to classify a function
  1. Find the domain.
  2. Look for any value in the domain where the pencil must be lifted to draw the graph through that point.
  3. If there is none, it is a continuous function. A value that is not in the domain, such as \(x=0\) for \(y=\dfrac1x\), does not count.
