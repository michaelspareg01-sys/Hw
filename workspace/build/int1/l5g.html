Lesson 5G

Areas of compound regions

When a region is formed by two or more different curves, it usually has to be cut into pieces before integrals can find its area. This lesson finds where the curves meet, then either adds the areas of the pieces under different curves, or integrates the top curve minus the bottom curve. A careful sketch comes first every time.
{{sec:teal:key:Concept 1:regions under a combination of curves}}
Compound regions

When a region is formed by two or more different curves, some dissection process is usually needed before integrals can be used to calculate its area. Thus a preliminary sketch of the region becomes all the more important.

Areas of regions under a combination of curves

Some regions are bounded by different curves in different parts of the \(x\)-axis.

Cutting the region into pieces
  1. Sketch the curves and find where they meet, by solving their equations simultaneously.
  2. Cut the region with a vertical line through the meeting point. Each piece now lies under just one curve.
  3. Integrate each curve over its own piece, then add the areas.
The primitive of \((x-2)^2\)

From Lesson 4J, a primitive of \((ax+b)^n\) is \(\tfrac{(ax+b)^{n+1}}{a(n+1)}\). With \(a=1\), \(b=-2\) and \(n=2\), a primitive of \((x-2)^2\) is \(\tfrac13(x-2)^3\). Check by differentiating:

\[\frac{d}{dx}\,\frac{(x-2)^3}{3}=\frac{3(x-2)^2}{3}=(x-2)^2.\]

Worked example 1From the text: keep for homework
a Sketch the curves \(y=x^2\) and \(y=(x-2)^2\) on one set of axes.
b Shade the region bounded by \(y=x^2\), \(y=(x-2)^2\) and the \(x\)-axis.
c Find the area of this shaded region.
Solution

a The two curves intersect at \((1,1)\), because it is easily checked by substitution that this point lies on both curves.

b The whole region is above the \(x\)-axis, but it will be necessary to find separately the areas of the regions to the left and right of \(x=1\).

c First,

\[\begin{aligned}\int_0^1 x^2\,dx&=\Big[\frac{x^3}{3}\Big]_0^1\\&=\tfrac13.\end{aligned}\]

Secondly,

\[\begin{aligned}\int_1^2(x-2)^2\,dx&=\Big[\frac{(x-2)^3}{3}\Big]_1^2\\&=0-\big(-\tfrac13\big)\\&=\tfrac13.\end{aligned}\]

Combining these,

\[\begin{aligned}\text{area}&=\tfrac13+\tfrac13\\&=\tfrac23\text{ square units.}\end{aligned}\]
{{svg:c5g_e28}}
Note

Note: In this worked example, the second parabola is the first shifted right 2, and a parabola is symmetric about its axis of symmetry. This is why the two pieces have the same area.

Worked example 1, line by line

a Checking \((1,1)\): \(1^2=1\) and \((1-2)^2=(-1)^2=1\). To find the point without guessing, solve \(x^2=(x-2)^2\). Expanding, \(x^2=x^2-4x+4\), so \(4x=4\) and \(x=1\). Then \(y=1^2=1\).

c First piece: \(\tfrac{1^3}{3}-\tfrac{0^3}{3}=\tfrac13-0=\tfrac13\). Second piece: at \(x=2\), \(\tfrac{(2-2)^3}{3}=\tfrac{0}{3}=0\); at \(x=1\), \(\tfrac{(1-2)^3}{3}=\tfrac{(-1)^3}{3}=-\tfrac13\). So the integral is \(0-\big(-\tfrac13\big)=\tfrac13\).

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 2:regions between curves}}
Areas of regions between curves

Suppose that one curve \(y=f(x)\) is always below another curve \(y=g(x)\) in an interval \(a\le x\le b\). Then the area of the region between the curves from \(x=a\) to \(x=b\) can be found by subtraction.

17 Area between curves

If \(f(x)\le g(x)\) in the interval \(a\le x\le b\), then

\[\text{area between the curves}=\int_a^b\big(g(x)-f(x)\big)\,dx.\]

That is, take the integral of the top curve minus the bottom curve.

The assumption \(f(x)\le g(x)\) is important

The assumption that \(f(x)\le g(x)\) is important. If the curves cross each other, then separate integrals will need to be taken or else the areas of regions where different curves are on top will begin to cancel each other out.

Why subtraction works

Suppose first that both curves are above the \(x\)-axis. The area under the top curve \(g\), minus the area under the bottom curve \(f\), leaves the area between them:

{{svg:c5g_sub1}}\(-\){{svg:c5g_sub2}}\(=\){{svg:c5g_sub3}}

In symbols, using the properties of the definite integral from Lesson 5C,

\[\int_a^b g(x)\,dx-\int_a^b f(x)\,dx=\int_a^b\big(g(x)-f(x)\big)\,dx.\]
Finding the limits and the top curve
  1. The limits \(a\) and \(b\) are usually the \(x\)-coordinates of the points where the curves meet. Put the two expressions for \(y\) equal, collect every term on one side, and solve the equation.
  2. To see which curve is on top, substitute a value of \(x\) between \(a\) and \(b\) into both equations. The larger \(y\)-value belongs to the top curve.
  3. Simplify (top) \(-\) (bottom) before integrating. Put the bottom curve in brackets, because the minus sign changes the sign of every one of its terms.

Worked example 2From the text: keep for homework
a Find the two points where the curve \(y=(x-2)^2\) meets the line \(y=x\).
b Draw a sketch and shade the area of the region between these two graphs.
c Find the shaded area.
Solution

a Substituting \(y=x\) into \(y=(x-2)^2\) gives

\[\begin{aligned}(x-2)^2&=x\\x^2-4x+4&=x\\x^2-5x+4&=0\\{}(x-1)(x-4)&=0,\\x&=1\text{ or }4,\end{aligned}\]

so the two graphs intersect at \((1,1)\) and \((4,4)\).

b The sketch is drawn to the right.

{{svg:c5g_e29}}

c In the shaded region, the line is above the parabola.

\[\begin{aligned}\text{Hence area}&=\int_1^4\big(x-(x-2)^2\big)\,dx\\&=\int_1^4\big(x-(x^2-4x+4)\big)\,dx\\&=\int_1^4(-x^2+5x-4)\,dx\\&=\Big[-\frac{x^3}{3}+\frac{5x^2}{2}-4x\Big]_1^4\\&=\big(-21\tfrac13+40-16\big)-\big(-\tfrac13+2\tfrac12-4\big)\\&=2\tfrac23+1\tfrac56\\&=4\tfrac12\text{ square units.}\end{aligned}\]
Worked example 2, line by line

At \(x=4\): \(-\tfrac{4^3}{3}=-\tfrac{64}{3}=-21\tfrac13\), \(\tfrac{5\times4^2}{2}=\tfrac{80}{2}=40\) and \(4\times4=16\). So the first bracket is \(-21\tfrac13+40-16=2\tfrac23\).

At \(x=1\): \(-\tfrac{1^3}{3}=-\tfrac13\), \(\tfrac{5\times1^2}{2}=2\tfrac12\) and \(4\times1=4\). So the second bracket is \(-\tfrac26+\tfrac{15}{6}-\tfrac{24}{6}=-\tfrac{11}{6}=-1\tfrac56\).

Subtracting, \(2\tfrac23-\big(-1\tfrac56\big)=2\tfrac23+1\tfrac56=\tfrac{16}{6}+\tfrac{11}{6}=\tfrac{27}{6}=4\tfrac12\).

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 3:a region between curves that crosses the x-axis}}
Note

Note: The formula given in Box 17 for the area of the region between two curves holds even if the region crosses the \(x\)-axis.

To illustrate this point, the next example is the previous example shifted down 2 units so that the region between the line and the parabola crosses the \(x\)-axis. The area of course remains the same — and notice how the formula still gives the correct answer.

Why the formula still works

Shift both curves up by the same number \(c\), far enough that the whole region is above the \(x\)-axis. The area does not change, and Box 17 works for the shifted curves. But the height of each strip is unchanged too:

\[\big(g(x)+c\big)-\big(f(x)+c\big)=g(x)-f(x).\]

So the integral of the top curve minus the bottom curve gives the area wherever the region lies. There is no need to split the region at the \(x\)-intercepts, as Lesson 5F did, because \(g(x)-f(x)\) is never negative between \(a\) and \(b\).

Solution

\[\begin{aligned}x^2-4x+2&=x-2\\x^2-5x+4&=0\\{}(x-1)(x-4)&=0\\x&=1\text{ or }4,\end{aligned}\]

so the two graphs intersect at \((1,-1)\) and \((4,2)\).

{{svg:c5g_e30}}

b Again, the line is above the parabola,

\[\begin{aligned}\text{so area}&=\int_1^4\big((x-2)-(x^2-4x+2)\big)\,dx\\&=\int_1^4(-x^2+5x-4)\,dx\\&=\Big[-\frac{x^3}{3}+\frac{5x^2}{2}-4x\Big]_1^4\\&=\big(-21\tfrac13+40-16\big)-\big(-\tfrac13+2\tfrac12-4\big)\\&=4\tfrac12\text{ square units.}\end{aligned}\]
Worked example 3, line by line

The integrand: \((x-2)-(x^2-4x+2)=x-2-x^2+4x-2=-x^2+5x-4\), the same integrand as in Worked example 2.

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 4:regions between curves that cross}}
Areas of regions between curves that cross

Now suppose that one curve \(y=f(x)\) is sometimes above and sometimes below another curve \(y=g(x)\) in the relevant interval. In this case, the conditions of Box 17 no longer hold, and separate integrals will need to be calculated.

One integral for each piece
  1. Find every point where the curves meet inside the interval. These points cut the region into pieces.
  2. In each piece, decide which curve is on top, and integrate (top) \(-\) (bottom) over that piece.
  3. Add the areas of the pieces.

A single integral of (one curve) \(-\) (the other) across a crossing point would subtract one piece from the other, which is the cancelling that the box above warns about.

Worked example 4From the text: keep for homework
The diagram below shows the curves
\(\qquad y=-x^2+4x-4\quad\text{and}\quad y=x^2-8x+12\)
meeting at the points \((2,0)\) and \((4,-4)\). Find the area of the shaded region.
Solution

In the left-hand region, the second curve is above the first,

{{svg:c5g_e31}}
\[\begin{aligned}\text{So}\quad\text{area}&=\int_0^2\big((x^2-8x+12)-(-x^2+4x-4)\big)\,dx\\&=\int_0^2(2x^2-12x+16)\,dx\\&=\Big[\frac{2x^3}{3}-6x^2+16x\Big]_0^2\\&=5\tfrac13-24+32\\&=13\tfrac13\text{ u}^2.\end{aligned}\]

In the right-hand region, the first curve is above the second,

\[\begin{aligned}\text{so}\quad\text{area}&=\int_2^4\big((-x^2+4x-4)-(x^2-8x+12)\big)\,dx\\&=\int_2^4(-2x^2+12x-16)\,dx\\&=\Big[-\frac{2x^3}{3}+6x^2-16x\Big]_2^4\\&=\big(-42\tfrac23+96-64\big)-\big(-5\tfrac13+24-32\big)\\&=-10\tfrac23+13\tfrac13\\&=2\tfrac23\text{ u}^2.\end{aligned}\]
\[\begin{aligned}\text{Hence total area}&=13\tfrac13+2\tfrac23\\&=16\text{ u}^2.\end{aligned}\]
Worked example 4, line by line

Left integrand: take away each term of the bottom curve.

\[\begin{aligned}(x^2-8x+12)-(-x^2+4x-4)&=x^2-8x+12+x^2-4x+4\\&=2x^2-12x+16.\end{aligned}\]

At \(x=2\): \(\tfrac{2\times2^3}{3}=\tfrac{16}{3}=5\tfrac13\), \(6\times2^2=24\) and \(16\times2=32\). At \(x=0\) every term is 0, so the area is \(5\tfrac13-24+32=13\tfrac13\).

Right integrand:

\[\begin{aligned}(-x^2+4x-4)-(x^2-8x+12)&=-x^2+4x-4-x^2+8x-12\\&=-2x^2+12x-16.\end{aligned}\]

At \(x=4\): \(-\tfrac{2\times4^3}{3}=-\tfrac{128}{3}=-42\tfrac23\), \(6\times4^2=96\) and \(16\times4=64\), so the bracket is \(-42\tfrac23+32=-10\tfrac23\). At \(x=2\): \(-5\tfrac13+24-32=-13\tfrac13\). Then \(-10\tfrac23-\big(-13\tfrac13\big)=-10\tfrac23+13\tfrac13=2\tfrac23\).
