Lesson 5A

Areas and the definite integral

This lesson extends the study of areas to regions bounded by curves. Cutting a curved region into thin strips, adding up the strips, and taking a limit leads to a new symbol, the definite integral \(\displaystyle\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\), which is defined to be an area. When the graph is made of straight lines or parts of circles, the integral is found with the area formulae. For other curves, upper and lower rectangles trap the integral, and a limiting process gives its exact value.
{{sec:teal:key:Concept 1:a new symbol, the definite integral}}
The two principles of area

All area formulae and calculations of area are based on two principles:

  1. Area of a rectangle \(=\) length \(\times\) breadth.
  2. When a region is dissected, the area is unchanged.

A region bounded by straight lines, such as a triangle or a trapezium, can be cut up and rearranged into rectangles with a few well-chosen cuts. Dissecting a curved region into rectangles, however, requires an infinite number of rectangles, and so must be a limiting process, just as differentiation is.

A new symbol: the definite integral

Some new notation is needed to reflect this process of infinite dissection as it applies to functions and their graphs.

The diagram on the left below shows the region contained between a given curve \(y=f(x)\) and the \(x\)-axis, from \(x=a\) to \(x=b\), where \(a\le b\). The curve must be continuous and, for the moment, never go below the \(x\)-axis.

{{svg:c5a_reg}}{{svg:c5a_strips}}{{svg:c5a_strip}}

In the middle diagram, the region has been dissected into a number of thin strips. Each strip is approximately a rectangle, but only roughly so, because the upper boundary is curved. The area of the region is the sum of the areas of all the strips.

The third diagram shows just one of the strips, above the value \(x\) on the \(x\)-axis. Its height at the left-hand end is \(f(x)\), and provided the strip is very thin, the height is still about \(f(x)\) at the right-hand end. Let the width of the strip be \(\delta x\), where \(\delta x\) is, as usual in calculus, thought of as being very small. Then, roughly,

\[\begin{aligned}\text{area of strip}&\doteq\text{height}\times\text{width}\\&\doteq f(x)\,\delta x.\end{aligned}\]

Adding up all the strips, using sigma notation for the sum,

\[\begin{aligned}\text{area of shaded region}&\doteq\sum_{x=a}^{b}(\text{area of each strip})\\&\doteq\sum_{x=a}^{b}f(x)\,\delta x.\end{aligned}\]

Now imagine that there are infinitely many of these strips, each infinitesimally thin, so that the inaccuracy disappears. This involves taking the limit, and we might expect to see something like this

\[\text{area of shaded region}=\lim_{\delta x\to0}\sum_{x=a}^{b}f(x)\,\delta x,\]

but instead, we use the brilliant and flexible notation introduced by Leibniz.

The width \(\delta x\) is replaced by the symbol \(dx\), which suggests an infinitesimal width, and an old form \(\displaystyle\int\) of the letter S is used to suggest an infinite sum under a smooth curve. The result is the strange-looking symbol \(\displaystyle\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\). We now define this symbol to be the shaded area,

\[\int_a^b f(x)\,dx=\text{area of shaded region}.\]

This new object \(\displaystyle\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\) is called a definite integral. The rest of the chapter is concerned with evaluating definite integrals and applying them.

1 The definite integral

Let \(f(x)\) be a function that is continuous in a closed interval \([a,b]\), where \(a\le b\). For the moment, suppose that \(f(x)\) is never negative in the interval.

  • The definite integral \(\displaystyle\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\) is defined to be the area of the region between the curve and the \(x\)-axis, from \(x=a\) to \(x=b\).
  • The function \(f(x)\) is called the integrand, and the values \(x=a\) and \(x=b\) are called the lower and upper limits (or bounds) of the integral.
Why the name ‘integration’

The name ‘integration’ suggests putting many parts together to make a whole. The notation arises from building up the region from an infinitely large number of infinitesimally thin strips. Integration is ‘making a whole’ from these thin slices.

Reading the symbol

Read \(\displaystyle\int_1^4 x^3\,dx\) as ‘the integral from 1 to 4 of \(x^3\) \(dx\)’. The lower limit is written at the bottom of the integral sign and the upper limit at the top. The \(dx\) at the end says that \(x\) is the variable along the horizontal axis, and it closes the integral. Before using Box 1, check that the integrand is never negative between the limits.

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 2:evaluating definite integrals using area formulae}}
When the graph is made of lines or circles

When the function is linear or circular, the definite integral can be calculated from the graph using well-known area formulae, although a quicker method will be developed later for linear functions.

Here are the relevant area formulae:

2 Area formulae for triangle, trapezium and circle
Triangle:\(\text{Area}=\tfrac12bh\)\(=\tfrac12\times\text{base}\times\text{height}\)
Trapezium:\(\text{Area}=\tfrac12(a+b)h\)\(=\text{average of parallel sides}\times\text{width}\)
Circle:\(\text{Area}=\pi r^2\)\(=\pi\times\text{square of the radius}\)
Notes on the formulae

For a trapezium, \(h\) is the perpendicular distance between the parallel sides. Depending on the orientation, the word ‘height’ or ‘width’ may be more appropriate. Similarly, any side of a triangle may be taken as its ‘base’.

Semicircles and quadrants

A semicircle is half a circle, with area \(\tfrac12\pi r^2\), and a quadrant is a quarter of a circle, with area \(\tfrac14\pi r^2\). The graph of \(y=\sqrt{r^2-x^2}\) is the upper semicircle with centre the origin and radius \(r\), because squaring gives \(y^2=r^2-x^2\), that is, \(x^2+y^2=r^2\), with \(y\ge0\) (see the Revision page).

Method
  1. Sketch the graph of \(y=f(x)\) and shade the region from \(x=a\) to \(x=b\).
  2. Name the shape: a rectangle, triangle, trapezium, semicircle or quadrant, or several of these together.
  3. Read the lengths from the graph. Widths are differences of \(x\)-values, such as \(b-a\). Heights are values of the function, such as \(f(a)\) and \(f(b)\).
  4. Apply the area formula, and add the pieces if there are several.

{{svg:c5a_e1a}}

b The area represented by the integral is the shaded trapezium, with width \(4-2=2\) and parallel sides of length 1 and 3 (the values of \(x-1\) at \(x=2\) and at \(x=4\)).

\[\begin{aligned}\text{Hence }\int_2^4(x-1)\,dx&=\text{average of parallel sides}\times\text{width}\\&=\frac{1+3}{2}\times2\\&=2\times2\\&=4.\end{aligned}\]
{{svg:c5a_e1b}}

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 3:using upper and lower rectangles to trap an integral}}
Upper and lower rectangles

First-principles integration calculations are more elaborate than those in first-principles differentiation. But they are used when proving the fundamental theorem of calculus, which will soon make calculations of integrals straightforward. One should therefore carry out a very few such calculations in order to understand what is happening. The technique, in geometric forms, was already highly developed by the Greeks.

We shall find the integral \(A=\displaystyle\int_0^1 x^2\,dx\) sketched on the right.

{{svg:c5a_sq}}

The first step in the process is to trap the integral using upper rectangles (or outer rectangles) that the shaded region lies inside, and lower rectangles (or inner rectangles) that lie inside the shaded region. To begin the process, notice that the shaded area is completely contained within the unit square, so

\[0<A<1.\]

In the four pictures below, the region has been sliced successively into two, three, four and five strips, then upper and lower rectangles have been constructed so that the region is trapped, or sandwiched, between the upper and lower rectangles. Calculating the areas of these upper and lower rectangles provides tighter and tighter bounds on the area \(A\).

{{svg:c5a_tr2}}{{svg:c5a_tr3}}{{svg:c5a_tr4}}{{svg:c5a_tr5}}
Reading the pictures

In the pictures, the lower rectangles are grey and the upper rectangles are hatched. Each upper rectangle covers the lower rectangle below it.

Finding the heights

Because \(y=x^2\) is increasing for \(x\ge0\), the lowest point of the curve over each strip is at its left-hand end, and the highest point is at its right-hand end. So each lower rectangle has the height of the curve at the left end of its strip, and each upper rectangle has the height at the right end. The first lower rectangle has height \(0^2=0\), so it has zero area. Every rectangle has the same width, \(\tfrac1n\) for \(n\) strips, so that width can be taken out as a common factor.

Let \(A=\displaystyle\int_0^1x^2\,dx\). Use two, three, four and then five strips of equal width to trap \(A\) between the sums of the lower rectangles and the sums of the upper rectangles.
Solution

In the first picture, there are two strips of width \(\tfrac12\). The lower rectangles have heights \(0^2=0\) and \(\big(\tfrac12\big)^2\), and the upper rectangles have heights \(\big(\tfrac12\big)^2\) and \(1^2\).

\[\begin{aligned}\tfrac12\times\big(\tfrac12\big)^2&<A<\tfrac12\times\big(\tfrac12\big)^2+\tfrac12\times1^2\\\tfrac12\times\tfrac14&<A<\tfrac12\times\tfrac14+\tfrac12\times1\\\tfrac18&<A<\tfrac18+\tfrac48\\\tfrac18&<A<\tfrac58.\end{aligned}\]

In the second picture, there are three strips of width \(\tfrac13\).

\[\begin{aligned}\tfrac13\times\big(\tfrac13\big)^2+\tfrac13\times\big(\tfrac23\big)^2&<A<\tfrac13\times\big(\tfrac13\big)^2+\tfrac13\times\big(\tfrac23\big)^2+\tfrac13\times1^2\\\tfrac13\times\tfrac19+\tfrac13\times\tfrac49&<A<\tfrac13\times\tfrac19+\tfrac13\times\tfrac49+\tfrac13\times1\\\tfrac{1}{27}+\tfrac{4}{27}&<A<\tfrac{1}{27}+\tfrac{4}{27}+\tfrac{9}{27}\\\tfrac{5}{27}&<A<\tfrac{14}{27}.\end{aligned}\]

In the third picture, there are four strips of width \(\tfrac14\). Taking out the common factor \(\tfrac14\), the lower and upper sums are

\[\begin{aligned}\tfrac14\Big(\big(\tfrac14\big)^2+\big(\tfrac24\big)^2+\big(\tfrac34\big)^2\Big)&=\tfrac14\times\tfrac{1+4+9}{16}=\tfrac{14}{64},\\\tfrac14\Big(\big(\tfrac14\big)^2+\big(\tfrac24\big)^2+\big(\tfrac34\big)^2+\big(\tfrac44\big)^2\Big)&=\tfrac14\times\tfrac{1+4+9+16}{16}=\tfrac{30}{64},\end{aligned}\]

so \(\tfrac{14}{64}<A<\tfrac{30}{64}\).

In the fourth picture, there are five strips of width \(\tfrac15\). The lower and upper sums are

\[\begin{aligned}\tfrac15\Big(\big(\tfrac15\big)^2+\big(\tfrac25\big)^2+\big(\tfrac35\big)^2+\big(\tfrac45\big)^2\Big)&=\tfrac15\times\tfrac{1+4+9+16}{25}=\tfrac{30}{125},\\\tfrac15\Big(\big(\tfrac15\big)^2+\big(\tfrac25\big)^2+\big(\tfrac35\big)^2+\big(\tfrac45\big)^2+\big(\tfrac55\big)^2\Big)&=\tfrac15\times\tfrac{1+4+9+16+25}{25}=\tfrac{55}{125},\end{aligned}\]

so \(\tfrac{30}{125}<A<\tfrac{55}{125}\).

As decimals, the four sandwiches are \(0.125<A<0.625\), \(0.185\ldots<A<0.518\ldots\), \(0.21875<A<0.46875\) and \(0.24<A<0.44\): the bounds are getting tighter.

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 4:the limiting process}}
Turning the sandwich into a limit

The bounds on the area are getting tighter, but the exact value of the area can only be obtained if this sandwiching process can be turned into a limiting process. The calculations below will need the formula for the sum of the first \(n\) squares, proven in Question 2f of Exercise 2A by induction:

\[1^2+2^2+\cdots+n^2=\tfrac16n(n+1)(2n+1).\]
The four steps
  • A Divide the interval into \(n\) subintervals of equal width. Then (sum of lower rectangles) \(\le A\le\) (sum of upper rectangles), however many strips there are.
  • B Write the sum of the upper rectangles in terms of \(n\), using the formula, and find its limit as \(n\to\infty\).
  • C Do the same for the sum of the lower rectangles. The formula is used with \(n-1\) in place of \(n\).
  • D Both sums have the same limit, and \(A\) is trapped between them, so \(A\) equals that limit.
The algebra and the limits
  • Write \(n^3\) in the denominator as \(n\times n\times n\), and split the fraction into factors that each have a limit: for example \(\dfrac{n+1}{n}=1+\dfrac1n\).
  • As \(n\to\infty\), \(\dfrac1n\to0\) and \(\dfrac{1}{2n}\to0\), so \(1+\dfrac1n\to1\) and \(1-\dfrac{1}{2n}\to1\).
  • Substituting \(n-1\) for \(n\) in \(\tfrac16n(n+1)(2n+1)\) gives \(\tfrac16(n-1)\big((n-1)+1\big)\big(2(n-1)+1\big)=\tfrac16(n-1)n(2n-1)\).

Worked example 3From the text: keep for homework
Use upper and lower rectangles, and take the limit as the number of strips approaches infinity, to prove that \(\displaystyle\int_0^1x^2\,dx=\tfrac13\).
Solution
{{svg:c5a_trn}}

A Divide the interval \(0\le x\le1\) into \(n\) subintervals, each of width \(\dfrac1n\).
On each subinterval form the upper rectangle and the lower rectangle.
Then the required region is entirely contained within the upper rectangles, and, in turn, the lower rectangles are entirely contained within the required region. Thus however many strips the region has been dissected into,

\[(\text{sum of lower rectangles})\le A\le(\text{sum of upper rectangles}).\]

B The heights of the successive upper rectangles are \(\dfrac{1^2}{n^2},\ \dfrac{2^2}{n^2},\ \dots,\ \dfrac{n^2}{n^2}\), and so, using the formula quoted above,

\[\begin{aligned}\text{sum of upper rectangles}&=\frac1n\Big(\frac{1^2}{n^2}+\frac{2^2}{n^2}+\cdots+\frac{n^2}{n^2}\Big)\\&=\frac1n\times\frac{1}{n^2}\times(1^2+2^2+\cdots+n^2)\\&=\frac{1}{n^3}(1^2+2^2+\cdots+n^2)\\&=\frac{1}{n^3}\times\frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}{6}\qquad\text{(the formula)}\\&=\frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}{3\times n\times n\times2n}\qquad(6n^3=3\times n\times n\times2n)\\&=\frac13\times\frac nn\times\frac{n+1}{n}\times\frac{2n+1}{2n}\\&=\frac13\times\Big(1+\frac1n\Big)\times\Big(1+\frac{1}{2n}\Big),\end{aligned}\]

hence the sum of the upper rectangles has limit \(\tfrac13\times1\times1=\tfrac13\) as \(n\to\infty\), because \(\dfrac1n\to0\) and \(\dfrac{1}{2n}\to0\).

C The heights of the successive lower rectangles are \(0,\ \dfrac{1^2}{n^2},\ \dfrac{2^2}{n^2},\ \dots,\ \dfrac{(n-1)^2}{n^2}\), so substituting \(n-1\) for \(n\) into the quoted formula,

\[\begin{aligned}\text{sum of lower rectangles}&=\frac1n\Big(0+\frac{1^2}{n^2}+\frac{2^2}{n^2}+\cdots+\frac{(n-1)^2}{n^2}\Big)\\&=\frac{1}{n^3}\big(1^2+2^2+\cdots+(n-1)^2\big)\\&=\frac{1}{n^3}\times\frac{(n-1)n(2n-1)}{6}\\&=\frac13\times\frac nn\times\frac{n-1}{n}\times\frac{2n-1}{2n}\\&=\frac13\times\Big(1-\frac1n\Big)\times\Big(1-\frac{1}{2n}\Big),\end{aligned}\]

hence the sum of the lower rectangles also has limit \(\tfrac13\) as \(n\to\infty\).

D Finally, (sum of lower rectangles) \(\le A\le\) (sum of upper rectangles), and both these sums have the same limit \(\tfrac13\), so it follows that \(A=\tfrac13\).

The conclusion of all this heavy machinery of limits is simply that \(\displaystyle\int_0^1x^2\,dx=\tfrac13\). You will be relieved to know that we will soon have much quicker methods.
