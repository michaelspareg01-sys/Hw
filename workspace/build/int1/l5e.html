Lesson 5E

The indefinite integral

Now that primitives have been established as the key to calculating definite integrals, this lesson turns again to the task of finding primitives. A new and convenient notation, the indefinite integral \(\int f(x)\,dx\), is introduced for the general primitive, and the constant of integration must always be included. The two standard forms for integration then deal with powers of \(x\) and powers of \(ax+b\), with positive, negative and fractional indices. Brackets are expanded first where needed, using the special expansions.
{{sec:teal:key:Concept 1:the indefinite integral and the constant of integration}}
Back to primitives

Now that primitives have been established as the key to calculating definite integrals, this section turns again to the task of finding primitives. First, a new and convenient notation for the primitive is introduced.

The indefinite integral

Because of the close connection established by the fundamental theorem between primitives and definite integrals, the term indefinite integral is often used for the general primitive. The usual notation for the indefinite integral of a function \(f(x)\) is an integral sign without any upper or lower limits. For example, the primitive or the indefinite integral of \(x^2+1\) is

\[\int(x^2+1)\,dx=\frac{x^3}{3}+x+C,\qquad\text{for some constant }C.\]

The word ‘indefinite’ suggests that the integral cannot be evaluated further because no limits for the integral have yet been specified.

Check by differentiating

An indefinite integral can always be checked by differentiating the answer. The derivative of each constant is zero, so

\[\frac{d}{dx}\Big(\frac{x^3}{3}+x+C\Big)=\frac{3x^2}{3}+1+0=x^2+1,\]

which is the function that was integrated.

The constant of integration

A definite integral ends up as a pure number. An indefinite integral, on the other hand, is a function of \(x\). The pronumeral \(x\) is carried across to the answer.

It also contains an unknown constant \(C\) (or \(c\), as it is often written) and the indefinite integral can also be regarded as a function of \(C\) (or of \(c\)). The constant is called a ‘constant of integration’ and is an important part of the answer. It must always be included.

The only exception to including the constant of integration is when calculating definite integrals, because in that situation any primitive can be used.

Note: Strictly speaking, the words ‘for some constant \(C\)’ or ‘where \(C\) is a constant’ should follow the first mention of the new pronumeral \(C\), because no pronumeral should be used without having been formally introduced. There is a limit to one’s patience, however (in this book there is often no room), and usually it is quite clear that \(C\) is the constant of integration. If another pronumeral such as \(D\) is used, it would be wise to introduce it formally.

A number or a function

Compare the definite integral and the indefinite integral of the same function \(x^2+1\).

\[\begin{aligned}\int_0^3(x^2+1)\,dx&=\Big[\frac{x^3}{3}+x\Big]_0^3\\&=(9+3)-(0+0)\\&=12\end{aligned}\]

This is a number. The indefinite integral \(\displaystyle\int(x^2+1)\,dx=\frac{x^3}{3}+x+C\) is a function of \(x\) and of \(C\). If the constant were included in the definite integral, it would cancel: \((9+3+C)-(0+0+C)=12\). This is why any primitive can be used for a definite integral (Lesson 5B).

Standard forms for integration

The two rules for finding primitives given in the last section of Chapter 4 can now be restated in this new notation.

14 Standard forms for integration

Suppose that \(n\ne-1\). Then

  • \(\displaystyle\int x^n\,dx=\frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1}+C,\quad\) for some constant \(C\).
  • \(\displaystyle\int(ax+b)^n\,dx=\frac{(ax+b)^{n+1}}{a(n+1)}+C,\quad\) for some constant \(C\).
Integration and integral

The word ‘integration’ is commonly used to refer both to the finding of a primitive, and to the evaluating of a definite integral. Similarly, the unqualified term ‘integral’ is used to refer both to the indefinite integral and to the definite integral.

Sums, multiples and constants (from Lesson 4J)

The last section of Chapter 4 is Lesson 4J of this pack. Integrate a sum term by term, and keep each constant multiple in front. A constant \(k\) is \(kx^0\), so its integral is \(kx+C\). For example,

\[\begin{aligned}\int(3x^2+4x-5)\,dx&=3\times\frac{x^3}{3}+4\times\frac{x^2}{2}-5x+C\\&=x^3+2x^2-5x+C.\end{aligned}\]

Only one constant \(C\) is needed for the whole answer.

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 2:the second standard form, for powers of ax + b}}
Reading the second standard form

To use \(\displaystyle\int(ax+b)^n\,dx=\frac{(ax+b)^{n+1}}{a(n+1)}+C\):

  1. Write down \(n\), \(a\) and \(b\). The number \(a\) is the coefficient of \(x\) inside the bracket, with its sign.
  2. Keep the bracket \(ax+b\) as it is, and increase the index to \(n+1\).
  3. Divide by the new index \(n+1\) and by \(a\), then simplify the fraction in front.
Check by differentiating

Differentiating \((ax+b)^{n+1}\) gives \((n+1)(ax+b)^n\times a\), because the derivative of \(ax+b\) is \(a\). That is why the answer divides by \(a(n+1)\). For example,

\[\frac{d}{dx}\Big(\frac{(3x+1)^6}{18}\Big)=\frac{6(3x+1)^5\times3}{18}=(3x+1)^5.\]
A constant in front, and a fraction for a

A constant multiple stays in front: \(\displaystyle\int 3(2x-1)^{10}\,dx=3\times\frac{(2x-1)^{11}}{2\times11}+C\). When \(a\) is a fraction, dividing by a fraction is the same as multiplying by its reciprocal. For example, with \(a=\tfrac13\) and \(n+1=5\), dividing by \(\tfrac13\times5=\tfrac53\) is the same as multiplying by \(\tfrac35\).

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 3:negative indices}}
Negative indices

Both standard forms apply with negative indices as well as positive indices, as in the next worked example. The exception is the index \(-1\), where the rule is nonsense because it results in division by zero. We shall deal with the integration of \(x^{-1}\) in Chapter 6.

Negative indices, step by step
  1. Rewrite each fraction with a negative index. The whole bracket moves up: \(\dfrac{1}{(3x+4)^2}=(3x+4)^{-2}\).
  2. Increase the index by 1. For example, \(-3\) becomes \(-2\), not \(-4\).
  3. Divide by the new index, which is negative, so a minus sign appears.
  4. Write the answer as a fraction again, with a positive index: \(\dfrac{x^{-2}}{-2}=-\dfrac{1}{2x^2}\).

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 4:special expansions}}
Special expansions

In many integrals, brackets must be expanded before the indefinite integral can be found. The next worked example uses the special expansions. Part b also requires negative indices.

The special expansions
\[\begin{aligned}(A+B)^2&=A^2+2AB+B^2\\ (A-B)^2&=A^2-2AB+B^2\\ (A-B)(A+B)&=A^2-B^2\end{aligned}\]

\(A\) and \(B\) can be any terms, including powers of \(x\), negative numbers and fractions such as \(\dfrac{1}{x^2}\). Note that \(\Big(\dfrac{1}{x^2}\Big)^2=\dfrac{1}{x^4}\).

When the second standard form does not apply

The second standard form needs a linear function \(ax+b\) inside the bracket. In \((x^3-1)^2\), the inside \(x^3-1\) is not linear, so the brackets must be expanded first.

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 5:fractional indices}}
Fractional indices

The standard forms for finding primitives of powers also apply to fractional indices. These calculations require quick conversions between fractional indices and surds. The next worked example finds each indefinite integral and then uses it to evaluate a definite integral.

Surds and fractional indices
\[\sqrt x=x^{\frac12},\qquad\sqrt[3]{x}=x^{\frac13},\qquad\frac{1}{\sqrt x}=x^{-\frac12},\qquad\sqrt[3]{x^2}=x^{\frac23},\qquad x\sqrt x=x^{\frac32}\]

To evaluate a fractional power, take the root first and then the power:

\[4^{\frac32}=\big(\sqrt4\big)^3=2^3=8,\qquad 9^{\frac32}=\big(\sqrt9\big)^3=3^3=27,\qquad 8^{\frac23}=\big(\sqrt[3]{8}\big)^2=2^2=4.\]
Dividing by a fractional index

Dividing by \(\tfrac32\) is the same as multiplying by \(\tfrac23\), and dividing by \(\tfrac12\) is the same as multiplying by 2. So \(\dfrac{x^{\frac32}}{\frac32}=\tfrac23x^{\frac32}\) and \(\dfrac{x^{\frac12}}{\frac12}=2x^{\frac12}\).

Solution

\[\begin{aligned}\int_1^4\sqrt x\,dx&=\int_1^4 x^{\frac12}\,dx\\&=\tfrac23\Big[x^{\frac32}\Big]_1^4\\&=\tfrac23\times(8-1)\\&=4\tfrac23\end{aligned}\]

\[\begin{aligned}\int_1^4\frac{1}{\sqrt x}\,dx&=\int_1^4 x^{-\frac12}\,dx\\&=2\Big[x^{\frac12}\Big]_1^4\\&=2\times(2-1)\\&=2\end{aligned}\]

A surd of a linear function

The second standard form also applies with fractional indices. Write the surd as a power of the linear function, then use the standard form with \(n\) a fraction. For example, \(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{9-2x}}=(9-2x)^{-\frac12}\), so \(n=-\tfrac12\), \(a=-2\) and \(b=9\), and the new index is \(n+1=\tfrac12\).
