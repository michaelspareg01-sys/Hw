Lesson 5F

Finding areas by integration

An area is always positive, but a definite integral is negative when the curve is below the \(x\)-axis. This lesson turns definite integrals into areas: sketch the region first, integrate separately over the parts above and below the \(x\)-axis, use symmetry when the function is odd or even, and write the conclusion in square units. It ends by integrating with respect to \(y\), which finds the area between a curve and the \(y\)-axis.
{{sec:teal:key:Concept 1:areas and definite integrals, and regions above the x-axis}}
Finding areas by integration

The aim of this lesson and the next is to use definite integrals to find the areas of regions bounded by curves, lines and the coordinate axes.

Lessons 5F–5G ignore integrals that run backwards. Running an integral backwards reverses its sign, which would confuse the discussion of areas in these lessons. When finding areas, we decide what integrals to create, and we naturally avoid integrals that run backwards.

Areas and definite integrals

Areas and definite integrals are closely related, but they are not the same thing.

  • An area is always positive, whereas a definite integral may be positive or negative, depending on whether the curve is above or below the \(x\)-axis.

Problems on areas require care when finding the required integral or combination of integrals. Some particular techniques are listed below, but the general rule is to draw a diagram first to see which pieces need to be added or subtracted.

15 Finding an area

When using integrals to find the area of a region:

  1. Draw a sketch of the curves, showing relevant intercepts and intersections.
  2. Create and evaluate the necessary definite integral or integrals.
  3. Write a conclusion, giving the required area in square units.
Regions above the \(x\)-axis

When a curve lies entirely above the \(x\)-axis, the relevant integral will be positive, and the area of the region will be equal to the integral, apart from needing units.

Where the limits come from

‘The region bounded by the curve and the \(x\)-axis’ gives no limits. Find them from the sketch: they are the \(x\)-intercepts, found by solving \(y=0\). For \(y=4-x^2\), solve \(4-x^2=0\), so \(x^2=4\) and \(x=-2\) or \(x=2\). Areas are written in square units, shortened to \(\text{u}^2\).

Worked example 1From the text: keep for homework
Find the area of the region bounded by the curve \(y=4-x^2\) and the \(x\)-axis. (This was the example sketched in the introduction to this chapter.)
Solution

The curve meets the \(x\)-axis at \((2,0)\) and \((-2,0)\).
The region lies entirely above the \(x\)-axis and the relevant integral is

\[\begin{aligned}\int_{-2}^{2}(4-x^2)\,dx&=\Big[4x-\frac{x^3}{3}\Big]_{-2}^{2}\\&=\big(8-\tfrac83\big)-\big(-8+\tfrac83\big)\\&=5\tfrac13-\big(-5\tfrac13\big)\\&=10\tfrac23,\end{aligned}\]

which is positive because the curve lies entirely above the \(x\)-axis.
Hence the required area is \(10\tfrac23\) square units.

{{svg:c5f_e22}}
Worked example 1, line by line

Upper limit 2: \(4\times2-\tfrac{2^3}{3}=8-\tfrac83\). Lower limit \(-2\): \(4\times(-2)-\tfrac{(-2)^3}{3}=-8-\tfrac{-8}{3}=-8+\tfrac83\). Then \(8-\tfrac83=\tfrac{24}{3}-\tfrac83=\tfrac{16}{3}=5\tfrac13\) and \(-8+\tfrac83=-\tfrac{16}{3}=-5\tfrac13\), so the integral is \(5\tfrac13+5\tfrac13=10\tfrac23\).

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 2:regions below the x-axis}}
Regions below the \(x\)-axis

When a curve lies entirely below the \(x\)-axis, the relevant integral will be negative, and the area will be the opposite of this.

Why the integral is negative

Below the \(x\)-axis, every height \(f(x)\) is negative, so every strip \(f(x)\,\delta x\) has a negative value. This is the signed area of Lesson 5C (Box 5). The area is the integral with its sign changed: write the integral first, then give the area as a positive number.

Worked example 2From the text: keep for homework
Find the area of the region bounded by the curve \(y=x^2-1\) and the \(x\)-axis.
Solution

The curve meets the \(x\)-axis at \((1,0)\) and \((-1,0)\).
The region lies entirely below the \(x\)-axis, and the relevant integral is

\[\begin{aligned}\int_{-1}^{1}(x^2-1)\,dx&=\Big[\frac{x^3}{3}-x\Big]_{-1}^{1}\\&=\big(\tfrac13-1\big)-\big(-\tfrac13+1\big)\\&=-\tfrac23-\tfrac23\\&=-1\tfrac13,\end{aligned}\]

which is negative, because the curve lies entirely below the \(x\)-axis.
Hence the required area is \(1\tfrac13\) square units.

{{svg:c5f_e23}}
Worked example 2, line by line

At \(x=-1\): \(\tfrac{(-1)^3}{3}-(-1)=-\tfrac13+1\). Then \(\tfrac13-1=-\tfrac23\) and \(-\tfrac13+1=\tfrac23\), so the integral is \(-\tfrac23-\tfrac23=-\tfrac43=-1\tfrac13\). The area is the opposite, \(1\tfrac13\).

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 3:curves that cross the x-axis}}
Curves that cross the \(x\)-axis

When a curve crosses the \(x\)-axis, the area of the region between the curve and the \(x\)-axis cannot usually be found by means of a single integral. This is because integrals representing regions below the \(x\)-axis have negative values.

Split at every \(x\)-intercept
  1. Find the \(x\)-intercepts and sketch the curve.
  2. Write one integral for each piece, from one intercept to the next.
  3. Evaluate each integral. A negative value means the piece is below the axis: its area is the opposite.
  4. Add the areas (all positive).

A single integral over the whole interval adds the signed areas, so the parts below the axis cancel some of the parts above.

Solution

a The curve has \(x\)-intercepts \(x=-1\), \(x=0\) and \(x=2\), and is graphed on the right.

b Expanding the cubic,

\[\begin{aligned}y&=x(x+1)(x-2)\\&=x(x^2-x-2)\\&=x^3-x^2-2x.\end{aligned}\]
{{svg:c5f_e24}}

For the region above the \(x\)-axis,

\[\begin{aligned}\int_{-1}^{0}(x^3-x^2-2x)\,dx&=\Big[\frac{x^4}{4}-\frac{x^3}{3}-x^2\Big]_{-1}^{0}\\&=(0-0-0)-\big(\tfrac14+\tfrac13-1\big)\\&=\tfrac{5}{12},\end{aligned}\]

so area above \(=\tfrac{5}{12}\) square units.

For the region below the \(x\)-axis,

\[\begin{aligned}\int_{0}^{2}(x^3-x^2-2x)\,dx&=\Big[\frac{x^4}{4}-\frac{x^3}{3}-x^2\Big]_{0}^{2}\\&=\big(4-2\tfrac23-4\big)-(0-0-0)\\&=-2\tfrac23,\end{aligned}\]

so area below \(=2\tfrac23\) square units.

Adding these, \(\text{total area}=\tfrac{5}{12}+2\tfrac23=3\tfrac{1}{12}\) square units.

c

\[\begin{aligned}\int_{-1}^{2}x(x+1)(x-2)\,dx&=\Big[\frac{x^4}{4}-\frac{x^3}{3}-x^2\Big]_{-1}^{2}\\&=\big(4-2\tfrac23-4\big)-\big(\tfrac14+\tfrac13-1\big)\\&=-2\tfrac23+\tfrac{5}{12}\\&=-2\tfrac14.\end{aligned}\]

This integral represents the difference \(2\tfrac23-\tfrac{5}{12}=2\tfrac14\) of the two areas, and is negative because the area below is larger than the area above.

Worked example 3, line by line

At \(x=-1\): \(\tfrac{(-1)^4}{4}=\tfrac14\), \(-\tfrac{(-1)^3}{3}=+\tfrac13\) and \(-(-1)^2=-1\). So the bracket is \(\tfrac{3}{12}+\tfrac{4}{12}-\tfrac{12}{12}=-\tfrac{5}{12}\), and \(0-\big(-\tfrac{5}{12}\big)=\tfrac{5}{12}\).

At \(x=2\): \(\tfrac{2^4}{4}=4\), \(\tfrac{2^3}{3}=\tfrac83=2\tfrac23\) and \(2^2=4\). So the bracket is \(4-2\tfrac23-4=-2\tfrac23\).

Total: \(\tfrac{5}{12}+\tfrac{32}{12}=\tfrac{37}{12}=3\tfrac{1}{12}\). Part c: \(-\tfrac{32}{12}+\tfrac{5}{12}=-\tfrac{27}{12}=-\tfrac94=-2\tfrac14\).

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 4:areas associated with odd and even functions}}
Areas associated with odd and even functions

As always in mathematics, calculations are often much easier if symmetries can be recognised.

The symmetry, from Lesson 5C

An odd function, \(f(-x)=-f(x)\), has point symmetry in the origin. The region on the left of the origin is the region on the right rotated by \(180^\circ\), so the two regions have equal areas, one above and one below the \(x\)-axis. An even function, \(f(-x)=f(x)\), has line symmetry in the \(y\)-axis, so the regions on the two sides have equal areas on the same side of the \(x\)-axis. In both cases, find one area and double it.

a Show that \(y=x^3-x\) is an odd function.
b Using part a, find the area between the curve \(y=x^3-x\) and the \(x\)-axis.
Solution

a Let \(f(x)=x^3-x\).

\[\begin{aligned}\text{Then }f(-x)&=(-x)^3-(-x)\\&=-x^3+x\\&=-f(x),\text{ so }f(x)\text{ is odd.}\end{aligned}\]

b Factoring,

\[\begin{aligned}y&=x(x^2-1)\\&=x(x-1)(x+1),\end{aligned}\]

so the \(x\)-intercepts are \(x=-1\), \(x=0\) and \(x=1\).
The two shaded regions have equal areas because the function is odd.

{{svg:c5f_e25}}
\[\begin{aligned}\text{First, }\int_0^1(x^3-x)\,dx&=\Big[\frac{x^4}{4}-\frac{x^2}{2}\Big]_0^1\\&=\big(\tfrac14-\tfrac12\big)-(0-0)\\&=-\tfrac14,\end{aligned}\]

so area below the \(x\)-axis \(=\tfrac14\) square units.
Doubling, total area \(=\tfrac12\) square units.

Worked example 4, line by line

\(\tfrac14-\tfrac12=\tfrac14-\tfrac24=-\tfrac14\). The region from \(x=0\) to \(x=1\) is below the axis, so its area is \(\tfrac14\). The region from \(x=-1\) to \(x=0\) is above the axis and has the same area, so the total is \(2\times\tfrac14=\tfrac12\). (The single integral \(\int_{-1}^{1}(x^3-x)\,dx\) is 0, by Lesson 5C, so it is not the area.)

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 5:area between a graph and the y-axis}}
Area between a graph and the \(y\)-axis

Integration with respect to \(y\) rather than \(x\) can often give a result quickly, and avoid subtraction of areas.

Suppose then that we can find the inverse function, and write \(x\) is a function of \(y\).

  • A definite integral with respect to \(y\) represents the signed area of the region between the curve and the \(y\)-axis.
  • This means that the definite integral is the sum of areas of regions to the right of the \(y\)-axis, minus the sum of areas of regions to the left of the \(y\)-axis.
  • The limits of integration are values of \(y\) rather than values of \(x\).
16 The definite integral and integration with respect to \(y\)

Let \(x\) be a continuous function of \(y\) in some closed interval \(a\le y\le b\).

Then the definite integral \(\displaystyle\int_a^b x\,dy\) is the sum of the areas of regions to the right of the \(y\)-axis, from \(y=a\) to \(y=b\), minus the sum of the areas of the regions to the left of the \(y\)-axis.

Working with the inverse function

When the function is given with \(y\) as a function of \(x\), we first need to solve the equation for \(x\). We are thus working with the inverse function.

The same rules with the axes swapped

Everything in Concepts 1–3 still works, with \(y\) in place of \(x\): the strips are horizontal, of length \(x\) and width \(\delta y\); ‘above the \(x\)-axis’ becomes ‘to the right of the \(y\)-axis’; and the limits are read off the \(y\)-axis. Integrate the new variable as usual: \(\displaystyle\int(y-1)\,dy=\frac{y^2}{2}-y+C\).

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 6:regions on both sides of the y-axis}}
Regions to the left of the \(y\)-axis

By Box 16, an integral with respect to \(y\) over a region to the left of the \(y\)-axis is negative, because there \(x\) is negative. As in Concept 3, split the \(y\)-interval where the curve crosses the \(y\)-axis, evaluate each integral, and add the areas.

Fractional powers of negative numbers

Take the cube root first, then the power: \((-8)^{\frac43}=\big((-8)^{\frac13}\big)^4=(-2)^4=16\), and \((-1)^{\frac43}=\big((-1)^{\frac13}\big)^4=(-1)^4=1\). A primitive of \(y^{\frac13}\) is \(\dfrac{y^{\frac43}}{\frac43}=\tfrac34y^{\frac43}\) (increase the index by 1 and divide by the new index, Lesson 5E).

Solution

a The given equation is \(y=x^3\).

\[\begin{aligned}\text{Solving for }x,\quad x^3&=y\\x&=y^{\frac13}.\end{aligned}\]
{{svg:c5f_e27}}

b For the region to the right of the \(y\)-axis,

\[\begin{aligned}\int_0^8y^{\frac13}\,dy&=\tfrac34\Big[y^{\frac43}\Big]_0^8\\&=\tfrac34\times(16-0)\qquad\big(\text{because }8^{\frac43}=2^4=16\big)\\&=12,\end{aligned}\]

so area \(=12\) square units.

For the region to the left of the \(y\)-axis,

\[\begin{aligned}\int_{-1}^{0}y^{\frac13}\,dy&=\tfrac34\Big[y^{\frac43}\Big]_{-1}^{0}\\&=\tfrac34\times(0-1)\qquad\big(\text{because }(-1)^{\frac43}=(-1)^4=1\big)\\&=-\tfrac34,\end{aligned}\]

so area \(=\tfrac34\) square units.

c Adding these, total area \(=12\tfrac34\) square units.

Worked example 6, line by line

The right-hand region runs from \(y=0\) to \(y=8\) (the point \((2,8)\) on the curve), and the left-hand region from \(y=-1\) (the point \((-1,-1)\)) to \(y=0\). The \(\tfrac34\) is taken out of the bracket: \(\Big[\tfrac34y^{\frac43}\Big]_0^8=\tfrac34\Big[y^{\frac43}\Big]_0^8\). Then \(\tfrac34\times16=12\), and \(12+\tfrac34=12\tfrac34\).
