Lesson 2

The elimination method

In elimination, the two equations are added or subtracted so that one of the unknowns disappears. Sometimes this works straight away; sometimes one or both equations must first be multiplied by a number. This lesson teaches both cases, and then how to choose between elimination and the substitution method of Lesson 1. The teaching follows Year 11 Advanced section 1G Solving simultaneous equations.
{{sec:teal:key:Concept 1:adding or subtracting the equations}}
Solution by elimination (Year 11 Advanced, 1G)

This method, when it can be used, is more elegant, and usually involves less algebraic manipulation.

Solving simultaneous equations by elimination

  • Take suitable multiples of the equations so that one variable is eliminated when the equations are added or subtracted.
Setting out

Number the equations (1) and (2) and write what you do to them, in the book’s words: for example “Adding (1) and (2)” or “Subtracting (2) from (1)”. Once one unknown is found, substitute it into either of the original equations to find the other, and finish with a check in both original equations. The worked example of 3C Using simultaneous equations in Lesson 1 is solved this way.

Suitable multiples

When neither unknown has matching coefficients, the book’s rule above still applies: “take suitable multiples of the equations”. The aim is to make the coefficients of one unknown equal in size, so that adding or subtracting removes it; in Example 22 the book makes both \(x\)-coefficients 12. Write which equation is multiplied by which number, and give each new equation its own label, as the book does with (1A) and (2A).

Worked exampleYear 11 Advanced Example 22 (1G): keep for homework

Solve each pair of simultaneous equations by elimination.

a\(\begin{aligned}3x-2y&=29\quad(1)\\4x+5y&=8\quad(2)\end{aligned}\)

Solution
a Taking \(4\times(1)\) and \(3\times(2)\), \(12x-8y=116\)(1A) \(12x+15y=24.\)(2A)  Subtracting (1A) from (2A), \(23y=-92\) \(\div 23\)\(y=-4.\)  Substituting into (1). \(3x+8=29\) \(x=7.\)

 Hence \(x=7\) and \(y=-4\).

Steps the book skips: Example 22
\(4\times(1)\) gives \(12x-8y=116\) and \(3\times(2)\) gives \(12x+15y=24\). Subtracting, the \(12x\) terms cancel: \(15y-(-8y)=23y\) and \(24-116=-92\), so \(23y=-92\) and \(y=-4\). In (1), \(3x-2(-4)=29\), that is \(3x+8=29\), so \(3x=21\) and \(x=7\).
Check in (1): \(3(7)-2(-4)=21+8=29\). Check in (2): \(4(7)+5(-4)=28-20=8\). Both equations are true.

Part b of the book’s example has \(x^2\) and \(y^2\) in it, which is not linear, so it is left out of this pack.

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 3:choosing a method}}
The two methods (Year 11 Advanced, 1G)

Solution by substitution. This method can be applied whenever one of the equations can be solved for one of the variables.

Solution by elimination. This method, when it can be used, is more elegant, and usually involves less algebraic manipulation.

Both methods give the same answer

Either method gives the same solution, so the choice is about which is quicker and safer. When a question names a method (“using the elimination method”, “using the substitution method”), use that method: some schools take a mark off for using the other one.
