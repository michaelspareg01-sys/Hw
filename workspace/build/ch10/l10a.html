Lesson 10A

Projectile motion: the time equations

A projectile is thrown or fired into the air and then moves under gravity alone. We treat it as a point, ignore air resistance, and take \(g\doteq9.8\) m/s² (or 10 m/s²). This lesson covers the two ways to describe velocity, resolving velocity, the fundamental equations \(\ddot x=0\) and \(\ddot y=-g\), and problems where the speed or angle is unknown.
{{sec:teal:key:Concept 1:two ways to specify velocity}}
Position and velocity in two dimensions
  • Place the origin at the point of projection. Measure horizontal distance \(x\) and vertical distance \(y\) from it. Treat time \(t\) as a parameter: at each time \(t\) the projectile is at some point \(P(x,y)\) on its path.
  • Take \(\tv{i}\) and \(\tv{j}\) as unit vectors in the positive \(x\) and \(y\) directions. The point \(P(30,15)\) then has position vector \(\overrightarrow{OP}=30\tv{i}+15\tv{j}\).
  • Velocity is a vector. Its speed is its magnitude.

The velocity at any time \(t\) can be given in two ways:

  • By its speed and angle of inclination. The angle of inclination is the acute angle between the path and the horizontal. It is positive if the object is travelling upwards and negative if it is travelling downwards.
  • By its horizontal and vertical components \(\dot x\) and \(\dot y\), so that \(\tv{v}=\dot x\,\tv{i}+\dot y\,\tv{j}\), where \(\dot x=\tv{v}\cdot\tv{i}\) and \(\dot y=\tv{v}\cdot\tv{j}\).
Resolution of velocity

To convert between the two descriptions, draw a diagram to resolve the velocity into its horizontal and vertical components, or use the conversion equations

\[\begin{cases}\dot x=v\cos\theta\\ \dot y=v\sin\theta\end{cases}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\begin{cases}v^2=\dot x^2+\dot y^2\\ \tan\theta=\dfrac{\dot y}{\dot x}\end{cases}\]

(where ambiguity between \(\theta\) and \(180^\circ-\theta\) may need to be clarified by the diagram).

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 2:the fundamental equations of projectile motion}}
The vertical and horizontal motions are independent

No force acts on a projectile except the downwards force of gravity. This acceleration is downwards, so it affects the vertical component \(\dot y\) of the velocity according to \(\ddot y=-g\). It has no effect on the horizontal component \(\dot x\), so \(\ddot x=0\). In vector form, \(\tv{a}=-g\tv{j}\).

  • Unless otherwise indicated, every question on projectile motion should begin with the two equations \(\ddot x=0\) and \(\ddot y=-g\).
  • The working usually involves four integrations, two for \(\ddot x\) and two for \(\ddot y\), with four corresponding substitutions of the initial conditions (resolve the initial velocity first).
  • This gives six equations: the two accelerations, the two velocities \(\dot x,\ \dot y\), and the two displacements \(x,\ y\). Number them, so you can refer to them.

Key ideas: at the greatest height, \(\dot y=0\). The projectile lands when \(y\) equals the height of the ground (0, or negative if it lands below the origin). The horizontal range is the value of \(x\) when it lands. To find the impact velocity, resolve \(\dot x\) and \(\dot y\) back into speed and angle.

Worked example 2Keep for homework
A ball is thrown with initial speed 40 m/s and angle of inclination \(30^\circ\) from the top of a stand 25 metres above the ground.
a Using the stand as the origin and \(g=10\) m/s², find the six equations of motion.
b Find how high it rises, how long it takes to get there, what its speed is then, and how far it is horizontally from the stand.
c Find the flight time, the horizontal range, and the impact speed and angle.
Solution

Initially \(x=y=0\), and \(\dot x=40\cos30^\circ=20\sqrt3\), \(\dot y=40\sin30^\circ=20\).

a To begin, \(\ddot x=0\).  (1)

Integrating, \(\dot x=C_1\). When \(t=0\), \(\dot x=20\sqrt3\), so \(C_1=20\sqrt3\) and

\(\dot x=20\sqrt3.\)  (2)

Integrating, \(x=20\sqrt3\,t+C_2\). When \(t=0\), \(x=0\), so \(C_2=0\) and

\(x=20\sqrt3\,t.\)  (3)

To begin, \(\ddot y=-10\).  (4)

Integrating, \(\dot y=-10t+C_3\). When \(t=0\), \(\dot y=20\), so \(C_3=20\) and

\(\dot y=-10t+20.\)  (5)

Integrating, \(y=-5t^2+20t+C_4\). When \(t=0\), \(y=0\), so \(C_4=0\) and

\(y=-5t^2+20t.\)  (6)

b At the top of its flight, the vertical component of velocity is zero, so put \(\dot y=0\). From (5), \(t=2\) seconds. From (6), \(y=-20+40=20\) metres above the stand, and from (3), \(x=40\sqrt3\) metres from the stand. Because \(\dot y=0\) there, the speed is \(\dot x=20\sqrt3\) m/s.

c It hits the ground when it is 25 metres below the stand, so put \(y=-25\). From (6), \(-5t^2+20t=-25\), so \(t^2-4t-5=0\), \((t-5)(t+1)=0\), and \(t=5\) (\(t=-1\) is inadmissible). From (3), the horizontal range is \(x=100\sqrt3\) metres.

{{svg:stand}}

At impact, \(\dot x=20\sqrt3\) and \(\dot y=-50+20=-30\), so \(v^2=1200+900\) and the impact speed is \(v=10\sqrt{21}\) m/s. Also \(\tan\theta=-\dfrac{30}{20\sqrt3}\), so \(\theta\doteq-40^\circ54'\): the impact angle is about \(40^\circ54'\) below the horizontal.

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 3:using pronumerals for the initial speed or angle}}
Unknown angle of projection

Many problems require the initial speed or angle of inclination to be found so that the projectile behaves in some particular way. Often the muzzle speed of a gun is fixed but the angle \(\alpha\) can be altered. There are then usually two solutions: a low-flying shot and a "lobbed" shot that goes high into the air.

  • Resolve with pronumerals: \(\dot x=V\cos\alpha\), \(\dot y=V\sin\alpha\), and derive the six equations as before.
  • Substitute the target's \(x\) into the \(x\)-equation to find \(t\) in terms of \(\alpha\), then substitute into the \(y\)-equation.
  • Use \(\dfrac{1}{\cos^2\alpha}=\sec^2\alpha=1+\tan^2\alpha\) to obtain a quadratic in \(\tan\alpha\).

Worked example 3Keep for homework
A gun at \(O\) fires shells with an initial speed of 200 m/s, but a variable angle of inclination \(\alpha\). Take \(g=10\) m/s².
a Find the two possible angles at which the gun can be set so that it will hit a fortress \(F\) that is 2 km away on top of a mountain 1000 metres high.
b Show that the inclination of the lower angle to \(OF\) is the same as the inclination of the higher angle to the vertical.
c Find the corresponding flight times and the impact speeds and angles.
Solution

Place the origin at the gun. Resolving, \(\dot x=200\cos\alpha\) and \(\dot y=200\sin\alpha\). Integrating from \(\ddot x=0\) and \(\ddot y=-10\) exactly as before:

\[\dot x=200\cos\alpha\ \ (2)\qquad\qquad x=200t\cos\alpha\ \ (3)\] \[\dot y=-10t+200\sin\alpha\ \ (5)\qquad\qquad y=-5t^2+200t\sin\alpha\ \ (6)\]

a Because the fortress is 2 km away, \(x=2000\), so from (3), \(t=\dfrac{10}{\cos\alpha}\).

Because the mountain is 1000 m high, \(y=1000\), so from (6),

\[-\dfrac{500}{\cos^2\alpha}+\dfrac{2000\sin\alpha}{\cos\alpha}-1000=0\] \[\sec^2\alpha-4\tan\alpha+2=0.\]

But \(\sec^2\alpha=\tan^2\alpha+1\), so \(\tan^2\alpha-4\tan\alpha+3=0\), \((\tan\alpha-3)(\tan\alpha-1)=0\), giving \(\tan\alpha=1\) or 3, and \(\alpha=45^\circ\) or \(\tan^{-1}3\doteq71^\circ34'\).

{{svg:fortress}}

b The line \(OF\) is inclined at \(\tan^{-1}\tfrac12\doteq26^\circ34'\) to the horizontal, so the \(45^\circ\) shot is inclined at \(18^\circ26'\) to \(OF\), and the \(71^\circ34'\) shot is inclined at \(18^\circ26'\) to the vertical.

c When \(\alpha=45^\circ\): \(t=\dfrac{10}{\cos45^\circ}=10\sqrt2\) seconds. Then from (5), \(\dot y=-100\sqrt2+200\times\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}=0\), so from (2) the shell hits horizontally at \(100\sqrt2\) m/s.

When \(\alpha=\tan^{-1}3\): \(\cos\alpha=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{10}}\) and \(\sin\alpha=\dfrac{3}{\sqrt{10}}\), so \(t=\dfrac{10}{\cos\alpha}=10\sqrt{10}\) seconds. From (5), \(\dot y=-100\sqrt{10}+60\sqrt{10}=-40\sqrt{10}\), and from (2), \(\dot x=20\sqrt{10}\).

Then \(v^2=16\,000+4000=20\,000\), so \(v=100\sqrt2\) m/s. Also \(\tan\theta=\dfrac{\dot y}{\dot x}=-2\), so \(\theta=-\tan^{-1}2\doteq-63^\circ26'\). This shell hits at \(100\sqrt2\) m/s, at about \(63^\circ26'\) below the horizontal.
