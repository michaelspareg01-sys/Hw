Lesson 10B

Projectile motion: the equation of path

Eliminating \(t\) from the time equations gives the Cartesian equation of the path, which is always an upside-down parabola. This lesson covers deriving the equation of path, using it to find heights, range and direction, the range formula \(\frac{V^2}{g}\sin2\alpha\), and the equation of path as a quadratic in \(\tan\alpha\).
{{sec:teal:key:Concept 1:deriving the equation of path}}
The general case

The formulae for \(x\) and \(y\) in terms of \(t\) are a parametric equation of the path. A projectile fired from the origin with initial speed \(V\) and angle of elevation \(\alpha\) has \(\dot x=V\cos\alpha\), \(\dot y=V\sin\alpha\) initially, and integrating \(\ddot x=0\) and \(\ddot y=-g\) gives

\[x=Vt\cos\alpha\ \ \text{(3)}\qquad\qquad y=-\tfrac12gt^2+Vt\sin\alpha\ \ \text{(6)}\]

From (3), \(t=\dfrac{x}{V\cos\alpha}\). Substituting into (6),

\[y=-\dfrac{gx^2}{2V^2\cos^2\alpha}+\dfrac{Vx\sin\alpha}{V\cos\alpha}\qquad\text{so}\qquad y=-\dfrac{gx^2}{2V^2}\sec^2\alpha+x\tan\alpha.\]

The Pythagorean identity \(\dfrac{1}{\cos^2\alpha}=\sec^2\alpha=1+\tan^2\alpha\) gives the alternative form

\[y=-\dfrac{gx^2}{2V^2}\left(1+\tan^2\alpha\right)+x\tan\alpha.\]
  • This formula is not to be memorised: the working must always be shown, unless the equation is given in the question.
  • The last form is quadratic in \(x\), quadratic in \(\tan\alpha\), and linear in \(g\) and \(y\).
  • Differentiating the equation of path gives the gradient \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) at any value of \(x\). This is an alternative way to find the angle of inclination in flight: \(\tan\theta=\dfrac{dy}{dx}\).

Worked example 1Keep for homework
A ball is thrown from the origin with speed 20 m/s at \(60^\circ\) to the horizontal. Take \(g=10\) m/s².
a Derive the Cartesian equation of the path.
b Find the horizontal range.
c Find the direction of motion when \(x=15\sqrt3\).
Solution

a Initially \(\dot x=20\cos60^\circ=10\) and \(\dot y=20\sin60^\circ=10\sqrt3\).

From \(\ddot x=0\): \(\dot x=10\), and \(x=10t\) (using \(x=0\) when \(t=0\)).

From \(\ddot y=-10\): \(\dot y=-10t+10\sqrt3\), and \(y=-5t^2+10\sqrt3\,t\).

From \(x=10t\), \(t=\dfrac{x}{10}\). Substituting,

\[y=-5\times\dfrac{x^2}{100}+10\sqrt3\times\dfrac{x}{10}=-\dfrac{x^2}{20}+\sqrt3\,x.\]

b Put \(y=0\): \(x\left(\sqrt3-\dfrac{x}{20}\right)=0\), so \(x=0\) or \(x=20\sqrt3\). The range is \(20\sqrt3\doteq34.6\) m.

c Differentiating, \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=-\dfrac{x}{10}+\sqrt3\).

When \(x=15\sqrt3\), \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=-\tfrac32\sqrt3+\sqrt3=-\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\).

So \(\tan\theta=-\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\) and \(\theta\doteq-40^\circ54'\). The ball is moving at about \(40^\circ54'\) below the horizontal.

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 2:the range on level ground, and the maximum range}}
Range and greatest height

Putting \(y=0\) in the equation of path, \(x=0\) or

\[\dfrac{gx}{2V^2\cos^2\alpha}=\dfrac{\sin\alpha}{\cos\alpha},\quad\text{so}\quad x=\dfrac{2V^2}{g}\sin\alpha\cos\alpha=\dfrac{V^2}{g}\sin2\alpha.\]
  • The range on level ground is \(\dfrac{V^2}{g}\sin2\alpha\).
  • Because \(\sin2\alpha\) has a maximum value of 1 when \(\alpha=45^\circ\), the maximum range is \(\dfrac{V^2}{g}\), when \(\alpha=45^\circ\).
  • Any range less than the maximum can be reached with two angles, \(\alpha\) and \(90^\circ-\alpha\), because \(\sin2\alpha=\sin(180^\circ-2\alpha)\).
  • By symmetry, the greatest height is reached halfway, at \(x=\dfrac{V^2\sin2\alpha}{2g}\), and it is \(\dfrac{V^2\sin^2\alpha}{2g}\).

Worked example 2Keep for homework
A particle is projected from the origin with initial speed \(V\) at an angle \(\theta\) to the horizontal. You may assume that its path is \(y=x\tan\theta-\dfrac{gx^2\sec^2\theta}{2V^2}\). Find \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\), and hence show that the vertex of the parabola is \(\left(\dfrac{V^2\sin2\theta}{2g},\ \dfrac{V^2\sin^2\theta}{2g}\right)\).
Solution
\[\dfrac{dy}{dx}=\tan\theta-\dfrac{gx\sec^2\theta}{V^2}\]

At the vertex the path is horizontal, so put \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=0\):

\[x=\dfrac{V^2\tan\theta}{g\sec^2\theta}=\dfrac{V^2}{g}\sin\theta\cos\theta=\dfrac{V^2\sin2\theta}{2g}.\]

Substituting \(x=\dfrac{V^2}{g}\sin\theta\cos\theta\) into the two terms of the equation of path:

\[x\tan\theta=\dfrac{V^2\sin^2\theta}{g}\qquad\text{and}\] \[\dfrac{gx^2\sec^2\theta}{2V^2}=\dfrac{g}{2V^2}\times\dfrac{V^4\sin^2\theta\cos^2\theta}{g^2\cos^2\theta}=\dfrac{V^2\sin^2\theta}{2g},\]

so \(y=\dfrac{V^2\sin^2\theta}{g}-\dfrac{V^2\sin^2\theta}{2g}=\dfrac{V^2\sin^2\theta}{2g}\), as required.

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 3:the equation of path as a quadratic in tan α}}
Which points can be hit?

Multiplying the equation of path through by \(2V^2\) and rearranging gives a quadratic in \(\tan\alpha\):

\[gx^2\tan^2\alpha-2V^2x\tan\alpha+\left(2V^2y+gx^2\right)=0.\]
  • To hit a given point \(P(x,y)\) at a given speed, substitute \(x\) and \(y\) and solve for \(\tan\alpha\). Two roots give the low shot and the lobbed shot.
  • There is a solution if and only if \(\Delta\ge0\), which simplifies to \(2V^2gy\le V^4-g^2x^2\).
  • Geometrically, \(P\) can be reached only if it lies on or under the parabola \(y=\dfrac{V^4-g^2x^2}{2V^2g}\), whose vertex \(\left(0,\dfrac{V^2}{2g}\right)\) lies on the \(y\)-axis and whose zeroes are \(x=\pm\dfrac{V^2}{g}\).

Worked example 3Keep for homework
Use the equation of path \(y=-\dfrac{gx^2}{2V^2}\left(1+\tan^2\alpha\right)+x\tan\alpha\). Arrange it as a quadratic in \(\tan\alpha\), and hence show that with a given initial speed \(V\) and variable angle \(\alpha\) of elevation, a projectile can be fired through the point \(P(x,y)\) if and only if \(2V^2gy\le V^4-g^2x^2\). What does this mean geometrically?
Solution

Multiplying through by \(2V^2\),

\[2V^2y=-gx^2\left(1+\tan^2\alpha\right)+2V^2x\tan\alpha\] \[gx^2\tan^2\alpha-2V^2x\tan\alpha+\left(2V^2y+gx^2\right)=0.\]

This is a quadratic in \(\tan\alpha\), so it has a solution when \(\Delta\ge0\):

\[\left(2V^2x\right)^2-4gx^2\left(2V^2y+gx^2\right)\ge0\] \[4V^4x^2-8x^2V^2gy-4g^2x^4\ge0\]

Dividing by \(4x^2\): \(\ 2V^2gy\le V^4-g^2x^2\).

Rearranging again, \(y\le\dfrac{V^4-g^2x^2}{2V^2g}\).

This means that the target \(P(x,y)\) must lie on or under the parabola \(y=\dfrac{V^4-g^2x^2}{2V^2g}\), whose vertex \(\left(0,\dfrac{V^2}{2g}\right)\) lies on the \(y\)-axis, and whose zeroes are \(x=\dfrac{V^2}{g}\) and \(x=-\dfrac{V^2}{g}\).

Check: the zeroes agree with the maximum range \(\dfrac{V^2}{g}\), and the vertex agrees with the greatest height of a vertical throw, \(\dfrac{V^2}{2g}\).
