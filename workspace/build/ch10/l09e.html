Lesson 9E

Related rates

Applying the chain rule to rates: expressing one quantity as a function of another, differentiating with respect to time, using geometry to eliminate a variable, rates of change of angles, and chains with more than two links. This reviews Year 11 Section 16A.
{{sec:teal:key:Concept 1:the chain rule applied to rates}}
Related rates

When two quantities both change with time, and one is a function of the other, their rates of change are related by the chain rule. For example, if a bubble's volume \(V\) is a function of its radius \(r\), then

\[\dfrac{dV}{dt}=\dfrac{dV}{dr}\times\dfrac{dr}{dt}.\]
  • Express one quantity as a function of the other quantity.
  • Then differentiate with respect to time \(t\) using the chain rule.
  • Substitute the given values after differentiating, never before.
  • Be very careful with units, here and anywhere calculus is applied. A rate has units such as mm³/s or m/s.

If the rate you are given is for the "wrong" variable, use \(\dfrac{dr}{dV}=1\Big/\dfrac{dV}{dr}\), or simply solve the chain-rule equation for the unknown rate.

Worked example 1Keep for homework
A hemispherical bubble on the water surface is growing in size.
a Write down the formula for the volume \(V\) mm³ in terms of the radius \(r\) mm, then differentiate with respect to time using the chain rule.
b Find the rate at which the volume is increasing when the radius is 6 mm, if the radius is increasing at 3 mm/s.
c Find the rate at which the radius is increasing when the volume is 18 mm³, if the volume is increasing at 90 mm³/s.
Solution

a The volume is \(V=\tfrac12\times\tfrac43\pi r^3=\tfrac{2\pi}{3}r^3\).

\[\dfrac{dV}{dt}=\dfrac{dV}{dr}\times\dfrac{dr}{dt}=2\pi r^2\,\dfrac{dr}{dt}\]

b Substituting \(r=6\) and \(\dfrac{dr}{dt}=3\):

\[\dfrac{dV}{dt}=2\pi\times36\times3=216\pi\ \text{mm}^3/\text{s}.\]

c When \(V=18\): \(18=\tfrac{2\pi}{3}r^3\), so \(r^3=27\pi^{-1}\) and \(r=3\pi^{-\frac13}\).

Substituting \(r=3\pi^{-\frac13}\) and \(\dfrac{dV}{dt}=90\):

\[90=2\pi\times9\pi^{-\frac23}\times\dfrac{dr}{dt}\] \[\dfrac{dr}{dt}=5\pi^{-\frac13}\ \text{mm/s}\doteq3.4\ \text{mm/s}.\]
{{sec:teal:key:Concept 2:using the geometry to eliminate a variable}}
One variable too many

Often the formula involves two changing lengths, such as the radius \(r\) and depth \(h\) of water in a cone, \(V=\tfrac13\pi r^2h\). Before differentiating, use the geometry of the situation (similar triangles, a given ratio, or Pythagoras' theorem) to write the quantity as a function of one variable only.

  • Draw a diagram and name every quantity that changes.
  • Write down the rate you are given and the rate you want, with their signs. A quantity that is decreasing has a negative rate.
  • Find the relation between the two quantities in one variable, then differentiate with respect to \(t\) by the chain rule.

Worked example 2Keep for homework
A 5-metre ladder leans against a vertical wall. Its foot slides away from the wall at 0.3 m/s. Find the rate at which the top of the ladder is moving down the wall when the foot is 3 m from the wall.
{{svg:ladder}}
Solution

Let the foot be \(x\) m from the wall and the top be \(y\) m above the ground. We are given \(\dfrac{dx}{dt}=0.3\), and we want \(\dfrac{dy}{dt}\).

By Pythagoras, \(y=\sqrt{25-x^2}=(25-x^2)^{\frac12}\), so

\[\dfrac{dy}{dx}=\tfrac12(25-x^2)^{-\frac12}\times(-2x)\] \[\phantom{\dfrac{dy}{dx}}=\dfrac{-x}{\sqrt{25-x^2}}.\]

By the chain rule,

\[\dfrac{dy}{dt}=\dfrac{dy}{dx}\times\dfrac{dx}{dt}\] \[\phantom{\dfrac{dy}{dt}}=\dfrac{-x}{\sqrt{25-x^2}}\times0.3.\]

When \(x=3\), \(\sqrt{25-9}=4\), so

\[\dfrac{dy}{dt}=-\dfrac34\times0.3=-0.225\ \text{m/s}.\]

The top of the ladder is sliding down the wall at 0.225 m/s. The negative sign shows that \(y\) is decreasing.

{{sec:teal:key:Concept 3:rates of change of angles}}
Angles that change with time
  • Use trigonometry to express a length in terms of the angle, for example \(x=\dfrac{h}{\tan\theta}\), then differentiate with respect to \(\theta\).
  • Calculus with trigonometric functions always uses radians, so \(\dfrac{d\theta}{dt}\) comes out in radians per unit of time.
  • To convert, multiply by \(\dfrac{180}{\pi}\) to get degrees, and divide by 60 or 3600 to change minutes or hours into seconds.
\[\dfrac{d}{d\theta}\left(\dfrac{1}{\tan\theta}\right)=\dfrac{d}{d\theta}\left(\dfrac{\cos\theta}{\sin\theta}\right)=-\dfrac{1}{\sin^2\theta}\qquad\qquad \dfrac{d}{d\theta}(\tan\theta)=\sec^2\theta=\dfrac{1}{\cos^2\theta}\]

Worked example 3Keep for homework
A balloon rises vertically at 5 m/s from a point \(B\) on level ground. An observer stands at \(A\), 120 m from \(B\). Find the rate at which the angle of elevation \(\theta\) of the balloon from \(A\) is increasing when the balloon is 50 m high.
Solution

Let the height be \(h\) m. Then \(h=120\tan\theta\), so \(\dfrac{dh}{d\theta}=120\sec^2\theta\) and

\[\dfrac{d\theta}{dh}=\dfrac{1}{120\sec^2\theta}=\dfrac{\cos^2\theta}{120}.\]

By the chain rule,

\[\dfrac{d\theta}{dt}=\dfrac{d\theta}{dh}\times\dfrac{dh}{dt}=\dfrac{\cos^2\theta}{24}.\]

When \(h=50\), the line of sight has length \(\sqrt{120^2+50^2}=130\), so \(\cos\theta=\dfrac{12}{13}\) and

\[\dfrac{d\theta}{dt}=\dfrac{144}{169}\times\dfrac{1}{24}=\dfrac{6}{169}.\]

The angle is increasing at \(\dfrac{6}{169}\doteq0.0355\) radians per second.

In degrees, \(\dfrac{6}{169}\times\dfrac{180}{\pi}\doteq2.03^\circ\) per second.

Chains can have more than two links. If an area \(A\) depends on an angle \(\theta\), which depends on a length \(x\), which changes with time, then \(\dfrac{dA}{dt}=\dfrac{dA}{d\theta}\times\dfrac{d\theta}{dx}\times\dfrac{dx}{dt}\) (see Question 12).
